First, and it's the big one. OpenAI held its DevDay on Tuesday the twenty-ninth, and the headline was something called dots. A dot is an always-on agent. Each one gets its own cloud computer, runs around the clock, connects to what OpenAI says is more than four thousand apps, and learns your preferences as it goes. You talk to it through ChatGPT, Slack or Teams, with text messaging promised later. I read OpenAI's own announcement for this one.

Access is the small print that matters. Dots are on the Pro and Business Premium plans "in eligible markets", and the page doesn't say which markets those are. So before you plan anything around it in the UK, check your own account. Pricing for extra dots isn't published either.

What this means. The pitch has moved from an assistant you ask to a colleague who keeps working after you close the laptop. I've been running businesses since two thousand and five, and I'd have killed for that in year one. I'd also have given it far too much access. Earlier this week OpenAI paused a training run after an agent got out of its sandbox. Same trust question, now with a subscription attached. My rule: fewest tools that do the job, log everything it does, and a human between it and anything that moves money or messages a customer.

[[STORY: s2]]
Second, same stage, same day. OpenAI released a new model, GPT six point one Sol. API pricing is two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output, with cached input at ten cents. OpenAI says it matches their bigger GPT six Astra on a coding benchmark at about a fifth of the cost. There's also a new top tier called Pro five hundred, with twenty-five times the Plus allowance, an Agents API that can drive a computer, and Sign in with ChatGPT, which lets other apps use your ChatGPT identity.

What this means. Anthropic's Claude Sonnet five point five came out at two dollars in and ten dollars out. Twenty-four hours later, OpenAI lands on the identical sticker price. When the two biggest labs match on price, price stops being the reason to choose. What's left is how many tokens each one burns to finish your actual task, and how often it gets it right first time. So don't switch because of a launch headline. Test on your own work.

[[STORY: s4]]
Fourth, Perplexity. On Tuesday its Computer product gained Automations. You can set an agent to run on a schedule, say every Monday at eight, or to fire when something happens in Slack, Gmail, Outlook, Linear or GitHub, with conditions attached, like only emails from certain senders. Each run remembers the last one, and credits are only spent when a task actually runs. Perplexity's own examples are drafting replies to customer emails and a weekly competitor pricing report. I read the announcement myself.

What this means. Put that next to OpenAI's dots and you can see the race. Everyone wants to own your recurring jobs, the stuff you'd otherwise do every Monday morning with a coffee and a sigh. Trigger-based agents are the narrow, cheaper version of the always-on colleague. For most small businesses, narrow and predictable beats clever and roaming. Hold that thought, because it's today's build.

Forbes lists the signatories as President Trump, Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, Jensen Huang, Dario Amodei, Sundar Pichai and Greg Brockman. [[CHAPTER: The build]]
Today's build falls straight out of the news. A Monday morning report agent.

Here's the idea. Pick one job you or someone on your team does every week that's mostly gathering and summarising. Checking three competitors' prices. Pulling last week's sales and flagging what dropped. Sorting the inbox into "needs me", "needs someone else" and "can wait". Then hand that one job to a trigger-based agent, and have it land a draft in front of you at eight on Monday.

Why now. Two of this week's launches, Perplexity's Automations and OpenAI's dots, are built for exactly this, and both charge by use or by plan rather than by a developer's day rate. You can have the first version running in an afternoon.

Step one. Write the job down as if you were briefing a new starter. What to look at, what counts as worth flagging, and what the output should look like. One page, no more.

Step two. Pick your tool. If you already use Perplexity, Automations is the obvious start. If you're on a ChatGPT plan that has dots in your market, try that. Either way, start with the fewest connections the job needs. Read-only where you can.

Step three. Set the trigger. A schedule, like Monday at eight, is easier to trust than an event trigger. Get a schedule working before you let it fire on incoming email.

Step four. Make the output a draft, never an action. A short summary, three things that changed, one thing it's unsure about. Ask it to say "I couldn't check this" rather than guess.

Step five. Run it for three weeks next to the way you do it now, and compare. If it misses things you'd have caught, tighten the brief. If it catches things you'd have missed, you've just bought back an hour a week.

The guardrails, and I'm serious about these. It may read, summarise, compare and draft. It may flag things for you to decide. It may not send anything to a customer, a supplier or the public. It may not touch payments, prices or refunds. It may not change a live system. And it doesn't get your admin logins, ever. If a tool needs more access than the job needs, that's the wrong tool.

No revenue figures on this one. It's a time saver, not a business, and I'd rather tell you that than dress it up.

[[CHAPTER: One action]]
Your one action today. Thirty minutes, no code.

Take the three AI jobs you run most often. Writing a customer reply, summarising a document, drafting a post, whatever yours are. Run each one through both Claude Sonnet five point five and GPT six point one Sol with the identical prompt. Note which answer you'd actually use, and what each run cost.

Now both list prices are identical, the only numbers that matter are yours. Half an hour tells you which model earns your money, instead of a launch-day headline telling you.
