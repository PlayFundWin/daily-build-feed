Part one. What shipped.

The biggest conceptual story of the week comes from Cloudflare. On Monday the fourth of August, they announced Cloudflare Wallets, and a payments system called Cloudflare Pay. In plain terms, they are giving AI agents two things they've never properly had: a verifiable identity, and their own money. An agent gets a wallet with spending rules a human sets in advance. Per transaction caps, weekly budgets, allow lists of who it's permitted to pay. It's built on an open micropayments protocol called x four oh two, and the idea is that an agent can pay for an API call, a data source, or a service by itself, without anyone handing it a company card. Handle claiming is live now, with full functionality coming soon. Why should you care? Because this is the missing plumbing for genuinely autonomous business processes. Imagine a research agent that tops up its own data subscription when it runs dry, spending at most ten pounds a week, and physically unable to spend on anything outside its allow list. The spending guardrail model is the bit worth understanding early, because agent commerce is coming whether we're ready or not, and the people who understand the guardrails will be the ones comfortable switching it on.

If you're paying for seats on any of these tools, expect them to get cheaper or better, probably both.

Story four, and this one's a cluster: voice AI took a real step forward this week. On Sunday, OpenAI published a deep dive on GPT Live, their new voice system. The key word is turnless. Part two. The build.

Today's build is deliberately unglamorous, which is exactly why it works: a niche paid listings directory. A website that lists every supplier in one narrow, commercial niche, makes money from featured listings and leads, and gets built almost entirely by Claude in two to four days.

First, the evidence, because I promised you real numbers, not hype. Case one: an American builder named Frey Chu, interviewed on Niche Pursuits, runs a portfolio of directory sites earning over two and a half thousand dollars a month. His niches are gloriously boring: daycares with cameras, liquidation stores by city, luxury portable toilet hire for events. That last one, the high ticket one, is his best earner, and that's a pattern to remember. Case two: a directory of AI tools called Toolio, built almost entirely with AI assistance, reached nine hundred and forty dollars a month within about four months, from thirty one thousand monthly visitors, with makers paying to be featured. Case three, and the most recent: a solo builder launched Gov Auctions dot app, an aggregator of government auctions, on Hacker News in July. Around a hundred thousand visitors, over two hundred thousand auctions processed, and paying customers for premium features, with UK coverage included. He hasn't published revenue, so I won't invent a number, but it proves the model is landing paying users right now, in twenty twenty six.

So what is the actual play? You pick one UK niche where buyers are searching, suppliers are fragmented, and the service is high ticket. You build a site that lists every supplier, with a page for every town and category combination, a get quotes form that captures leads, and a featured listing tier at somewhere around twenty nine pounds a month, paid through a simple Stripe link. That's the whole machine. Directories monetise three ways: featured listings, selling leads to suppliers, and eventually display ads once traffic is real. Ignore ads in year one.

Here's roughly how the build week looks. Day one, Claude Code scaffolds the whole site: listing pages, category and location pages, search, the technical SEO markup, the sitemap. Day one into two, data. You pull supplier records from Google Maps through a scraping service or the Places API, a few thousand records for somewhere between nothing and fifty pounds, and Claude writes the cleaning and deduplication scripts, then generates unique descriptive copy for every listing. Day two, the money parts: the quote request form with email routing, and the Stripe payment link for featured placement. Day three, deploy on a free hosting tier, submit the sitemap to Google, and have Claude write fifteen or twenty supporting articles targeting the long tail searches in the niche. Total cash outlay: a domain for about ten pounds, data for under fifty, maybe a logo. Under a hundred pounds, worst case one fifty. After launch, it's two to four hours a week: emailing listed businesses to claim and upgrade their listings, and refreshing the data monthly.

Now the honest money maths, and I'm going to be conservative because directory hype is everywhere and most of it is nonsense. Months one and two: expect zero. Search traffic lags. By month four, if the niche has genuine search volume, somewhere between fifty and a hundred and fifty pounds a month is defensible. By month nine to twelve, two hundred to five hundred a month. Three featured listings at twenty nine pounds is eighty seven pounds a month. Ten is nearly three hundred. Toolio's pace, nine hundred dollars a month by month four, is the optimistic end, so I've assumed a third of it. The realistic prize here isn't one big win. It's a slow burn asset that costs a hundred pounds and a few evenings, teaches you the whole programmatic SEO and lead generation playbook, and can be cloned into a second and third niche once the first one works.

The risks, plainly. Search is the main engine, and Google's AI Overviews are squeezing informational traffic, so you pick transactional intent: hire, supplier, near me searches, where someone wants a company, not an answer. Featured listing revenue from direct outreach doesn't depend on rankings at all, which is your hedge. Data goes stale, so the refresh script matters. And on the legal side, scraping business information is generally fine, and emailing companies at corporate addresses is permitted in the UK, but keep your opt outs clean. Check that yourself; that's a flag, not legal advice.

And here is where you have an advantage nobody else in this game has. The single best niche selection rule, from an indie hacker named Jonathan Geiger who's at six and a half thousand dollars a month across two products: competitors already making money is the validation. If nothing exists, don't build it. Now apply that to what you already own. You sit on top of a network of UK sports clubs and charities. A directory of fundraising suppliers, or club services, or sports facility hire, is a niche where you already have the distribution, the credibility, and the supplier relationships. Every club in your network is both a potential user and a source of supplier names. That is a moat, and it cost you nothing.

But, and this is the discipline that separates this from a toy: you validate before you build. The step most people skip. Before a single line of code, you email ten suppliers in the candidate niche and ask one question: would you pay twenty nine pounds a month to be featured, or ten pounds per enquiry? If three say yes or even maybe, build. If ten say no, you've saved yourself a fortnight. More on that in part four.

Part three. Applying this week to your three businesses.

Play Fund Win first, and this is the segment where the news is genuinely strategic. The UK prize draw sector is going through a regulatory squeeze right now, and for you that squeeze is an opportunity. On the first of July, the sector launched the Prize Competition Council, its first proper trade association, fifty plus operators, pushing a new voluntary code of conduct. The backdrop: the Gambling Commission logged over four hundred suspected illegal lotteries last year, and hundreds of cases of draws presenting themselves as something they're not. Meanwhile the Fundraising Regulator has been consulting on a minimum charity contribution for prize draws that use its badge, starting at ten per cent of ticket sales and stepping up to twenty per cent by twenty twenty eight, with a decision expected around September. So here's the question worth sitting with: when that September decision lands, is Play Fund Win positioned as the platform that was already compliant before compliance was cool? Your model already clears those thresholds. That's a ready made comms moment against low contribution commercial operators, and it's worth deciding now whether you want a voice inside the Prize Competition Council while the code is still soft. One cost note: if you operate under an external lottery manager licence, those licence fees are rising twenty five per cent, so budget for it. And one product idea from the same research sweep: in May, Just Giving launched Hope, an AI fundraising coach that interviews the fundraiser and generates personalised outreach and promotion tactics. They cite an average sixty three pounds of extra donations every time a page gets shared. Nothing stops you building the prize draw version. An AI draw coach that helps a club treasurer write the promotion messages, time the shares, and pick the supporter segments. Claude could prototype it in a weekend on your existing platform.

League Pages. EV Partnerships. Fresh government figures from the first of July: the UK public charging network stands at roughly a hundred and twenty one thousand chargers, up ten per cent year on year. But the growth rate is slowing, and it's concentrating in high powered hubs. The land grab phase is ending, which means the game shifts to gap filling: finding the local authorities with poor provision and putting chargers exactly there. Sports venues in underserved districts are precisely your pitch, and the government data tells you which districts those are. Zapmap's quarterly report adds a trend worth quoting in every venue boardroom: higher powered installations are moving into retail and leisure destinations, because dwell time suits them. The venue version writes itself. A stadium car park charger that serves fans on matchday and delivery fleets the other three hundred and forty days is twice the asset, and that argument might be the one that finally gets a venue board over the line.
