Part one. What shipped.

First up, OpenAI. On Thursday the sixth of August, OpenAI pushed an update to GPT five point six Sol, its top reasoning model, claiming roughly sixty eight per cent fewer factual errors, plus a new reasoning depth slider for Plus and Pro subscribers so you can trade speed for accuracy on the fly. The bigger news is for everyone else. Free ChatGPT users now get GPT five point six Luna by default, with unlimited text chats and a new Think button that was previously locked behind a paywall. Why you care: if you or your team have been limping along on the free tier, it just got meaningfully better, for nothing. Before you renew a paid seat for someone who barely uses it, check whether Luna on the free plan now covers what they actually do all day. It's also worth noting that the reasoning depth slider is a genuinely useful idea worth stealing conceptually, if you're building your own tool on top of any model's API, giving your users an explicit "fast and cheap" versus "slow and careful" toggle is a cheap way to let them manage their own cost, rather than you guessing at a single setting that suits nobody.

Second, and this one only landed this morning. Anthropic rewrote the safety classifier behind Claude Fable five, cutting false blocks on ordinary biology questions by around eighty five per cent. Genuine dual use topics, the virology and toxicology end of things, stay restricted. But if you've ever asked Claude a straightforward health, fitness, or clinical question and got redirected to a weaker fallback model for no good reason, that should mostly stop now. Why you care: anyone building content, coaching, or customer support in health, wellness, or fitness has been fighting this exact friction for months, watching a perfectly reasonable question about, say, a training plan or a supplement interaction get bounced to a noticeably worse model with a vague safety message attached. It's a small fix, but it's the kind of small fix that stops your AI assistant embarrassing you in front of a client, and if you'd previously built a workaround, a different model, a rephrased prompt, a manual fallback, it's worth testing again this week to see if you can simplify back down to just using Claude directly.

Third, and this is the one with a deadline. Google has confirmed that Google Assistant starts getting removed from Android phones, tablets, Wear OS, headphones, and Android Auto from the fourth of September, this year. Cars with Google built in are exempt, everything else isn't. Gemini becomes the permanent replacement, and crucially, the option to switch back is going away, not staying as a toggle. Why you care: if you or your business lean on Assistant routines anywhere, a shop floor speaker, a warehouse voice command, dictation in the van, you have about four weeks to re-test all of it on Gemini before the rug gets pulled. Do not find this out on the fourth of September, mid shift, with a customer waiting. This is also a useful reminder generally, if any part of your operation depends on a single vendor's voice assistant staying exactly as it is forever, that dependency is a risk worth writing down somewhere, not just carrying around in your head.

Fourth, a genuinely useful bit of grown-up behaviour from, of all companies, Microsoft. Internal reporting this week revealed Microsoft has capped how many AI tokens its own engineers can burn through, with per-division budgets and a memo that reportedly says, in as many words, tokenmaxxing is not what we are optimizing for. Why you care: if a company with Microsoft's balance sheet is putting a lid on unrestrained AI usage inside its own walls, that's permission for the rest of us to do the same. It's easy to assume more usage of a coding agent is automatically better, more code written, more problems solved, but that's exactly the assumption Microsoft's memo is pushing back on. If you're running Claude Code or a similar coding agent day to day, it is worth setting yourself an actual monthly token budget rather than letting the meter run, and reviewing what a heavy week actually produced versus a light one. Treat it like a cloud bill, because that's what it is, and most cloud bills reward someone occasionally checking them.

Fifth, a slower burn worth knowing about. Anthropic has confirmed, after Business Insider first reported it, that it's building an in-house chip design team, exploring Samsung as a manufacturing partner, aimed at bringing down the cost of running Claude. This doesn't replace its use of AWS, Google's chips, Nvidia, or AMD, it sits alongside them. Why you care: nothing changes for you this week, but it's a signal. If Anthropic gets even partial control of its own silicon, the long run direction of Claude's per token pricing is down, not up. File that away for when you're doing multi year maths on any product you build on top of the API.

And one more thing, filed as a rumour rather than a fact, because that's what it is. Business Insider is also reporting Google is negotiating something close to a billion and a half dollar deal, part licensing, part hiring, to bring in the team behind Mechanize, a startup that builds simulated environments for training and grading AI coding agents. Google itself hasn't confirmed a word of it, so I'm not treating it as news, only as a direction of travel. If it's true, it tells you where the big labs think the next real fight is, not chatbots, but who can most reliably grade whether an agent's code actually works. Worth watching, not worth acting on yet.

Part two. The build.

Right, here's today's business idea, and I want to be upfront about where it came from, because that matters more than the idea itself. It came from a Hacker News thread from December last year, called, roughly, those making five hundred dollars a month on side projects, where real people posted their actual numbers in public. Not projections. Not vibes. Numbers.

The idea is small, gap filler browser extensions for the AI tools you already use every day. Not a competitor to ChatGPT, Claude, or NotebookLM. A plug in that fixes one specific, annoying thing those tools don't do, sold to people who are already using them for free.

Here's the evidence, and I'll flag the age of each piece honestly, because one is fresh and one isn't. A Hacker News user posting as wonderfuly described building NotebookLM Web Importer, a one click tool for pulling web pages straight into NotebookLM, in under a day. They added paid premium features mid last year and said it quickly passed five hundred dollars a month. That's a single self reported figure from one comment, so treat it as a strong signal, not an audited account. The older but more detailed data point is Edmund Yong's Chrome extension, Easy Folders, which adds folders, search, and a reusable prompt manager to ChatGPT and Claude. Six months after launch, Yong posted on Indie Hackers that it had passed three thousand seven hundred dollars in monthly recurring revenue, with over forty two thousand dollars in total revenue by that point. That post is about two years old now, so I'm not claiming those numbers hold today, but it tells you the niche has legs, because people are still building in it.

Why this is roughly ninety per cent buildable by an AI coding agent in a matter of days. A browser extension, done properly under Chrome's current Manifest version three rules, is a small, well documented codebase. A content script that injects into the page, a popup for settings, local storage for state. There's no heavy backend required to ship a free tier, and gating a premium tier can be as simple as a Stripe checkout link plus a basic licence check. This is precisely the shape of project a coding agent handles start to finish, scaffolding, DOM logic, a settings screen, packaging for the store. The part that isn't automatable is picking the actual pain point, which means you, personally, need to be an annoyed daily user of the tool you're building for. That's not a technical step. It's a noticing step.

Why it's sellable to a UK audience without extra work. ChatGPT, Claude, and NotebookLM all have large UK professional and student user bases, the product is English first by default, so there's no localisation cost, and distribution runs through the Chrome Web Store, Reddit communities, and Product Hunt, none of which care where you're sat. Unlike a physical product or a service business, there's no shipping, no time zone support rota, and no currency conversion headache beyond however Stripe already handles it for you, which means your total addressable market on day one is genuinely global English speaking users, not just the UK, even though the UK is where you'll likely find your first real customers through your own network.

Why it fits comfortably under five hundred pounds to start. Chrome developer registration is a five dollar one off fee. Firefox's add on store is free to list on. You don't need paid advertising, both people I quoted grew through word of mouth and community posts, not ad spend. A basic landing page on something like Carrd costs well under twenty pounds a year, and Stripe takes a cut per transaction rather than charging you upfront. Realistically you're looking at domain, a screen recording tool for a demo, and maybe a small logo, comfortably inside your budget with room to spare.

Let's do the honest, conservative maths, because "someone made three thousand seven hundred dollars a month" is not the same as "you will too." Most side project extensions never clear a hundred dollars a month, and that Hacker News thread is a highlight reel, not a representative sample, survivorship bias is doing a lot of work in every one of these stories. A realistic first target is not matching Yong's numbers, it's getting to fifty paying users at, say, four pounds a month, which is two hundred pounds a month, inside three to six months of consistent posting and iterating. That's not retirement money, it's proof the mechanism works, and it costs you almost nothing to find out, your main investment is evenings and weekends, not capital. If it never gets past ten users, you've lost a few pounds in store fees and some spare time, not your savings. That asymmetry, small guaranteed downside against a real if unlikely upside, is the actual argument for doing it, not the headline numbers.

The build steps, in order. One, pick an AI tool you personally use every single day and name its single most annoying missing feature. This is the validation step, and you cannot skip it by guessing what other people want. Two, get an AI coding agent to scaffold the extension itself, content script, popup, storage. Three, ship one solid feature completely free, and hold back one or two features behind a paid unlock, don't try to monetise on day one. Four, submit to the Chrome Web Store and Firefox Add-ons, and put together a one page site with a short demo clip, thirty seconds is plenty, nobody needs a two minute product video for a browser extension. Five, post it, properly, in the relevant subreddit or as a Show HN, with an honest, specific title rather than a marketing pitch, people on those forums can smell a pitch instantly and will ignore it, and consider a small Product Hunt launch once you have a handful of real users who can vouch for it. Six, only add the paid tier once you can see actual usage, which is exactly the order both Yong and wonderfuly followed, build the audience first, monetise the audience second, not the other way round.

The honest risks. Chrome and Firefox can and do change their extension platforms under you, Manifest version three itself was exactly this kind of forced migration a few years back, so budget half a day every few months for maintenance. Store review can also be slow or arbitrary, and a rejected extension with no clear reason is a genuinely common complaint, so don't plan your cash flow around a same day approval. And this is a crowded lane, dozens of ChatGPT and Claude companion extensions already exist, so your edge has to be a specific, sharply defined annoyance, not a vague promise to make the tool better. There's also a platform risk worth naming directly, if the feature you're selling is small and obviously useful enough, the underlying tool's own team might simply build it in natively one day, and your extension becomes redundant overnight. That's not a reason not to build, every small software business lives with some version of this risk, but it is a reason to keep your costs low and your build time short, so a redundant extension is a minor disappointment rather than a wasted quarter.

Here's your edge, specifically. You already run three live ventures, which means you're comfortable with Stripe, with domain and hosting setup, with shipping something imperfect and improving it in public, because that's what PlayFundWin and LeaguePages already demand of you. You're sitting inside a Claude powered production pipeline right now, this very podcast is built by an agent committing code and dispatching a build workflow, so you have more hands on agentic coding experience than the vast majority of people posting on that Hacker News thread. The gap between "read about this idea" and "have a working prototype by tonight" is smaller for you than it is for almost anyone else who might hear this.

Three runner up ideas from the same thread, evidence quality noted honestly for each. One, a single purpose boring utility web tool, someone posting as skwee357 said a send a fax online style tool was, quote, consistently grossing over five hundred euros a month, unquote. Thin evidence, one unverified comment, no growth trajectory given. Two, a niche desktop tool for developers, a lightweight database client, someone posting as upmostly said theirs had just crossed one thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue about two months after an October launch. Also thin, one self reported figure, and desktop distribution is more fiddly to ship and update than a browser extension. Three, a real time AI copilot for a specific professional niche, live prompts for recruiters on calls, someone posting as Frajedo said they'd, quote, just signed our third customer, six hundred euros a month, unquote. Weakest evidence of the four, three customers is not a business yet, it's three conversations that went well, and building live audio AI integration solo is a meaningfully bigger lift than a browser extension. I'd rate that one at more like sixty to seventy per cent solo buildable, not ninety. All four of these came from people posting under handles, not verified identities, so treat every figure here as "a stranger said so on the internet", useful as a directional signal about what's working, not as due diligence you'd do before actually investing real money.

Part four. One action today.

Here's the thing to actually do, and it should take you well under thirty minutes. Open whichever AI tool you personally use most, ChatGPT, Claude, or NotebookLM, and for the next twenty minutes, just use it normally while paying close attention to the moment you get annoyed. Not "what feature would be nice", but the actual, specific instant where you think, why doesn't this just do the obvious thing. Write that single sentence down. That sentence is your entire validation step for today's build idea, and it costs you nothing but attention you were about to spend anyway. If nothing annoys you in twenty minutes, that's useful information too, it probably means you're not a heavy enough user of that particular tool to build the fix yourself, so ask someone on your team who lives in it all day the same question instead, and let them do the noticing for you.
