Part two. The build.

Right, today's deep dive. This one's a genuine change of category from the last two episodes — we've done directories, we've done browser extensions, so today it's a marketing automation tool. Specifically: AI-powered community outreach.

The evidence comes from an Indie Hackers interview published on the eighteenth of December last year, with a founder called Richard Wang, who built a product called Leadmore AI. In his own words, quoted directly in that piece: Leadmore AI is doing over thirty thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue, and it's still growing quickly. That's the one hard, quotable figure in the piece — no customer count, no launch date given, so treat everything else as context rather than proof, but that revenue line is real, named, and dated.

Here's what the product actually does. It watches a platform — in this case Reddit — for conversations relevant to a business. An AI model reads the threads, scores how relevant they are, and drafts a reply in the business's voice: helpful, on-topic, not obviously salesy. That draft either goes to a human for approval or gets posted automatically, and results get logged so you can see what's turning into actual leads. In other words, it's AI-assisted organic outreach instead of cold email or paid ads.

Why this is buildable fast: strip it down and it's four components. Something that polls a platform's API for new posts and comments. A prompt that scores relevance and drafts a reply in a defined voice. A simple database to track what's been actioned and what came of it. And a basic scheduler so it runs without you babysitting it. That's not a hard stack, and Wang himself said in the interview that with modern AI-assisted coding, a basic working version can ship in one to two weeks. Using Claude Code specifically, a UK builder could realistically get a single-platform, single-niche version live in days rather than weeks — this is squarely inside the kind of build Claude is good at end to end: the API integration, the prompt design, the lightweight backend, all of it.

Cost to get moving, kept honest and conservative: a domain for about ten pounds a year, hosting on something like Railway or Fly dot io at fifteen to twenty-five pounds a month, API usage while you're testing at twenty to fifty pounds a month, Stripe costs nothing to set up, and registering as a sole trader is free — or fifty pounds if you want to incorporate properly from day one. All in, you're comfortably testing this for somewhere between one hundred and fifty and three hundred and fifty pounds. Well under our five-hundred-pound ceiling.

If you actually wanted to build a week-one version, here's roughly how the days break down. Day one is picking the platform and the niche together — not separately, because the platform's automation tolerance should drive the niche choice, not the other way round. Day two is the API integration and a basic polling loop, which for most platforms with an official API is genuinely a few hours of Claude-assisted work, not days. Day three is the prompt: writing and testing the relevance-scoring and reply-drafting prompt against real historical posts from the community, so you can see failure modes before a human ever reviews a live draft. Day four is the review queue — a deliberately unglamorous screen where drafts sit until a human approves them, because skipping this step is exactly how you end up posting something that reads as spam. Day five is logging and a basic dashboard, so you can see what's converting, and a manual test run on a handful of real threads before you let it run unattended. Notice that four of those five days are about restraint and review, not generation — the AI drafting the reply is the easy twenty per cent of this build.

Now the honest risks, because this is where it gets uncomfortable. Reddit, and most communities like it, explicitly ban bulk automated posting and self-promotion in their terms. Get caught and accounts get shadow-banned or banned outright — that's not a minor inconvenience, it's existential to the model, because your entire distribution channel can vanish overnight with no appeal. There's also basically no moat here. It's an API call and a prompt — anyone else with Claude can build a competing version in a similar number of days, so differentiation has to come from niche selection and quality of execution, not the tech itself. Low-effort AI replies read as spam fast, and a client's reputation takes the hit before yours does. And that thirty-thousand-dollar figure is self-reported in an interview, not something independently audited, so hold it a little more loosely than a Stripe screenshot.

Where does someone like you have an edge? You already run three businesses with defined, narrow communities around them — sports club treasurers and fundraising volunteers, grassroots league organisers, EV site hosts. If you were going to try a version of this, I wouldn't point it at Reddit at all — I'd point it at somewhere with looser or friendlier automation norms and a tighter audience, like monitoring specific Facebook groups or forums for club treasurers asking "how do we run a raffle" and having a human-reviewed, clearly-branded reply ready to go, rather than trying to pass as an organic redditor. Same mechanics, much lower platform risk.

One more thing on risk, because I don't want to undersell it. The reason this category keeps producing thirty-thousand-dollar-a-month stories is precisely the reason it's fragile: there's no technical moat, so the founders who win are the ones who move fast and pick a defensible niche before everyone else notices. By the time a business model shows up in an Indie Hackers interview, the easy version of it is usually already getting harder to run, because platforms notice pattern-of-behaviour before they notice any one individual account. That's not a reason to skip it — it's a reason to treat the first month as a live experiment with an exit plan, not a business you've committed the next year to.

Three runner-ups, evidence a bit thinner but all real and named. First, Bearconnect dot io, LinkedIn automation and multi-account management, founder Pratham Naik, Indie Hackers post from the fourth of November last year: one thousand three hundred dollars a month in recurring revenue. Real and dated, but modest, and LinkedIn automation is a genuinely crowded space — evidence strength I'd call medium. Second, Meerkats dot ai, an AI go-to-market orchestration platform, founder Santanu Dasgupta, Indie Hackers post from the fifth of May this year: three thousand dollars a month reached within four weeks of launch, built using Claude and Gemini and the Claude Skills SDK. Strong and recent, but the actual product is a multi-agent orchestration platform, which is a lot more complex than a days-long build — you'd want to clone one narrow workflow out of it, not the whole platform. Third, AI Flow Chat and Starpop, content tools for marketers and e-commerce brands, founder Alexander Van Le, Indie Hackers post from the eighteenth of February this year: twenty thousand dollars a month combined across both products. That combined figure is the weak point — no per-product breakdown — but the build-speed claims are concrete: one product shipped in a week with sales in the first week, the other in two weeks off a single viral post.

If I had to rank all four for someone in your position specifically, I'd put the main pick first precisely because the underlying mechanic — watch a community, draft a relevant reply, log the result — is the most transferable to your existing audiences. The three runner-ups are all real and well-evidenced, but Bearconnect and Meerkats both compete in spaces that are already crowded with well-funded players, and the AI Flow Chat pairing is really a lesson in build speed more than a template you'd copy directly. None of these are recommendations to drop what you're doing — they're evidence that the pattern works, at small scale, for people with no more resources than you have.
