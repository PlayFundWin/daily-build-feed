Keep your experimental agent workflows away from anything touching live customer data or live payment rails until you have tested the boundary yourself.

Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is a booking and scheduling tool, and I want to be upfront about why I picked it and where the AI angle genuinely is, versus where it isn't.

The evidence comes from a product designer who goes by Chetan, who built a tool called Schedo, a branded booking page for solo service providers, think estate agents, pet sitters, coaches, financial advisers, personal trainers, recruiters, anyone who currently sends people a link to book time with them. He published his own numbers on Indie Hackers, not a journalist's estimate, his own figures. His very first paying customer, a local window-cleaning business, signed up at twelve dollars a month. Within two months of launch he had roughly seven premium subscribers. By around April last year he'd grown that to five thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue.

Here's the part I want to be straight with you about. Schedo itself is not an AI product. Chetan, who taught himself to code, spent about six months building the first version, then another four months adding Stripe payments and calendar integrations, roughly ten months of solo, spare-time work. He marketed it the unglamorous way: word of mouth through colleagues, posting in Slack and Discord communities relevant to his target users, engaging on LinkedIn and Reddit, a brief and, in his words, unsustainable paid-search experiment, before settling into content and search as his real channel.

So why is this today's pick rather than a straightforward AI success story? Because the product itself is a few days' build for Claude today, not ten months of hand-written code. The category, hosted booking page plus calendar sync plus Stripe, is genuinely simple once you're not building it from scratch. And the real opportunity is bolting on exactly what Chetan didn't have time for: an AI concierge embedded on the booking page that answers a prospective client's questions before they book, AI-drafted reminder and no-show follow-up messages that sound like a person wrote them, or an AI summary of intake notes waiting for the provider before the appointment even starts. That's your differentiation against Calendly and Acuity, which are template-driven and generic. A solo operator, using Claude to build it, could plausibly ship a working version in days, not months.

Let's talk build steps, roughly how you'd actually sequence this. Start with the booking mechanics only, no AI at all, because that's the part that has to work perfectly before anything clever gets layered on: a public booking page, a calendar that blocks out real availability, and Stripe collecting a card on file or a deposit. Claude can scaffold that in an afternoon using an off-the-shelf calendar API rather than building scheduling logic from first principles, which is exactly the part that ate most of Chetan's six months. Once bookings and payments work end to end and you've tested it on yourself for a week, then add the AI layer: a small, tightly scoped assistant that only answers questions from a fixed knowledge base you write yourself, availability windows, pricing, cancellation policy, nothing freeform. Freeform is where hallucination lives, and hallucinating your own cancellation policy to a paying customer is the fastest way to lose their trust. Keep the concierge boring and accurate before you make it clever.

Honest money maths. Domain registration, a few pounds a year. Hosting on something like Vercel or Railway, free tier to start. Stripe takes its standard transaction cut but charges nothing upfront. Calendar sync through Google's or Microsoft's free developer APIs. You could realistically be live for under fifty pounds, well inside the five-hundred-pound ceiling I use as a rule of thumb for these ideas. Chetan's actual revenue curve, twelve dollars from customer one, five thousand dollars a month within roughly a year, is a reasonable base case if you execute the marketing as patiently as he did. It will not happen faster just because AI wrote the code; the code was never the bottleneck, the customer acquisition was.

The risks, honestly stated. This is a crowded category. Calendly, Acuity, Cal dot com, and a dozen smaller players already exist, so your AI concierge angle needs to be genuinely useful, not a gimmick bolted on for the pitch deck. Vertical focus matters enormously here, a generic scheduling tool competing head-on with Calendly will lose; a scheduling tool built specifically for, say, grassroots sports coaches booking pitch slots, or fundraising event volunteers booking shifts, has a much clearer wedge. And building the AI concierge well, so it actually answers questions correctly instead of hallucinating your cancellation policy, takes more careful prompt and guardrail work than the scheduling mechanics themselves.

There's also a slower, less dramatic risk worth naming: novelty fatigue. An AI concierge is a genuine differentiator today, in August of twenty twenty six, precisely because most small booking tools still don't have one. That window won't stay open forever, Calendly and Acuity both have the engineering resource to ship their own version the moment enough of their customers ask for it. Which is another argument for the vertical route over the generic one, a big incumbent chasing every vertical at once moves slowly on any single one of them, but a niche competitor who knows grassroots sports fixtures inside out can out-execute them on the details that actually matter to that specific customer, in the time it takes the incumbent's product team to even schedule the meeting.

Here's where Steve's existing assets genuinely give an edge. You already run three platforms that involve people booking things: PlayFundWin, where supporters engage with prize draws on a schedule, LeaguePages, where grassroots leagues manage fixtures, volunteer slots, and pitch bookings, and EV-Partnerships, where venues need to coordinate installer site visits. Rather than building a generic Schedo clone and hoping to out-market Calendly from zero, the sharper move is a vertical scheduling and AI-concierge layer purpose-built for one of those three worlds, grassroots sports fixture and volunteer booking is the most obvious fit, where you already have relationships, credibility, and a warm audience that a stranger with a Show HN post doesn't have. That is the whole advantage: distribution before you write a line of code.

Picture the first ninety days if you went this route. Weeks one and two, build the booking and calendar core and quietly test it on one friendly grassroots club you already have a relationship with through LeaguePages. Weeks three and four, add the narrow AI concierge, availability questions and volunteer reminders only, and watch where it gets confused, because it will, and fix the knowledge base rather than the model. Month two, offer it free to five more clubs in exchange for honest feedback and, ideally, a testimonial you can actually use. Month three, put a price on it, even a small one, twelve dollars a month worked for Chetan's very first customer, because a club willing to pay a small amount tells you far more than one taking it for free ever will. None of this requires you to guess at a big number up front, it requires you to get five real clubs through the whole loop and see what breaks.
