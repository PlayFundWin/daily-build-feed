Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is a UK-founded product, which doesn't happen often on this show, so pay attention. It's called My Ask AI, built by Mike Heap and his co-founder Alex Rainey. Here's what it is in one sentence: you feed it your own help docs, your FAQ pages, your knowledge base, and it becomes a chatbot on your website that answers visitor questions using your own content, and hands off to a human the moment it can't help.

The numbers, and I want to be precise about where these come from because that matters more than the number itself. This is all from a Starter Story interview with Mike Heap. Five thousand dollars in pre-launch sign-ups within two weeks, back in February twenty twenty-three. Roughly seven thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue within two months of actually launching. Twenty-five thousand dollars a month at the time of the interview. And the headline figure the piece leads with: three hundred thousand dollars a year in annualised revenue. There's also a secondary, unverified estimate floating around from a company-data site called GetLatka, putting recent annual revenue somewhere between one hundred and seventy and two hundred and twelve thousand dollars, but that's a third-party guess, not something Heap published himself, so treat the Starter Story numbers as the real headline and that one as background colour only.

How it was actually built, and this is the part that should make your ears prick up: the founders put the first version together in about two weeks using Bubble, a no-code tool, wired up to OpenAI's API plus a couple of off-the-shelf pieces for content ingestion. That's it. No custom machine learning. No proprietary model. It's a retrieval layer, meaning it searches your own documents for the relevant bit, then hands that to a language model to phrase the answer. Swap OpenAI's API for Claude's, swap Bubble for a straightforward web build, and you're looking at a genuinely achievable few-days project, comfortably under five hundred pounds to stand up once you're just paying for hosting and API calls.

If you wanted to sketch the actual build steps, they'd look roughly like this. Step one, pick one willing club or charity partner and pull together everything they'd want answered, their FAQ page, their rules, their gift aid explainer, their fixture and membership pages. Step two, get that content into a simple searchable index, what's called an embeddings database, so the system can find the right paragraph for any question asked in plain English. Step three, wire up an embeddable chat widget that takes a visitor's question, searches that index, and passes the result to Claude to turn into a proper, friendly answer. Step four, add a simple fallback, if confidence is low, hand off to a real person by email rather than guessing. Step five, test it for a week on real visitor questions before you ever charge anyone a penny for it. None of that is exotic engineering. It's an afternoon's work per step for someone who knows their way around a web build, which is exactly the kind of project this show exists to point you toward.

Now here's the bit I actually think matters most for you specifically. Every single sports club, grassroots league and charity you already work with fields the exact same repetitive questions, over and over, from members, donors and participants. How do I enter the prize draw. When's the next fixture. How does gift aid work. Can I renew my membership online. Most indie hackers building a product like this have to go find that audience cold, through search ads or content marketing or endless outreach. You don't. PlayFundWin and LeaguePages already sit inside hundreds of these relationships. A white-labelled "answers your club's questions" bot isn't a new business, it's a bolt-on to two businesses you already run, sold into a customer base that already trusts you.

Honest money maths, kept conservative. Say you price it as a simple add-on at fifteen to twenty-five pounds a month per club, sitting alongside your existing PlayFundWin or LeaguePages relationship rather than as a cold sell. Fifty clubs signed up gets you somewhere around nine hundred pounds a month in genuinely low-effort recurring revenue, on top of tooling costs that should stay in the tens of pounds a month range at that scale. That's not the three hundred thousand dollars a year headline, deliberately, because My Ask AI had to build a market from nothing and you wouldn't be. It's a smaller, steadier number built on distribution you already own.

Scale that up honestly and it still holds together. Two hundred clubs, which is a real number given how many organisations already touch PlayFundWin and LeaguePages between them, gets you toward three and a half thousand pounds a month before you've done anything clever like tiered pricing or an annual plan with a discount. That's not going to replace either core business overnight, and it isn't supposed to. It's the kind of steady, low-maintenance add-on revenue that compounds quietly in the background while you focus on the main event.

Risks, because I'm not going to sell you a fantasy. This category isn't empty. Intercom's Fin, Tidio and Crisp all offer AI-answers-from-your-docs features already, and some clubs might already be quietly using one bundled into a platform they pay for. Your edge isn't the technology, plenty of people can build this. Your edge is distribution and a specific, narrow niche nobody bigger has bothered to serve well: UK grassroots sports and community fundraising. You'll also be touching donor and member data, so whatever you build needs proper data handling from day one, not bolted on afterwards, particularly given everything we've covered this week about compliance tooling. And content goes stale. Someone has to keep the FAQ source pages current, or the bot starts confidently answering with last season's fixture list. One more risk worth naming plainly, because it's the boring one that actually kills projects like this: support burden doesn't disappear, it just moves. Someone still has to review the questions the bot couldn't answer, retrain the awkward edge cases, and make sure it never confidently invents a fundraising rule that isn't true. Budget real time for that, not just the initial build.

Three runner-ups, briefly, all with real named evidence behind them. First, Robopost, an AI social scheduling tool built by a founder who goes by Mako, who posted on Indie Hackers about reaching fifty-five thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue within a year. Strong number, but it needed proper integrations with Meta, LinkedIn, X and TikTok's advertising and posting systems, which is meaningfully more backend work than a few days, and most of that growth came from paid Meta ads spend rather than organic distribution. Second, Magai, a multi-model AI content platform built by Dustin Stout, which passed two million dollars in lifetime revenue this June per its own press release, after an earlier Indie Hackers milestone of twenty thousand dollars a month at just a hundred and sixty-three days old. Genuinely impressive, but it's now a full platform with dozens of integrations, not a weekend build anymore. Third, and I'll flag this one carefully given what we discussed back in episode seven, Photo AI, the AI headshot generator built by Pieter Levels, which reportedly passed a hundred thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue within nineteen months and now sits somewhere around a hundred and thirty-five thousand dollars a month. The build itself is refreshingly simple, plain code wired to an image API. But two honest caveats: most of that growth rode on Levels's own following of over six hundred thousand people, built over a decade, and it sits in the same broad image-generation category as the merch mockup idea you told me you're not interested in. Different use case, personal headshots rather than club design work, but close enough in spirit that I wanted to name it rather than quietly skip past it.

One more thing worth saying about why the UK angle matters here specifically. Most of the AI chatbot tooling flooding this space right now is built and marketed by American companies, with American pricing, American support hours, and American assumptions about things like gift aid, which frankly don't exist anywhere else. A version of this built with British fundraising and grassroots sport in mind, sold by people who already understand the sector, isn't a small differentiator. It's most of the pitch.

My pick stays the chatbot. Real UK evidence, buildable in days, and it plugs straight into distribution you already have.
