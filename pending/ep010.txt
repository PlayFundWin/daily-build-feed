Part one. What shipped.

First, and I read this directly from Anthropic's own announcement, so full confidence on the facts. From yesterday, the fourteenth of August, Claude has started embedding an invisible watermark inside the text it generates. It works by subtly altering which words the model picks at each step, in a pattern that's detectable by the right tool but doesn't change the answer you actually get, no hit to speed, cost or quality. This isn't Anthropic being cautious for its own sake. It's compliance with the European Union's AI Act code of practice on transparency, which around one hundred and ninety companies signed up to back in July. New Claude output gets watermarked by default going forward, older models get it rolled out gradually over the coming months, and anything that's an image rather than text, so a PNG, JPEG or SVG, gets a different kind of credential baked into its metadata instead. Why you care: if you or anyone on your team pastes Claude-written copy into a newsletter, a grant application or a website, and any part of your audience sits in the EU, assume AI-content detectors are about to start correctly flagging it as machine-written a lot more often than they used to. Worth knowing before a funder's compliance team notices before you do. It's also a reminder that this kind of quiet regulatory plumbing tends to arrive from Brussels first and everywhere else eventually, so if you're drafting anything client-facing with Claude's help, keep a light human editing pass in the loop rather than pasting output straight through.

Second, and this one I also read straight from the source. Google launched Gemini three point seven Flash yesterday, the thirteenth, pitched specifically at coding and agent work rather than general chat. On Google's own benchmarks it beats the previous Flash model by a wide margin on coding tasks, roughly forty-four per cent versus thirty-four per cent on one measure, and on long-running engineering tasks, roughly sixty-five per cent versus forty-nine per cent. It holds a million tokens of context, meaning it can hold a genuinely large codebase or document set in its head at once, and Google's launch pricing runs at less than half of what the previous Flash model cost, through the end of the year. It's live now inside Google's AI Studio and a few other Google developer tools. Why you care: this is exactly the kind of model that makes DIY internal tooling cheaper this quarter, not next year. If Claude Code or Cursor is your daily driver, this doesn't replace either, but it's a genuinely useful, genuinely cheap second option worth having in the toolbox for anything coding-adjacent, particularly if you're the kind of person who likes to prototype something scrappy on a Saturday afternoon before committing real time to it.

Third, and I'll be straightforward that this one's aimed at companies much bigger than yours, but it's worth twenty seconds because it tells you where the big labs are pointing their sales teams. IBM and OpenAI announced a formal partnership yesterday, the thirteenth, confirmed from IBM's own newsroom. IBM will build OpenAI's models into its consulting arm, and stand up a dedicated in-house practice of consultants certified on OpenAI's tools, aimed at large financial services, government, telecoms and retail clients modernising old systems. Why you care: not because you're an IBM client, you're not, but because it's a signal that enterprise AI-migration consulting is about to become a much bigger, much more normal line of business. If that trickles down into smaller consultancies over the next year, as these things tend to, it's worth remembering you saw the shape of it here first. It's also a useful gauge of how seriously the big, cautious, slow-moving organisations are now taking this stuff, which tends to be a leading indicator for what your own funders and partner organisations will expect from you eighteen months from now.

Fourth, a small one with a genuinely funny footnote for you specifically. OpenAI shipped a new opt-in macOS feature called Computer History, confirmed from their own release notes, thirteenth of August. Turn it on and ChatGPT can quietly reference which apps and websites you've had open, so you stop having to re-explain your own context every single session. It's off by default. And here's the footnote: it is not available in the European Economic Area, Switzerland, or the United Kingdom. So if you were about to get excited about it, don't bother, it doesn't work here yet, almost certainly a regulatory decision rather than a technical one. Why you care: mostly so you don't waste ten minutes looking for a toggle that isn't there. Worth watching for whether or when it eventually lands in the UK, and worth noting as one more small data point in a pattern we've flagged before on this show, that the newest, most convenience-focused AI features often reach the UK and the EU later than everywhere else, usually for regulatory reasons rather than technical ones.

Fifth and last on the news, a genuine quality-of-life upgrade if you're a Cursor user. Cursor's cloud agents now boot from a pre-built snapshot of your project instead of setting everything up from scratch every single time, confirmed from Cursor's own changelog, thirteenth of August. Roughly ten times faster to get an environment running, three times faster before the model even starts producing its first response. It's free, bundled into cloud agents you're probably already using. Why you care: if you or anyone building for you runs more than one Cursor agent in parallel, and this show has flagged that as a genuinely useful pattern before, this removes a chunk of the dead waiting time between kicking a task off and actually seeing it start working. Small upgrade, but small upgrades to tools you already use every day add up faster than flashy new launches you'll never actually touch.

Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is a testimonial and social-proof tool, and I want to be upfront that this is a genuinely different shape of business to anything we've covered on this show so far. It's not a directory, not a browser extension, not an outreach bot, not a voice app, not a chatbot trained on your documents. It's simpler than all of those, and the evidence behind it is some of the strongest we've had.

The company is called Senja, built by two co-founders, Wilson Wilson and Olly Meakings, one based in Nigeria, one in the UK, so this isn't even a purely American case study for once. Here's what it does in one sentence: businesses send their happy customers a simple link, the customer records a short video or types a quick quote, and Senja turns that into an embeddable widget, a scrolling wall of praise you drop straight onto a website or a landing page.

The numbers, and I want to walk through the actual timeline because it matters more than the headline figure on its own. Both founders published their milestones openly as they went, mostly on Indie Hackers and their own blog. First paying customer, June twenty twenty-two. One hundred dollars a month in recurring revenue by August that year. One thousand dollars a month by December. Five thousand dollars a month by May twenty twenty-three, which Olly himself called out as hitting his first real indie-hacking goal. Seven and a half thousand dollars a month, what they called ramen profitable, by July twenty twenty-three. Ten thousand dollars a month by August that year. Thirty thousand dollars a month by May twenty twenty-four. Fifty thousand dollars a month by October twenty twenty-four. And the headline figure, current as of November last year, so genuinely fresh evidence rather than something we're dusting off from years ago: eighty-three thousand three hundred dollars a month in recurring revenue, a million dollars a year, with over three thousand paying customers.

What I like about this evidence chain specifically is that it isn't a single lucky spike. It's two years of steady, published, step-by-step growth, which tells you the honest truth about this kind of business: it compounds slowly and then it compounds a lot, rather than exploding overnight. It's also worth saying plainly that both founders were building in public the entire way, posting the boring middle months alongside the exciting milestones, which is exactly the kind of receipts this show looks for before it puts a number in front of you. No single unverified screenshot, no one press release, just a paper trail stretching back three years.

How it was actually built, and this is comfortably within reach for you. At its core it's a collection form, somewhere for a customer to type or record their feedback, a bit of processing to clean up and format that into something presentable, and a widget generator that spits out a simple bit of embed code anyone can paste into their own website. None of that needs custom machine learning. A modern build would run on something like Claude Code scaffolding out the collection form, a database, and the embed widget, wired to Stripe for billing. Realistically a working first version in a handful of days, and well under five hundred pounds to get live, mostly hosting and a bit of API usage while you're testing.

If you wanted to sketch the actual build steps. Step one, a simple collection page where someone can type a quote or record a short video testimonial, no login required, so there's zero friction for the person you're asking a favour of. Step two, a lightweight cleanup pass, trimming a rambling video down to the good bit, or tidying grammar on a written quote without putting words in anyone's mouth. Step three, a widget generator, so a business can grab a small piece of code and drop a wall of genuine praise straight onto their own site. Step four, simple billing, a free tier capped at a small number of testimonials, a paid tier once someone wants more. Step five, and don't skip this one, get five real UK small businesses actually using it before you spend a single hour worrying about what it looks like at scale.

Now here's the bit that matters most for you specifically, and it's a genuinely good fit. Every club, every charity, every fundraising campaign you already touch through PlayFundWin and LeaguePages has exactly the kind of story that makes a testimonial land: a member who won a prize draw and it funded new kit for the under-elevens, a charity that hit a fundraising target it had been chasing for two years, a league that finally got its website sorted after a decade of a spreadsheet held together with hope. That's better raw material than most testimonial tools ever get handed, because it's emotionally real rather than someone saying a piece of software is "intuitive."

There's a second layer to this worth spelling out, because it's easy to miss on first listen. A testimonial tool built for your world doesn't just sell your own two platforms harder. It's also something PlayFundWin and LeaguePages could offer their own club and charity partners as a feature, letting a club collect and display its own supporter stories on its own page, powered by you, branded as part of what signing up with you gets them. That's the difference between building a side project that competes for attention against your two main businesses, and building one that quietly strengthens both of them at the same time.

Honest money maths, kept conservative. Price it as a simple add-on, somewhere around ten to fifteen pounds a month per club or charity, sitting on top of your existing PlayFundWin or LeaguePages relationship rather than as a cold sell to strangers. Fifty clubs signed up gets you somewhere around six hundred pounds a month, on tooling costs that should sit in the tens of pounds a month range at that scale. Two hundred clubs, a real number given how many organisations already touch your two platforms between them, gets you toward two and a half thousand pounds a month. Not going to replace either core business, and it isn't supposed to. It's steady, low-maintenance revenue that compounds quietly while you focus on the main event, much like Senja's own two-year climb.

Risks, because I'm not going to sell you a fantasy. This category isn't empty. Senja itself, plus Testimonial dot to and Endorsely, are already established, well-funded competitors in the general market. Your edge isn't the technology, plenty of people can build a form and a widget. Your edge is the same one we keep coming back to on this show: distribution into a specific niche nobody bigger has bothered to serve, UK grassroots sports and community fundraising, plus raw material, real emotional stories, that a generic tool aimed at software companies simply doesn't have access to. You'll be handling video and personal quotes from real supporters, so you'll want straightforward, honestly-worded consent and data handling from day one, not bolted on after the fact. And it took Senja about nineteen months to reach ramen profitability from their first paying customer. This is a slow, steady build, not a weekend lottery ticket, and for someone already running two other ventures, that's arguably a feature rather than a bug. One more practical risk worth naming: video testimonials specifically need somewhere to be hosted and streamed, which is a small but real technical decision to get right early, since a testimonial wall that loads slowly undermines the exact trust it's meant to build.

Three runner-ups, briefly, each with its own real evidence. First, Tweet Hunter, an AI-assisted social scheduling and content tool built by Tom Jacquesson and Tibo Louis-Lucas, one hundred dollars in revenue within three days of launch back in twenty twenty-one, twenty-one thousand dollars a month by that November, a million dollars a year by the following May, per their own Indie Hackers interview. Strong numbers, but this evidence is four to five years old now, flagged honestly on air, and the category's more crowded today. Second, Zigpoll, an AI-assisted survey and customer-feedback tool built solo by Jason Zigelbaum, currently running at around one hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars a month, a million and a half dollars a year, as of this June, with forty-four per cent growth in the first half of this year alone, per his own Indie Hackers post. That's the freshest evidence of the three, essentially this month, but it's a more technical build, squarely aimed at e-commerce and software companies rather than clubs and charities. Third, and I like this one for its honesty, ColdClicks, an AI cold-email personalisation tool built by William Paul, six hundred dollars a month in recurring revenue within six weeks of launch, published on his own Indie Hackers page in April last year. Genuinely modest, included on purpose, because it's a useful reminder that the actual bar for a first paying customer sits a lot lower than the headline six and seven-figure stories on this show tend to suggest.

My pick stays the testimonial tool. Real, current evidence, buildable in days, and it turns stories you're already collecting into something that sells your next club or charity partner for you.
