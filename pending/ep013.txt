Part two. The build.

Today's idea is something a bit different from what we've covered before on this show: a competitor pricing-intelligence tool. Not a directory, not a chatbot, not another content generator. Something that just watches prices, quietly, and tells you when to move.

The evidence, and I want to be upfront that this one comes with an important caveat before I give you the numbers. A founder called Nemanja Rakocevic, who goes by Misha, built a price-monitoring and pricing-analytics tool called Price2Spy, aimed at online sellers who want to track what their competitors charge. He posted an AMA on Indie Hackers laying his numbers out plainly: fifty-five thousand dollars in revenue in twenty twelve, rising to a hundred and sixty-seven thousand dollars the following year, then twenty-five to thirty per cent growth year on year after that, all the way up to two million dollars a year by the time he wrote the post. Bootstrapped. No outside investors. Over seven hundred paying e-commerce clients by then.

Here's the caveat, and I'd rather say it plainly than dress it up. Price2Spy took Misha roughly a decade to build into that two-million-a-year business, starting from an idea in twenty ten. I am not telling you that you'll be anywhere near two million dollars by Christmas. What his numbers actually prove is something narrower and more useful: that tracking what competitors charge, and turning it into a plain-English recommendation, is a real, durable, bootstrap-friendly category that people have paid for, year after year, at meaningful scale. What's changed since twenty ten is the build time. What took Misha years of hand-rolled scraping infrastructure, Claude Code can get you a working first version of in days, not years.

How you'd actually build it. Start narrow, one category, not the whole internet. Set up scrapers using something like Playwright against a defined list of competitor product pages; Claude Code can write and maintain these for you, including handling the annoying bits, pagination, the odd site that quietly changes its layout without warning. Run it on a schedule, once a day is plenty to begin with, and diff today's prices against yesterday's. Then the actual AI part of the product: instead of spitting out a raw table of numbers nobody will read, have a model turn the price changes into something a busy person will actually act on. Something like, your closest competitor cut kit prices by twelve per cent this week, here's what I'd do about it. Wrap that in a simple dashboard, something like Next dot J-S with Supabase underneath, bolt on Stripe for billing, and you have a sellable product. Domain, hosting, a scraping proxy service if you end up needing one, and Stripe's fees, and you're comfortably under five hundred pounds to get a first version live.

A couple of build details worth getting right from day one rather than fixing later. Keep what you store to the price and the timestamp, not a copy of the whole page, that keeps your data footprint small and sidesteps most of the copyright grey area around scraped content, even if the legal question around the scraping itself still needs that solicitor conversation. And because your customers would be UK clubs and suppliers, host the data in the UK or EU and be upfront in your terms about what you collect, standard practice for anything remotely GDPR-adjacent, and one less thing to retrofit once you have paying customers who ask.

The honest, conservative money maths, two ways to look at it. If you charge something modest for a narrow, well-targeted version of this, say twenty to forty pounds a month, you don't need Misha's seven hundred customers to make it worthwhile. Fifty clubs or suppliers paying thirty pounds a month is fifteen hundred pounds a month, which is a genuinely useful side income for something that runs largely on its own once it's built, and it compounds every time you add another product category. Push it further and it still holds up conservatively: two hundred customers, a realistic ceiling if you actually land the LeaguePages upsell across even a fifth of the clubs already on your platform, at that same thirty pounds a month is six thousand pounds a month, without ever touching a market outside people who already know your name.

The risks, because it would be doing you a disservice not to say them out loud. Scraping is fragile by nature. Sites change their structure, add anti-bot protection, or simply don't want to be scraped, and you'll spend real, ongoing time keeping scrapers alive rather than adding features. Some bigger retailers actively block automated price-checking, so you may need a scraping proxy service, which eats straight into a thin early margin. And there's a genuine legal grey area around scraping publicly available pricing pages that's worth ten minutes with a solicitor before you sell this commercially, not something to simply wing and hope nobody minds.

There's also an ongoing-time cost most people underestimate when they picture this kind of product as "set it and forget it." Realistically budget an hour or two a month per category to fix a broken scraper or add a supplier who's changed their site. That's not a reason to skip the idea, it's a reason to price it properly from day one rather than underpricing yourself into resenting your own product six months in.

Now here's where it gets genuinely interesting for you specifically, because this is the bit a generic pricing tool can't touch, and it's the whole reason I picked this idea for you over something flashier. You don't need to build pricing intelligence for e-commerce in general, that market's crowded and you'd be starting from zero distribution against people who've been at it for years. Three warm distribution channels, built in from day one, that most solo founders trying this exact idea simply don't have. That's the edge worth building around, and it's the reason this idea beats a dozen flashier ones that don't fit your actual assets.
