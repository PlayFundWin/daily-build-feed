Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is a content-repurposing tool for podcasters — you upload one episode, and the AI turns it into a full transcript, show notes, chapter timestamps, a newsletter draft, a blog post, social copy, quote-card images, and short audiogram clips, all from that single recording.

The evidence here is unusually well documented, across several sources that all line up with each other rather than one thin claim repeated everywhere. The product is called Podsqueeze, built by a founder named Tiago Ferreira with a co-founder, launched in January twenty twenty-three. Tiago posted his own numbers on Indie Hackers: six and a half thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue within two months of launch, built off the first ten customers found through a single Reddit community for podcasters. A separate interview on a site called FounderClix has him at nine thousand dollars a month, over one hundred thousand a year, by month five, with three hundred and twenty paying users. And a case study published on a newsletter platform called Beehiiv lays out the full climb month by month: eight hundred dollars in March, two thousand in April, six thousand six hundred in May, ten thousand by July, fourteen thousand three hundred in October, with ten thousand seven hundred of that as profit. A later podcast interview puts the business at sixteen thousand dollars a month roughly eighteen months in. Every figure there is Tiago's own, stated by him, not estimated by me.

Here's how the pipeline actually works, and why it's genuinely close to a few days' build with today's tools rather than a specialist engineering project. Someone uploads an audio file or pastes a YouTube link. A transcription service — something like AssemblyAI or Deepgram, or Whisper if you want to run it yourself — turns that into a timestamped transcript. That transcript gets fed to an AI model, and Claude will do this job well, with a set of prompt templates: one for show notes, one for chapter markers, one for a newsletter draft, one for a blog post, one for a handful of social posts and pull-quotes. A tool called FFmpeg, fully scriptable, cuts short audio clips for audiograms, and a simple template generates quote-card images. Underneath all of that sits a usage-metering table, because pricing here is based on minutes of audio processed per month, and a dashboard where the customer downloads or copies out whatever they need. None of that is exotic. It's five or six API calls stitched together behind a subscription paywall, and the actual skill isn't the code, it's writing prompts that produce show notes and blog posts genuinely worth publishing rather than generic AI sludge.

A rough build order, if you wanted to sketch it: day one, get the upload and transcription flow working end to end, one real episode in, a clean transcript out. Day two, wire in the content-generation prompts and get one complete set of outputs — notes, blog post, social copy — even if the wording needs work. Day three onward, add the audiogram clips and quote cards, put a Stripe subscription in front of it, and get your first real user, ideally someone whose podcast you already listen to, testing it on an actual episode rather than a demo file.

Two practical details worth knowing before you start, because they're the sort of thing that only shows up once you're actually building. First, transcription cost is your main variable expense, and it scales with audio length, not with how much content you generate from it, so a customer with three long weekly episodes costs you meaningfully more than one with a single short monthly show — price by minutes processed, not by a flat monthly fee, or your unit economics quietly go negative on your heaviest users. Second, the quality of every downstream output — show notes, blog post, social copy — depends entirely on transcript quality, so it's worth spending real time on transcription accuracy and speaker labelling before you spend any time polishing the prompts that turn that transcript into finished content. A slightly wrong prompt produces mediocre copy you can iterate on. A slightly wrong transcript produces confidently wrong copy, which is a much worse customer experience.

Now the honest, conservative money maths, because Tiago's numbers came from eighteen months of genuinely hard outreach work, not a growth hack. Podsqueeze's pricing today runs from around nine dollars a month for a light plan up to around ninety dollars a month for an agency tier, so think in similar bands if you're pricing a UK version — somewhere between eight and eighty pounds a month depending on how much audio a customer processes. A realistic first month is close to nothing, maybe your first two or three customers found through direct outreach to podcasters you already know or follow. By month three, if you've built even a small amount of word of mouth and you're consistently posting in podcasting communities, ten to twenty paying customers at an average of, say, twenty-five pounds a month puts you somewhere between two hundred and fifty and five hundred pounds a month. By month six, with a reasonable content-marketing habit and maybe a Product Hunt launch behind you, one to three thousand pounds a month is a fair, unstretched target. Getting anywhere near Tiago's numbers would take the same thing his did: hundreds of cold emails a day, sustained for months, which is unglamorous work most people underestimate before they start and give up on once they realise what it actually takes.

The risks are worth sitting with. This category has real competition now — Castmagic, Descript, and a handful of direct Podsqueeze-alikes are all fighting for the same customer, so differentiation matters more than it did when Tiago started in early twenty twenty-three. Transcription and generation costs scale with usage, which means a heavy user on a capped plan can quietly erode your margin if you're not enforcing limits properly. Transcription accuracy on genuinely poor audio — someone recording on a laptop mic in a noisy room — creates support headaches that a text-only SaaS never has to deal with. And churn is a real risk once a podcaster clears their content backlog and stops needing you every week.

There's also a distribution lesson buried in Tiago's own numbers that's easy to skip past. His first ten paying customers came from one Reddit community, not a launch campaign — he went where podcasters already gather and were already complaining about exactly this problem, rather than trying to build an audience from nothing. That's a repeatable move for almost any niche tool: find the subreddit, the Discord, or the Facebook group where your exact buyer already vents about the exact problem you're solving, and show up there with a working demo before you show up anywhere else. It's slower and less glamorous than a big launch, but it's also nearly free, which matters a great deal when you're testing whether an idea has legs at all.

Three runner-up ideas the research turned up, each worth a sentence in case podcasting isn't your world. An AI quiz and assessment generator called Questgen, built by a solo founder named Ramsri Goutham Golla, documented on his own account at three to four thousand dollars a month with roughly seventy per cent margin — sellable to UK teachers, corporate trainers, and e-learning providers. A pair of single-purpose speech-writing tools, an eulogy generator and a wedding speech generator, from a small studio called Niche Mates — Indie Hackers' own verified figure for the eulogy tool alone is five hundred dollars a month, though a separate, less-verified source claims the pair combined reach twelve thousand a month, so treat that higher number with real caution until someone confirms it directly. And an AI virtual staging tool for property photos, called IACrea, built by a founder named Pauline Clavelloux, documented at roughly eight and a half thousand euros a month — upload an empty or dated room, get back a furnished, renovated version for a property listing, genuinely useful for UK estate agents right now.

Notice what all four of today's ideas have in common, because it's a pattern worth carrying forward past today's episode. Every one of them is a single, narrow job done well for a specific buyer who already knows they have that job — a podcaster with a backlog, a teacher who needs a quiz, someone writing a eulogy under real time pressure, an estate agent with an empty room to photograph. None of them are trying to be a platform. That narrowness is precisely what makes them buildable by one person with an AI coding assistant in days rather than months, and it's the same discipline worth applying to whatever you build next, LeaguePages blog included: pick the one job, for the one buyer, and resist the urge to generalise it before you've proven it works once.

And here's the part that actually matters for you specifically. You already produce a podcast. This very show gets a full script written and, per how this pipeline works, that script gets kept as a permanent transcript rather than thrown away after each episode airs. That's raw material sitting there right now for exactly the kind of repurposing Podsqueeze charges other people money for — a blog post, a set of social clips, a newsletter version, all pulled from an episode you've already made. You don't need to build the full multi-customer product to get value from the idea; you could prototype the single-user version against your own back catalogue this week and know within days whether it's worth productising for anyone else. And if you're about to build a blog section for LeaguePages, as you mentioned wanting to do earlier today, notice the shape of the two ideas sitting next to each other: one turns podcast audio into written content, the other turns fixture and results data into written content. Same underlying pipeline, different source material. Worth keeping that in mind if you end up building both.

Part four. One action today.

Thirty minutes, no more. Since you're already looking at adding a blog section to LeaguePages, do the one step that actually determines whether it works before you write a line of code: pick one real club, and write down ten keyword phrases that pair the club's name, its town, and its age group or league, phrased the way an actual parent would type them into a search bar — something like "under twelves football" and the town name, or the club's name plus "fixtures" plus the town. That's the entire input a content pipeline needs to get right before a single blog post gets generated, and it's exactly the step Podsqueeze and Journalist AI both depend on getting right first. Don't touch the pipeline itself today. Just get real keyword data down on a page for one club, based on how people actually search, not how the club would describe itself. If you want a second angle on the same thirty minutes, read the keyword phrases back out loud — if any of them sound like something a marketing department would write rather than something a parent would type into a phone at nine at night trying to find a Saturday match, cut it and try again.
