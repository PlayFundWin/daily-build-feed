Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is a scheduling tool called Postiz, and I want to walk you through the numbers in order, because the shape of the growth is the actual lesson, not just the final figure.

Postiz is a social media scheduler, open source, built by a solo founder named Nevo David. It posts to more than twenty eight platforms: X, LinkedIn, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Reddit, Discord, Bluesky, the lot. In September twenty twenty four, Nevo posted on the developer community site Dev dot to that he was making seven hundred dollars a month from it. That's a founder-stated figure, from his own post, and it's a completely unremarkable number. Most side projects stall around there.

Then, according to a case study published by Superframeworks in July this year, something changed. Early this year Postiz was sitting around seventeen thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue, with around four hundred and seventy paying subscribers. By March it had ticked up to twenty one thousand. Then in April it jumped to eighty thousand. By June it was at a hundred and eighteen thousand. And by the second of July, the case study puts it at a hundred and forty five thousand dollars a month, growing by roughly a thousand dollars a day. I'll flag the confidence level honestly: that trajectory comes from one case study source, so treat the exact weekly numbers as directionally strong rather than independently audited. But it's corroborated from a second angle. A separate revenue tracking site called TrustMRR currently lists Postiz at a hundred and eighty one and a half thousand dollars in the trailing thirty days, which is roughly in line with where that growth curve would land if it kept climbing through the summer.

So what actually happened between seventeen thousand and a hundred and forty five thousand in four months. Not a new feature. A repositioning. Early this year, Nevo rebuilt Postiz's pitch around one sentence: this is a scheduling tool for AI agents, not just for humans. He shipped a command line interface and an MCP server, which is the connector standard that lets an AI agent call a tool directly, so that any AI agent workflow could post to social media as one of its own actions rather than a human copying and pasting into a dashboard. That launched at almost exactly the moment a viral open source agent framework called OpenClaw took off, and every builder experimenting with autonomous agents suddenly needed a way for their agent to actually post somewhere. Postiz was sitting right there with the plumbing already built.

Here's why I think this is genuinely buildable, not just a nice story. The hard part of a scheduling tool has never been the scheduling. It's the dozens of individual platform integrations, the OAuth handshakes, the rate limits, the API quirks of X versus LinkedIn versus TikTok. That's exactly the kind of well documented, repetitive integration work Claude is fast at. And you don't even need to build every platform connector yourself from scratch. Unified social posting APIs already exist specifically so you don't have to; you wrap one of those, add a scheduling calendar, add Stripe billing, and you have a sellable minimum product in days, not the roughly two years Nevo actually spent getting here.

It's also worth pausing on that MCP piece, because it's a bigger story than one scheduling tool. MCP stands for model context protocol, it's the emerging standard that lets an AI agent discover and call a piece of software's functions directly, the same way a person would click a button in an app. What Nevo actually built wasn't just an API, it was a small piece of infrastructure that any AI agent, from any framework, could plug into and use to post on someone's behalf. That's the same pattern showing up again and again this year: the winners aren't necessarily the tools with the best human interface anymore, they're the tools that made themselves easy for an agent to operate. If you're building anything at all right now, it's worth asking the same question about your own product. Could an AI agent use this without a human in the loop. If the honest answer is no, that's a gap, and increasingly, a gap worth closing before a competitor does.

Now, the honest, conservative money maths, because I'm not going to sell you the top-line number as your starting point. You are not going to out-Postiz Postiz. It's open source, it's well known, and going head to head with an established free alternative is a losing game for a first product. The move is the same one that worked for Nevo: pick a narrow slice nobody's serving well. Two directions, both realistic. One, a scheduler built specifically for a single vertical Postiz doesn't care about, say UK tradespeople and local service businesses, wired into Google Business Profile alongside Instagram and Facebook, sold on "get found locally" rather than "post everywhere." Two, lean directly into the same agentic angle that worked for Postiz: sell API access to the growing pool of UK founders and freelancers building their own AI agent workflows, who need a reliable "post this to social media" tool call and don't want to build the plumbing themselves.

Let me actually break down what building this looks like, week by week, because "buildable with Claude in days" is a claim I should back up rather than just assert. Week one is wiring: pick a unified social posting API, connect it to two or three platforms first rather than all of them, get one post going out end to end from a basic form. That's a couple of days with Claude doing the integration grunt work. Week two is the calendar and scheduling logic, plus Stripe for billing, plus a genuinely narrow landing page that names the niche in the first sentence, not "social media made easy" but "posting for your five-a-side league, sorted." Week three, if you've got a real audience to test on, which Steve does through LeaguePages, is showing it to twenty actual clubs and watching what breaks. That's roughly three weeks to a sellable first version, not the two years Nevo spent, because you're not building the twenty eight platform integrations he eventually needed, you're building the three that matter to your niche.

Costs to get a first version live: a domain, call it ten pounds. Hosting on something like Render or Fly dot io, free to start, maybe fifteen pounds a month once you have real users. A reseller tier on a unified social API, somewhere between thirty and a hundred pounds a month depending on volume. Stripe is free until you're actually taking payment. All in, comfortably under five hundred pounds to get to a working, sellable version. The risk is real, and I'll say it plainly: this is a crowded category with strong free incumbents, and if you build the generic version you will lose to Postiz, to Buffer, to Hootsuite, every time. The whole bet rests on the niche being genuinely narrow and genuinely underserved. Don't skip that part. The second risk is platform dependency: every social scheduler lives or dies by API access, and any of these platforms can change their terms, rate limits, or pricing for third party posting tools with very little warning. Build assuming at least one integration breaks a year, and don't put all your eggs in a single platform's basket.

And here's the edge Steve specifically has that a random builder doesn't. LeaguePages already sits inside hundreds of grassroots sports clubs' weekly workflow, and every one of those clubs has someone, usually unpaid, badly juggling posting fixture results and sponsor shout outs across three or four platforms by hand on a Sunday evening. A scheduler built into LeaguePages itself, sponsor logo watermarking, fixture result templates, automatic posting the moment a result gets entered, isn't a cold-start product. It's a feature with a captive, already-trusting audience on day one. That's not a general observation, that's a specific unlock only this build idea plus this existing asset combination gives you.

Three runner ups, quickly, each with its own real evidence and each in a category we haven't touched before on this show.

First, a post-purchase feedback platform called Zigpoll, founder Jason Zigelbaum. According to his own post on Indie Hackers, he started this year at just over a million dollars in annual recurring revenue and has grown it to a hundred and twenty five thousand dollars a month, a forty four per cent increase in the first half of the year, aiming for two million in annual revenue. The interesting bit for the script isn't the number, it's how he got the jump: he didn't ship a big new feature, he re-priced for one specific customer segment, agencies managing multiple client stores, and that alone drove the growth. Worth remembering: sometimes the unlock is pricing, not product. For PlayFundWin specifically, that's a useful nudge too: before building anything new, it's worth asking whether there's a segment already on the platform, a bigger club, a regional league body, that would happily pay a different price for a slightly different package of the exact same tools you already have.

Second, an Instagram and WhatsApp AI sales assistant called SetSmart, founder goes by Victor. TrustMRR lists it at roughly twenty four thousand dollars a month recurring, nearly sixty thousand in the trailing thirty days, growing twenty seven per cent month on month, with a seventy per cent profit margin. It automatically qualifies leads and books sales calls through DMs for coaches and course creators, hooking into Calendly and GoHighLevel. A UK version of this for personal trainers or online coaches, built on the WhatsApp Business API, is a very buildable weekend project, and there's an obvious grassroots-sports flavour of it too: a club membership enquiry bot that answers "do you have space for an under-tens team" on WhatsApp at eleven at night, when the volunteer secretary is asleep, and books the parent straight into a trial session.

Third, and I'm including this one specifically because it's small and early, which is its own kind of useful evidence: an AI helpdesk tool called Ticketdesk AI, founder Vikash Rathee, founded July last year. TrustMRR shows it at just over two thousand dollars in the last thirty days, under seventeen thousand all time, but a ninety six per cent profit margin. It's currently listed for sale at a hundred and one thousand dollars. That's proof a modest, honestly small, single founder tool in a crowded category like helpdesk support can still be real, monetising, and eventually sellable. Not every good build idea needs to hit six figures a month to be worth doing.

Put those three side by side and there's a pattern worth naming. None of them are the biggest player in their category. All three found growth either by re-pricing for a specific segment, by riding a platform shift nobody else had positioned for yet, or by simply being small, focused, and honestly run. None of that requires being first to market. It requires being specific about who you're for.
