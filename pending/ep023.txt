Part one. What shipped in the last two days.

The story that actually matters this week is a fight, not a feature. SpaceX has acquired Cursor, the AI coding tool a huge number of developers use every day, and OpenAI's response, posted on its own site, was blunt: it is winding down the contract that gives Cursor access to OpenAI's models, with a cutoff date of the twelfth of November. I read OpenAI's post directly. The stated reason isn't technical, it's trust. OpenAI points at what it calls SpaceX's compliance history, and specifically at past contract breaches connected to Twitter and to Musk's own AI company, xAI, as grounds for not wanting its models running inside a Musk-owned product any more. This is the Musk-Altman rivalry escalating into actual infrastructure, and if you or anyone on your team builds inside Cursor and leans on GPT models to do it, the twelfth of November is now a real deadline, not an abstract one. Worth flagging on air because it's exactly the kind of dependency that's invisible until the day it isn't: check what your tools are actually calling under the bonnet.

It's also worth sitting with what this actually says about how these companies see each other. OpenAI didn't cite a technical failure, a security hole, or a broken integration. It cited trust in the owner. That's a different kind of risk than the one most of us plan for when we pick a tool, because it has nothing to do with the product and everything to do with who happens to sign the cheque behind it, and it can change overnight for reasons that have nothing to do with your business at all.

Away from the Musk-Altman soap opera, Google DeepMind ran what it's calling the world's first double-blind AI evaluation of a frontier model, on the twenty-seventh of August. I read the announcement myself on this one. The setup uses something called Google Cloud Confidential Space, essentially a cryptographic box, to test a Gemini Flash Lite model in a way where the evaluators never see the prompts they're being tested with, and DeepMind never sees the model's weights. The point is to stop the two oldest tricks in AI benchmarking: labs quietly training on the test, and evaluators' methods leaking back to the lab being graded. Partners on this include Singapore's AI Safety Institute, a privacy-tech nonprofit called OpenMined, and the industry benchmarking body MLCommons. Nothing here changes what you can build tomorrow, but it's the kind of trust plumbing that eventually decides whose safety claims you can actually believe, so it's worth a line rather than a headline. The reason it earns even that much airtime is what it implies is coming: if labs start submitting to independently verified, tamper-proof evaluations as a matter of course, the marketing claims every vendor currently makes about their own model's safety and capability get a lot harder to hide behind. For anyone buying AI tools rather than building the underlying models, that's a slow-moving but genuinely useful shift, because right now you're mostly taking a vendor's word for it.

Anthropic also had something on the twenty-eighth: Claude for Teachers is now generally available to entire American school districts, not just individual teachers signing up on their own. The programme itself launched back in the middle of July, so this isn't a brand-new product, it's Anthropic widening the door from one classroom to a whole administration deciding to switch everyone on at once. If you sell anything into schools, or you're watching where the big labs think the next wave of "safe" institutional AI adoption is going to come from, that's the signal: education, at district scale, is now a real sales motion for these companies, not a side project.

And then there's the quiet bit, which is its own small story. I checked ElevenLabs, Replit, Lovable and Bolt directly, and none of them shipped anything dated in this window. Cursor's own "build from scratch with no repository connected" feature, which we covered last time, is still the freshest thing out of that whole agent-tooling cluster, and now it's sitting under a company that just had its main model supplier walk out. Draw your own conclusions about the timing.

One more thing worth flagging before we move on, mostly so you know it was checked rather than skipped: there was a report doing the rounds this week about an internal OpenAI model that reportedly chained together real exploits to breach systems at Hugging Face during a security test back in July. That's already been covered on this show, and the underlying incident is more than a month old now, so I'm not re-running it as news, I just didn't want a Hugging Face headline sitting in your feed with no context if you'd seen it elsewhere.

If you're running a small AI-powered business, or several, the Cursor story is the one to actually sit with, because it's a preview of a problem that gets more common as this industry consolidates, not less. Every tool in your stack that leans on a third party's model, whether that's a coding assistant, a customer-support bot, or a content generator, carries a second, invisible layer of risk sitting underneath the product risk: who owns the company you're renting the model from, and does the model supplier trust them. Most of the time that's a non-issue. Occasionally, as this week shows, it isn't, and the people affected get roughly eleven weeks' notice to sort themselves out. The cheap insurance is knowing, today, which of your tools that applies to, rather than finding out on the morning of the twelfth of November.

Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is a lesson in restraint, because the most tempting move with an idea like this is to force it onto one of your three ventures, and I'm not going to do that, because it doesn't actually fit, and pretending otherwise would waste your time.

The product is Three-Dee AI Studio, built by a founder named Jan Hammer, out of Stuttgart. It takes a text prompt or an image and turns it into an actual three-dimensional model file, ready to drop into a game engine like Unity or Unreal, or send straight to a three-dee printer. Hammer's own account of building it, published as a Starter Story case study, is refreshingly ordinary: Django and Python on the back end, Node and React on the front, hosted on AWS and Vercel, and for the actual model generation, he didn't train anything himself, he called AI models already hosted on Hugging Face. That last part is the whole lesson. He built a login system, a billing system, and a nice interface, and wrapped it around somebody else's generation engine. Total build cost, by his own account: two hundred dollars.

The results, and I want to flag upfront that these come from Hammer's own posts and interviews rather than an audited set of accounts, so take the trajectory as directionally real rather than to the last dollar. Launch day, back in February twenty twenty-four: three thousand users, five thousand within four days, two hundred and eighty-four of them paying, and roughly three thousand five hundred dollars a month in recurring revenue, profitable from day one on that two-hundred-dollar spend. By the six-month mark, in a post on Ex, Hammer put the figure at three hundred thousand dollars a year in recurring revenue, calling himself a one-person team running at sixty per cent profit margin. An interview with Authority Magazine a little further on puts it at one point seven million euros in annual recurring revenue within the first twelve months, and more than seven hundred thousand creators on the platform. And by his own site today, the number he quotes is roughly two million dollars a year, with over a million users total.

It's also worth being clear-eyed about how Hammer actually found his first customers, because that part is less glamorous than the revenue graph suggests. He didn't buy his way to three thousand day-one users. By his own account, he built in public on Reddit and Discord communities full of indie game developers and three-dee printing hobbyists, posting demos and progress updates as he went, so that by the time the tool was ready, there was already an audience waiting to try it rather than a cold product hoping to be found. That's a slower, less comfortable path than paid acquisition, and it's also the part almost nobody copies when they retell this kind of story, which is exactly why I'm flagging it rather than skipping straight to the money.

Here's why the underlying shape is worth stealing rather than the specific product. Hammer wasn't the one doing anything clever with the actual artificial intelligence. Companies like Meshy, Tripo Three-Dee and Rodin now sell exactly the kind of text-to-three-dee generation he was originally scraping together from Hugging Face, as a straightforward pay-per-generation application programming interface. Which means the hard part of his original build, stitching together a rough generation pipeline from open models, doesn't need doing any more. What's left is the part an AI coding agent is genuinely good at: authentication, a credit or subscription billing system tied to Stripe, a clean upload-and-download interface, and export formatting for whichever engine your customer uses. Realistically, with Claude doing the heavy lifting on code, you're looking at five to ten days to a working version, and total cost, once you add a starter bundle of generation credits from one of those commercial APIs on top of hosting and a domain, comfortably under five hundred pounds. Which is, not coincidentally, almost exactly what Hammer himself spent, just in a different currency and a slightly kinder market.

Roughly how those days break down, if you wanted a plan rather than a vague estimate: the first day or two goes on picking and testing a generation API against a handful of real prompts, because that decision shapes everything downstream. The middle stretch, three or four days, is the actual application: sign-up and login, Stripe subscriptions or credit packs, the upload-a-prompt-get-a-model interface, and hooking it all to the chosen API. The last couple of days go on the parts that are boring but decide whether anyone pays twice: sensible error handling when a generation comes out badly, clear export options for the file formats your buyers actually need, and a basic gallery or history page so a returning customer can find what they made last time. None of that is exotic, which is precisely the point.

None of that makes it easy money, so let's be straight about the risks. Your margin is capped by what the underlying three-dee generation API charges you per call, because you are, in the most honest sense, reselling somebody else's model with a nicer wrapper on it. Quality and consistency across different prompts varies more than you'd like on every text-to-three-dee tool I've looked at, which means customer support load is real. The buyer base, indie game developers, hobbyist three-dee printer owners, and virtual and augmented reality creators, is a genuine niche rather than a mass market, so Hammer's actual growth engine was distribution on Reddit, Discord, and short-form video demos, not search traffic, and that's work you'd have to do yourself rather than skip. And you'd be stepping into a category where Meshy, Tripo and Luma are already funded, established, and actively competing on price, so you'd need either a sharper sub-niche or a meaningfully better bit of export tooling to have a reason to exist. As I said at the top, I don't see a natural door from any of that into prize-draw fundraising, grassroots league websites, or destination EV charging, and I'd rather tell you that plainly than manufacture a connection that isn't there. If you were going to build it anyway, purely as a standalone side project rather than something tied to the existing businesses, I'd treat it as a two-week evaluation, not a two-day one: spend the first few days getting quotes and sample outputs from two or three of the commercial generation APIs before you write a line of the wrapper, because the single biggest variable in whether this makes money isn't your code, it's whether the underlying model you're reselling actually produces output good enough that a stranger will pay for it twice.

Three runner-ups, lighter detail, all backed by named evidence rather than guesswork. First, FounderPal, built by Dan Kulkov and Sveta Bay, an AI marketing co-pilot that generates strategy documents, buyer personas and prompt packs. Documented at sixty thousand dollars earned across its first six months and roughly ten thousand dollars a month in one-time payments as of April twenty twenty-four, built without writing a line of code on a platform called Bubble, running on about one hundred and fifty dollars a month in hosting and AI costs. Second, Tech Lockdown, from a founder called Ben Bozzay: device and network-level content filtering paired with an accountability layer, aimed at adults who want serious help staying off distracting or harmful sites. It reportedly reached twenty thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue, up from under two thousand dollars a month with around three hundred customers when he started it during a layoff in twenty twenty-three. Fair warning on this one: the filtering engine itself is real infrastructure work, not something an agent knocks out in an afternoon, so the honest build here is the guidance and accountability layer around it, not the whole product, and I'd flag it as the least turnkey of the four ideas in today's list. And third, ByeDispute, from the prolific indie builder Marc Lou: a tool that helps merchants prevent Stripe payment disputes before they escalate into an account ban. The revenue here is small and genuinely volatile, three hundred and thirty-three dollars in one month, a hundred and fifty-two dollars in another, by Marc's own public monthly reporting, but it's real, it's dated, and it comes from someone who posts his numbers in public every single month whether they're flattering or not. The build itself, a Stripe integration paired with templated dispute responses, is about as close to a genuine few-days agent build as you'll find on this list.

Part three. Apply it.

Starting with PlayFundWin. The Department for Culture, Media and Sport's call for evidence on how National Lottery good-causes funding gets distributed is still open, and it closes on the twenty-third of September, which puts you at roughly three and a half weeks out as of today. This is the first review of that funding since nineteen ninety-four, sport holds a statutory twenty per cent share of it, and the call explicitly invites grassroots input. Grassroots clubs are structurally bad at responding to consultations like this compared with national charity policy teams who do it professionally, and that gap is exactly where a considered PlayFundWin submission, or a template you hand to the clubs on your platform, could actually move a needle rather than just sitting in a folder. The lowest-effort version of this is refreshingly simple: a one-page plain-English template a club treasurer can fill in and submit in ten minutes, distributed through the platform you already have, would likely outperform a single polished PlayFundWin submission on volume alone, and volume is what a call for evidence is actually counting. I'd treat that closing date as a real one, not a someday one.
