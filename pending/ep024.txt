This is The Daily Build, episode twenty-four, for Sunday the thirtieth of August, twenty twenty-six. Quick housekeeping before we start: everything here is research and ideas, not financial advice, so do your own numbers before you spend a penny.

Let's start with Anthropic, because it's the strangest release of the week. On Thursday the twenty-seventh of August, Anthropic opened a research preview of something it's calling the Model Hardware Standard. Think of it as an equivalent of MCP, but for physical equipment. Instead of writing bespoke integration code every time you want an AI agent to talk to a microscope, a liquid handler or a robotic arm, labs get one shared interface and the agent simply drives it. Anthropic's own claim is that integration work drops from weeks to hours. I read the announcement myself on this one, so it's solid. Why you care, even if you've never been near a lab in your life: this is the same trick that made MCP useful for software agents, now pointed at hardware. It's aimed at biotech and manufacturing today, not small businesses, but it's worth watching, because this exact pattern, standardise something fiddly and then let agents drive it, tends to show up in warehouses and logistics long before it shows up anywhere consumer-facing.

Google had an unusually busy few days on the API side, and both items are the kind of thing that changes what's worth building without anyone announcing it loudly. On the twenty-sixth of August, Gemini three point five Transcribe and Transcribe Live went generally available. That's high-accuracy transcription across more than eighty-five languages, with speaker separation, word-level timestamps, and support for up to a thousand custom vocabulary terms, plus a low-latency streaming version built for live audio rather than uploaded files. The next day, the twenty-seventh, Gemini Omni Flash's video tools also reached general availability, adding the ability to extend an existing clip and to interpolate between a first and last frame, with resolution now going up to four K. Both of these I've confirmed straight from Google's own developer changelog, not a secondary write-up. Why you care: if you've been putting off building a call-transcription feature, a meeting-notes tool, or a short-form video pipeline because the underlying models felt like preview-quality toys, that excuse just expired. This is production-grade, production-priced infrastructure now, with an SLA behind it. One date worth writing down if you're already building on the old video preview endpoint: it switches off on the thirtieth of September, so migrate before then or your pipeline stops working with no warning.

Cursor shipped something smaller but properly clever on the twenty-seventh. Its Cloud Agents no longer need a connected GitHub repository to start working. You describe what you want, Cursor spins up a repository behind the scenes under its own new product, called Origin, gives you a live preview in the browser as it builds, and lets you publish straight to Vercel with one click. I want to be precise about what this is and isn't, because it sits right next to a bigger story we covered on Friday. It is a separate, smaller feature. It is not part of the SpaceX and OpenAI situation, where OpenAI announced it's cutting Cursor off from its GPT models by the twelfth of November, following SpaceX's acquisition of the company. That thread is still open, and I'll come back to it nearer the date, particularly if Cursor says anything about a fallback plan. But today's change stands on its own two feet: it's a genuine drop in the barrier between having an idea and having a live web address, with nobody making you set up version control first.

Replit made two changes on the twenty-eighth worth knowing about, and the second one is the more interesting signal. First, its agent now automatically routes each task to whichever model and effort level fits the job, rather than leaving you to pick manually and eat whatever cost difference that choice implies. Second, it bundled in something it's calling Growth Skills, pre-wired integrations with tools like Apollo, Clay, Stripe and PostHog, aimed specifically at getting the app you just built its first actual users rather than just shipping working code and leaving you to figure out distribution on your own. That second part matters more than it sounds. The AI app-builder market is quietly admitting, out loud now, that building the thing was never the hard part. Getting anyone to use it was always the hard part, and that's where the tooling is heading next.

And Lovable, on the twenty-sixth, added a native Slack integration. You can chat with a build by mentioning it directly inside Slack, publish a finished app as a standalone Slack app without needing a developer account of your own, and let it work continuously on a single instruction for up to ten hours before checking back in with you. If you run a small team that already lives inside Slack for everything else, that's a genuine shortcut to an internal tool that nobody ever has to leave the app to use.

Part two now, and this is the one I want you to actually sit with, because the evidence is a bit strange and I think being upfront about that serves you better than smoothing it over into a clean pitch.

The company is Comp AI, built by founder Lewis Carhart along with co-founders Mariano Fuentes and Claudio Fuentes. What it does is automate the paperwork side of security and data-protection compliance, things like SOC two, ISO two seven oh oh one, HIPAA and GDPR, generating the actual policies, collecting the evidence an auditor wants to see, and getting a company from having no idea what its data policy even is to being audit-ready in what the company claims is two to three weeks, instead of months spent working with a consultant. It launched out of stealth in April last year and raised two point six million dollars in pre-seed funding in August twenty twenty-five, led by OSS Capital and Grand Ventures. That funding figure is corroborated in trade coverage, not just a company press release, so I'm comfortable with it.

Here's where it gets strange. A Stripe-linked revenue tracker called TrustMRR, updated on the twenty-ninth of August, lists Comp AI's current monthly recurring revenue at twelve thousand one hundred and eighty-five dollars. Fine, that's a real, healthy number for a young company. But the same page lists last-thirty-days revenue at six hundred and sixty-six thousand, five hundred and fifty-one dollars. Those two figures should not be that far apart for a normal subscription business, and I don't have a clean explanation for the gap. My best guess, and I want to flag clearly that this is a guess rather than something I've confirmed anywhere, is that it reflects large annual contracts being booked in full the moment they're signed, which can spike a thirty-day revenue snapshot without moving the underlying recurring number much at all. Take the six hundred and sixty-six thousand figure with real caution. Don't repeat it as if it were monthly recurring revenue, because on the evidence in front of me, it almost certainly isn't.

The twelve-thousand-dollar monthly figure is more believable on its own, and it's backed up by named customer case studies rather than a single flashy number: Persona AI citing four hundred thousand dollars of annual revenue unlocked by getting compliant faster, RiskInMind citing a hundred-thousand-dollar deal that hinged on having the paperwork ready, and Patentia citing fifty thousand dollars of deals unlocked within three months of starting to use the tool. Put together, that paints a smaller but more credible picture: a real, growing business-to-business product, more than a thousand customers, positioning itself as an open-source alternative to the bigger incumbents in this space, the likes of Vanta and Drata, who charge enterprise prices for broadly the same job.

I'm spending this much time on one messy revenue figure for a reason. It would have been easy to open this section with "a compliance tool doing six hundred and sixty-six thousand dollars a month" and let that be the headline, because it's a striking number and nobody listening would have checked it against the source. That's exactly the kind of shortcut this show doesn't take. The underlying business is still a good one, the smaller number still stands up, and you don't need the inflated figure to make the case for building something in this space.

Now, to be clear, you are not building Comp AI. You don't need the full audit platform, the evidence-collection engine, or the actual SOC two certification machinery behind it. What you're building is a much narrower slice of the same underlying idea, and it happens to fit a market Steve already sits inside like a glove.

Grassroots sports clubs and small charities in this country need a specific, boring, completely unavoidable stack of paperwork just to function. A safeguarding policy for anyone working with under-eighteens. A data protection policy for anything holding member details. An equal opportunities policy that funders and County Football Associations ask for before they'll even look at a grant application. And, because PlayFundWin's whole world involves running prize draws, documentation that actually reflects real Gambling Act obligations rather than a template someone downloaded a decade ago and never looked at again.

Here's the build, and it's properly small. A short structured questionnaire, how many volunteers, what data you hold, whether you run prize draws, whether you take payments directly, built in something like Tally or Typeform in an afternoon. Those answers feed a prompt to Claude, which drafts each policy from a set of vetted templates that you control, not from whatever Claude happens to remember about UK charity law off the top of its training data. Turn the output into a branded PDF or Word document, put a Stripe checkout in front of the whole flow, and you're done. Domain name, a handful of pounds a month in hosting, and API costs measured in pennies per document generated. This comfortably clears the five-hundred-pound ceiling with plenty of room to spare.

The honest, conservative money maths: sell finished policy packs somewhere between forty-nine and ninety-nine pounds a time, or run a lighter ongoing subscription, call it fifteen to twenty-nine pounds a month, for clubs who want their policies kept current as rules change around them. Twenty one-off sales a month at sixty-nine pounds gets you to roughly one thousand three hundred and eighty pounds monthly revenue against costs that barely register against it. That's nowhere near Comp AI's territory, and it shouldn't be. This is a lean, solo-buildable slice of the idea, not a funded platform play, and treating it as anything more than that would be setting yourself up to be disappointed.

The risk that actually matters here is liability, not competition. If a generated safeguarding policy is wrong and a club relies on it, that's a real problem, not a hypothetical one, so you need an unambiguous "this is not legal advice, have it checked by your governing body" disclaimer sitting on every single document, and you may want to deliberately keep the scope to lower-stakes policies rather than anything that could be read as formal legal sign-off. Trust is the other genuine hurdle. A volunteer club treasurer with no legal background has no real way to judge whether the output is actually correct, so any credibility signal you can borrow, a named reviewer, a partnership with a governing body, testimonials from clubs who got a grant approved off the back of using it, does far more work than the product itself ever will.

And the fit here isn't forced, which is rare enough to call out on its own. PlayFundWin already has a direct relationship with exactly this customer base. A policy-pack generator works perfectly well as a standalone lead magnet, or, more usefully, as a bolt-on that gives clubs a genuine reason to log into the platform between prize draws rather than only showing up when there's money to collect.

There's also a distribution advantage here that's easy to miss if you're only thinking about the product. Most solo builders in this space have to cold-start an audience from nothing, the way Comp AI itself had to when it launched, and the way Jan Hammer's Three-Dee AI Studio had to when we covered it last episode. You don't. Every club already on LeaguePages or running a draw through PlayFundWin is a warm lead for this specific product, because they already trust the platform and they already have the underlying problem, they just don't know yet that there's a cheap fix for it. That's the actual edge, more than the build itself, and it's worth remembering the next time an idea on this show looks unremarkable on paper but happens to land on an audience you already own.
