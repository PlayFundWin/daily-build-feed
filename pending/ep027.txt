Wednesday the second of September, and there's a new most-capable hacking model in the world, courtesy of OpenAI, on the very same day Anthropic made its own coding models substantially cheaper to run. Meanwhile, somewhere in Silicon Valley, a chat app that helps you flirt is turning over half a million dollars a month, which tells you something uncomfortable about what people are willing to pay for.

This is the Daily Build, episode twenty-seven, for the second of September, twenty twenty-six. As always, this is research and ideas, not financial advice — I'm not qualified to tell you what to do with your money, only to tell you what other people have apparently done with theirs. On today's show: a properly alarming new AI capability worth taking seriously, a price cut that should show up on your own bill without you lifting a finger, two other model updates worth thirty seconds each, a wingman app doing half a million dollars a month and what it teaches a fundraising or sports business, and what's moving — or stubbornly refusing to — across PlayFundWin, LeaguePages, and EV-Partnerships.

Part one. What shipped.

First up, and the more useful of today's two Anthropic-adjacent stories: on the first of September, Anthropic released Claude Fable five point one, its public flagship, alongside Claude Mythos five point one, a more restricted model that comes with a new Life Sciences Verification Program attached. I read the announcement myself. Both beat their predecessors — Fable five, Opus five, and OpenAI's GPT five point six Sol — on coding and research benchmarks, and Fable five point one throws roughly sixty per cent fewer false alarms on security-review tasks, which matters if you've ever had an AI tool cry wolf about a vulnerability that wasn't there. The bit that'll land on your invoice: cache-read pricing is down seventy-five per cent, which Anthropic says cuts typical workload costs by around twenty-five per cent, and by as much as forty-five per cent for heavier agentic work — the kind of thing this very show runs on. If you're already paying for Claude to do anything automated, there's a decent chance your next bill is smaller than your last one, and you didn't have to ask. It's a small thing on its own, but stack it against a few other model updates from the last month and the pattern is fairly clear: the cost of running an AI-powered small business is heading in one direction, and that direction is down, faster than most people are noticing month to month.

Second, and this is the one worth pausing on: also on the first of September, OpenAI announced Astra, which it says is the first model to cross what its own safety framework calls the "critical" threshold for cybersecurity capability. In plain terms, Astra can find and weaponise a zero-day exploit in a hardened system without a human walking it through the steps — it posted a perfect score on ExploitBench, an internal benchmark built for exactly this. OpenAI is keeping access locked down for now and released a defensive companion tool alongside it, branded Daybreak Blue. I read OpenAI's own post directly on this one, so it's about as solid as sourcing gets. Why you should care, even running a small operation: the gap between "sophisticated nation-state hacking tool" and "thing that exists in the world" just got smaller, and the basic cyber hygiene you've been putting off — proper password management, two-factor authentication everywhere that touches money, patched software — stops being a box-ticking exercise and starts being the actual thing standing between you and a much rougher afternoon. Nobody's suggesting Astra itself is coming for a grassroots sports club's website, but tools like this have a habit of trickling down into cheaper, less careful copies within months, and the businesses that get caught out are almost never the ones with a dedicated security team — they're the ones like yours and mine, running lean, betting nobody would bother.

Third: OpenAI also announced, on the first of September, that ChatGPT now integrates directly with Epic's electronic health record system, letting clinicians pull patient data into a ChatGPT query, alongside a separate Healthcare Public Data plugin that unifies sources like PubMed, DailyMed and the US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. I read that one directly too. This is squarely a US, HIPAA-flavoured story, so there's no immediate UK action item — but it's a signal worth clocking. When the big labs move into a regulated, high-stakes vertical, the compliance patterns they build tend to become the template everyone else copies, eventually, including here. Worth remembering next time you're weighing up building something in a regulated space of your own — health, finance, or, say, gambling and prize draws. The specific integration doesn't touch anyone listening today, but the direction of travel does: big labs building bespoke plumbing for one regulated sector tend to end up open-sourcing the pattern, if not the code, and a UK equivalent for something like grant compliance or Gambling Act paperwork wouldn't be a surprising next step within a year or two.

Fourth: Lovable, the no-code builder, shipped Custom API Connectors on the thirty-first of August, alongside priority-lane processing for supported Gemini models if you're willing to pay for it. Their own changelog confirms it, read directly. Under the old system, wiring up some bespoke third-party API inside a no-code build was one of the classic points where you'd hit a wall and need an actual developer. Now you can describe the API — its endpoints, its credentials, its documentation — and Lovable will generate the connector itself. If anyone on your team touches Lovable for internal tools, that's a real hour saved, possibly several — think a fixtures feed for LeaguePages, or a payment gateway for a PlayFundWin integration, the sort of unglamorous wiring work that used to eat an afternoon and now might not.

Put those four together and there's a theme, even if none of the individual companies would frame it this way: the tools you'd use to build a small AI business are getting cheaper and more capable in the same fortnight that the tools someone else could use to attack that business are getting sharper too. Neither side of that is going away, so the sensible response isn't panic, it's just making sure your side of the ledger — backups, authentication, who has admin access to what — keeps pace with how much easier everything else has just become.

One quick note on the Cursor story we've been tracking: no fresh movement since OpenAI confirmed it's cutting Cursor off from its models on the twelfth of November. I checked directly and found nothing new beyond what we already covered — still worth auditing your own tooling for OpenAI dependency before that date lands, but there's nothing today that changes the picture.

Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is Rizz, and I'll say upfront: this one doesn't niche neatly into any of your three ventures, so I'm not going to force it. What's transferable is the mechanic, and I think that's worth thirty seconds of patience before you decide whether to switch off.

Rizz is an AI dating assistant. You upload a screenshot of a chat, or a profile, and it reads the text and generates witty, personalised opening lines or replies for you to send. Founder Roman Khaves co-founded it in 2021, the app itself launched in December 2022, built with one other, unnamed co-founder. And the number worth sitting with: Starter Story published a founder interview on the eleventh of December 2024 — I read it directly — putting Rizz at five hundred thousand dollars a month in revenue, close to six million dollars a year, bootstrapped and profitable, sold on a seven-dollar-a-week subscription. That's not a lone tweet either. Forbes covered it on the ninth of September 2024, naming Rizz the fifth most downloaded dating app in the country at the time, and Time magazine put it on their Best Inventions list for 2024. Three separate named sources, three separate outlets. This one's about as well corroborated as these stories get. And it's worth noticing why the mechanic works so well commercially, beyond the dating angle itself: personalised, high-stakes text — the kind you're nervous about getting wrong — is exactly the situation where people will happily pay for a second opinion, whether that's a first message to someone they fancy, a description of a raffle prize, or an email to a sponsor. The category doesn't matter nearly as much as the nervousness does.

Here's the build, stripped down. You don't need a dating app licence or an app store presence to prove the concept — a web app is enough for version one, and it sidesteps a lot of platform friction. The pipeline is: an image upload screen, a vision-capable model that reads the text out of the screenshot, a carefully tuned prompt that turns that text into three to five reply options in whatever tone you're asking for, and a Stripe paywall sitting on top, weekly or monthly. None of that is exotic engineering. Break it into a realistic few-day plan: day one is the upload screen and getting a vision-capable model to reliably pull clean text out of a messy screenshot, which sounds trivial until you try it on a genuinely badly-lit phone photo. Day two is the prompt itself — this is where almost all the real work lives, because the difference between replies that sound like a person and replies that sound like a chatbot pretending to be a person is entirely in how carefully you've written and tested that prompt against real examples. Day three is the paywall, the account system, and enough of a landing page to not embarrass you. Day four, if you've got it, is testing the whole thing against thoroughly awkward inputs — dark photos, cropped screenshots, screenshots in a different language — because that's where a v1 built in a hurry usually falls over first. Claude Code can carry most of that build itself, end to end, including the Stripe integration, which is by now about as well-trodden a path as exists in this kind of tooling. Khaves himself has said the moat here was never the technology — it was positioning, timing, and relentless iteration on what people actually wanted to read back.

Now the honest money maths, because "half a million dollars a month" is the outcome after two years of grinding, not the starting point. Standing this up yourself: a domain name, a few pounds a year. Basic hosting, call it twenty to thirty pounds a month to start. Vision-model API calls billed per use — cheap at low volume, worth watching once you scale, since Astra-style cost creep isn't just a security story, it's a straightforward unit-economics one too. Stripe takes its usual small percentage plus a flat fee per transaction. All told, you could have something live and charging for well under five hundred pounds, likely closer to a hundred once you strip out anything speculative. If you priced it at, say, four or five pounds a week and picked up fifty paying users in your first couple of months — a modest, entirely plausible outcome for a niche version of this, not a viral one — you're looking at somewhere around a thousand pounds a month before costs. That's a very different number from half a million dollars, and it should be: Rizz got there through years of iteration and a genuine cultural moment, not a lucky first weekend. Treat the thousand pounds a month as the honest floor for a well-executed niche version, and treat half a million dollars a month as the ceiling that happens to maybe one attempt in a thousand — both numbers are real, they're just describing very different points on the same curve.

The risks are worth naming plainly rather than skating past. First, privacy: a screenshot of someone else's messages is, legally and ethically, their data too, and building a product around uploading other people's private conversations without their knowledge sits in seriously murky territory under UK data protection law — that's not a small print issue, it's a design decision you'd need to think through properly before launch. Second, the category is crowded and well-funded, and Rizz itself has strong brand recognition now, so a direct clone competing head-on would be fighting uphill. Third, dependency risk: anything reading screenshots off a third-party platform is at the mercy of that platform's own anti-bot and screenshot-detection rules changing under you. Fourth, and this one's easy to miss until it happens: vision-API costs scale with usage in a way that text-only tools don't, so a viral spike that looks like a great problem to have can turn into a seriously expensive week if you haven't put usage caps or a hard budget alert in place from day one.

Which is exactly why I wouldn't copy Rizz directly for any of your three businesses — I'd steal the mechanic instead. Read an image, generate persuasive, personalised text back. Picture a grassroots club uploading a photo of a donated raffle prize and getting back three ready-to-post descriptions pitched to get people buying tickets, built for PlayFundWin. Or a club committee forwarding a screenshot of a messy WhatsApp thread about Saturday's result and getting a clean, shareable match report out the other end, built for LeaguePages. Or, further out, an EV-Partnerships angle: a venue manager photographs their car park, and the tool reads the layout back and drafts the copy and floor-plan notes for a destination-charging pitch to send to an operator — speculative, admittedly, but built from the same three ingredients. Same underlying pipeline as Rizz every time — image in, tuned prompt, persuasive text out — applied somewhere you already have an audience, rather than somewhere you'd be starting from zero in a saturated, well-funded market.

Three runner-ups, all real, all named, all smaller and rougher-edged than Rizz, which is exactly why they're worth a mention. Baller: an AI app giving footballers personalised training drills and video analysis, founded by Lino Leighton, sitting at two thousand three hundred and thirteen dollars a month in verified subscription revenue as of this month, per TrustMRR's Stripe-linked listing, with three hundred and seventy active subscribers since launching in June. That's the closest thing I found this week to a sports-angle AI product with real numbers attached — worth watching as a training and coaching adjacency, distinct from what LeaguePages does on the admin side. Ploxto, an AI photo-editing app from a founder going by Manu, at one thousand seven hundred and seventy-seven dollars a month, over fifteen thousand dollars total revenue, three hundred and thirty-six subscribers, grown almost entirely off a TikTok presence that's racked up more than sixty million views. And Draft AI, from founder Valery Alexeev, which turns a voice recording into ready-to-post social captions and scripts — twenty-four thousand seven hundred and twenty-seven dollars a month in verified revenue, just under two thousand active subscribers, tracked since December.

Worth a word on why I keep leaning on TrustMRR for these smaller ones rather than a founder's own claim on social media. TrustMRR figures are pulled directly from Stripe or RevenueCat via API, which means they're independently verified rather than self-reported — a meaningfully stronger form of evidence than a screenshot someone chose to post. It's not infallible, and it's still worth treating any single figure as a snapshot rather than a guarantee the number holds next month, but it's a genuinely useful filter for cutting through the pile of "I made six figures" posts that don't survive five minutes of scrutiny.

Part three. Apply it.

PlayFundWin first, and there's a real deadline attached to this one. DCMS's call for evidence on National Lottery good causes funding closes on the twenty-third of September — that's three weeks away as of today, which makes this the last episode where "closing soon" is technically true rather than a bit late. I'd go and read the submission questions themselves rather than a summary of them, because a proper response from a working prize-draw operator carries more weight in a consultation like this than the polished submissions national charities tend to send in — grassroots operators are consistently underrepresented in exactly this kind of process, and this is a rare, time-limited chance to change that for your corner of it. If nothing else survives from today's episode, let it be that one: read the questions yourself, this week, not the week the deadline arrives. Separately, Astra's arrival is worth an internal moment too: PlayFundWin moves real money through prize draws, so this is a sensible week to double-check two-factor authentication is switched on everywhere it touches payments, that admin access is tight, and that nobody's still reusing a password from three jobs ago.

LeaguePages next. The Fable and Mythos price cuts are good, boring, useful news if you've built anything AI-powered into the platform already — whatever that feature costs to run today, it should cost less from this week, with no work required on your end beyond checking the invoice. If you're using Lovable anywhere in your own tooling, the new Custom API Connectors are worth a look next time you need to hook up a fixtures feed or a payments provider without waiting on developer time. And the same Astra story applies here too: if any Cursor-built or OpenAI-dependent tooling sits inside LeaguePages, this is as good a week as any to check what it touches and how it's secured, particularly with that twelfth of November OpenAI cutoff still sitting on the calendar with no resolution in sight. And if today's build idea landed at all, the match-report mechanic is the one I'd prototype first — it's a smaller lift than a full raffle-copy generator, and it's the kind of feature that makes a club's page look alive every single week without anyone on the committee having to sit down and write anything.

EV-Partnerships is, once again, mostly a story about what hasn't happened. The sports-venue destination-charging gap is now open for a tenth consecutive episode — I checked all eight named operators directly again this week, IONITY, RAW Charging, Allego, Believ, InstaVolt, Osprey, GRIDSERVE and Pod Point, and not one of them has announced anything aimed specifically at a sports or leisure venue, as opposed to a motorway, a retail park, or a residential street. Allego's hundred-million-euro UK rollout remains firmly road-corridor and retail-prioritised. Worth folding in today's Astra story here too: public charging infrastructure sits on the grid, takes card payments, and increasingly reports back to a network operator's app — exactly the kind of connected, payment-touching system that a more capable attacker would find interesting, so this is as good a prompt as any to ask any charging partner you work with what their own security posture actually looks like, rather than assuming it's someone else's problem. Ten episodes of nobody moving isn't nothing — it's a fairly strong signal that the gap is real rather than something everyone else has already spotted and quietly ruled out. At some point that streak either ends with a competitor announcement, or it ends with EV-Partnerships making the first move — and the longer it runs, the more that second outcome looks like the interesting one.

Part four. One action today.

Here's the thirty-minute version of today's episode. Open the DCMS call for evidence page yourself, read the actual submission questions rather than a news summary of them, and draft rough answers to two or three of them in a document, thinking specifically about how a working prize-draw platform serving grassroots clubs would answer differently than a national charity's policy team. If you want to make the thirty minutes stretch further, paste the questions straight into Claude alongside a couple of sentences on how PlayFundWin operates day to day, and ask for a first-pass draft answer to each one — you'll still need to read it critically and fix anything that doesn't sound like you, but it turns a blank page into an editing job, which is a much easier place to start from. You don't need a finished submission today — you need a skeleton you can hand off or finish properly before the twenty-third of September actually arrives, rather than discovering with three days left that you're starting cold.
