Item one. Anthropic announced something called Enterprise Frontier Safeguards on the first of September. The pitch: enterprise customers can now store their Claude interaction logs in their own cloud, encrypted with their own keys and governed by their own access policies, instead of on Anthropic's servers. Any automated misuse alerts route straight to the customer's own security team rather than to an Anthropic reviewer sitting somewhere else entirely. Anthropic says it built this with input from more than a hundred enterprises, including banks, and the phased rollout starts this autumn at no extra charge. I read Anthropic's own announcement on this one, not a summary of it.

Why you care: if you've ever hesitated before uploading a client contract, a membership database, or a set of payment records into Claude, this is Anthropic directly answering that hesitation, at least for its biggest customers. It won't touch you tomorrow if you're a two-person operation working out of a spare room. But it's a preview of what "trustworthy enough for regulated data" is going to mean by the time it does reach you, and smaller AI vendors are going to feel pressure to match it whether they like it or not.

Item two, and this one is a close cousin of item one. Cursor, the AI coding tool, shipped self-hosted machines on the second of September. Its cloud coding agents can now run their tool calls entirely on your own infrastructure — either a personal pool of machines or a shared team pool — instead of Cursor's cloud, meaning your codebase, your secrets, and your build output never leave your own network. They've also added desktop and computer-use automation for Mac and Linux workers in the same release, so the agent can now drive applications on your own machine directly. Straight from Cursor's own changelog, dated the second.

Why you care: if you're a solo developer or a small agency working under an NDA, or handling anything with a scrap of regulated data in it, you can now let an AI agent write and run code on your own machines without that code ever touching a third party's servers. That's a genuinely useful lever for the kind of client work where "where does our code actually live" is the first question in the contract, not just a compliance tick-box you fill in once and forget. Picture a freelance developer building a membership portal for a charity: before this release, the honest answer to "does our data pass through your AI tool's servers" was yes, briefly, in transit. Now it can be a flat no, and that's the kind of detail that closes a contract with a nervous trustee board.

Item three. Lovable, the no-code app builder, now lets you buy and connect a custom domain from inside the chat window itself, shipped the first of September. Ask for a domain, it shows you what's available with pricing, and it handles the purchase or connects one you already own, with a confirmation screen before anything gets charged. Confirmed against Lovable's own changelog.

Why you care: that's one more piece of "spin up a real business this weekend" friction gone. Prompt to a live site on your own domain, no context switch to a registrar, no fumbling with DNS records at eleven at night trying to remember which nameserver goes where. Small feature, but it's the kind of small feature that quietly makes the gap between idea and live product a little shorter every month — and every one of these tiny frictions that disappears is one more excuse gone for the version of you that keeps meaning to start something.

Item four. Perplexity shipped something called Hybrid Compute for its Computer agent on Mac, also on the first of September. Instead of sending everything to the cloud, the agent now splits work between the cloud and models running locally on Apple silicon, so sensitive files and information can stay on the device rather than travelling anywhere. They paired the release with a piece of research called PII-TRACE, aimed at detecting personal information before it ever leaves your machine in the first place. Read directly off Perplexity's own blog post, dated the same day.

Why you care: same theme as items one and two, different vendor entirely. Three separate companies, three days, all converging on "keep the sensitive stuff local." When an entire industry moves the same direction at the same time without coordinating, that's not marketing copy dressed up as a trend — that's customers telling vendors, loudly and consistently, what they actually need before they'll trust an agent with real work.

Item five, and this is the smallest of the bunch, but worth a mention purely as a trend marker. Google's Android update on the first of September lets you tell Gemini to remember things inside a feature called Find Hub — say "remember I put my passport in the bedroom drawer," optionally with a photo attached, and Gemini retrieves it later just by asking out loud. Small, consumer-facing, rolling out gradually on newer Android versions. But it's another data point that Gemini is turning into the default memory layer sitting underneath ordinary phone apps, not just a chatbot you open on purpose when you remember it exists.

Step back and look at those three together — Anthropic, Cursor, Perplexity, forty-eight hours, one theme. None of these three companies compete directly with each other in the way OpenAI and Google do. They're not copying a rival's feature to avoid losing a headline. What they're actually doing is responding to the same customer conversation happening in parallel across three different sales pipelines: "I want the capability, but I'm not comfortable with where my data ends up." A year ago the answer to that objection was a reassuring paragraph in a privacy policy nobody read. Now it's an architecture change, shipped, with a changelog entry and a date on it. For a small operator, the lesson isn't "go build your own local infrastructure this afternoon" — most of you don't need to. It's that data residency and control are becoming a genuine selling point rather than fine print, and it's worth watching which of your own suppliers and tools start offering the same thing, because it'll tell you who's paying attention to where this is heading.

Today's deep dive is Zigpoll, and the founder is Jason Zigelbaum. He runs it entirely solo — no co-founder, no outside funding, no sales team chasing leads on his behalf. In an Indie Hackers interview published on the second of July this year, he said the business closed June at around a hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue, which works out to roughly a million and a half dollars a year. That's not a launch-week spike either. An earlier public data point from GetLatka put Zigpoll at about thirty thousand four hundred dollars in annual revenue with around a thousand customers, back in twenty twenty-three. So this is multi-year, compounding growth, not one lucky month someone screenshotted.

What does Zigpoll actually do? It's a survey tool, but not the kind you're picturing. Forget the testimonial widget that begs happy customers for a five-star quote to slap on a landing page. Zigpoll fires a single, sharply timed question at the exact moment it matters most — right as someone's about to complete a purchase, right after they've just bought, or right as they click cancel on a subscription. "What almost stopped you from buying today?" "What made you choose us over the alternative you were also looking at?" "Why are you leaving?" One question, asked at the moment of truth, not a ten-field form nobody ever finishes.

Here's why that's a genuinely different product from anything we've covered on this show before. A testimonial tool wants you to look good in public. A review-reply tool wants to manage your reputation after the fact, once the damage or the praise is already out there. Zigpoll wants to catch the truth while it's still warm — the actual reason someone hesitated, in their own words, seconds after they felt it, before they've had time to forget it or soften it for politeness. For an e-commerce store or a SaaS business trying to fix a leaky checkout funnel, that's worth more than a hundred generic star ratings, because it tells you exactly where people are falling off and why, rather than leaving you to guess from a bounce-rate graph.

Could you build this? Actually, yes, and fairly quickly too. The core of it is a small JavaScript snippet you drop onto a checkout page, a thank-you page, or a cancellation flow, a couple of trigger conditions for when the question should appear, a lightweight backend to store responses — Supabase or plain Postgres does the job for a first version, no machine learning required at all — and a dashboard where the business owner reads what came back and can filter or search it. Add Stripe for billing on top and you've got a sellable product with a real, if narrow, feature set. This is the kind of build Claude can take from a blank folder to a working prototype in a matter of days, not months of planning meetings. Hosting and a Stripe account get you started for well under five hundred pounds, and most of that is optional polish rather than anything you strictly need on day one.

Now the honest, conservative money maths, because five and six-figure founder stories always sound tidier than the years that actually built them. Say you charge twenty-nine pounds a month for a starter tier and forty-nine pounds for a growth tier aimed at busier stores with more traffic to test against. Ten paying customers on the lower tier is two hundred and ninety pounds a month — not life-changing on its own, but real money, and it covers your hosting costs with room to spare. Fifty customers blended across both tiers lands somewhere around fifteen hundred to two thousand pounds a month, which genuinely starts to look like a proper side income rather than a hobby. Zigelbaum's own hundred-and-twenty-five-thousand-dollar month took years of compounding, not a single good weekend of shipping, and building a competing tool doesn't erase that reality. It just gives you a fast, cheap way to find out whether the idea has legs for your own niche before you spend real months of your life chasing it on faith alone.

The risks are worth naming plainly rather than glossing over. This is a crowded-adjacent space — Hotjar, Typeform, and a dozen smaller survey tools already exist, even if none of them lean quite as hard into the exact moment-of-truth positioning Zigpoll built its whole pitch around. You'll need real customers willing to install a snippet and trust you with access to their checkout page, which is a considerably higher-friction sale than a browser extension someone installs on a whim. And a single-question survey lives or dies on asking the right question at the right moment — that takes real iteration and paying attention to what people actually say, not a one-and-done build you ship and walk away from.

Who actually buys something like this? Not enterprise — the big platforms already bundle a version of this into their analytics suites, however clumsily. The real customer is the same one who buys most of the tools we cover on this show: a small e-commerce store doing somewhere between fifty thousand and a few hundred thousand pounds a year in revenue, or a small SaaS business with a churn problem it can't quite explain from the numbers alone. Those owners already know something's wrong with their funnel; they just don't have the budget for a proper conversion-rate-optimisation consultant, and a generic survey tool feels like overkill for one specific question. That's the wedge — narrow enough to build fast, specific enough that a general-purpose competitor won't bother chasing you into it, and cheap enough that the sales conversation is "try it free on one page for a fortnight" rather than a lengthy procurement process.
