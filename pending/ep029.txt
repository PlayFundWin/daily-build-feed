Anthropic just gave away the blueprint for an AI shop assistant, for free, on GitHub, to anyone who wants it. Google made its cheapest coding model sharper without raising the price by a single cent. And a two-person studio built a bot that got its users banned from the one platform it was designed to work on, roughly as fast as it made them money.

This is The Daily Build, episode twenty-nine, for Friday the fourth of September, twenty twenty-six. Fifteen minutes on what shipped in AI over the last two days, one business idea backed by real published numbers rather than a founder's back-of-envelope guess, and what any of it means for PlayFundWin, LeaguePages, and EV-Partnerships. One line before we start, because it needs saying once and then we can get on with it: this is research and ideas, not financial advice.

Two research houses and one big platform all shipped something this week. Only one of them is actually useful to you today, and it isn't the one with the biggest headline. Let's get into why.

First, what shipped.

Anthropic released something called Commerce Agents on the second of September, an open-source blueprint that lets a development team stand up an AI shopping assistant in days instead of months. There are two halves to it. A shopping agent that searches a store's catalogue, builds a basket, compares products in conversation, and handles customer questions, with built-in guardrails against manipulation. And a merchant agent that watches sales data, flags stock problems, suggests dynamic pricing and promotions, and drafts marketing copy, with a human still approving anything that actually touches money. It plugs into Claude's own API, or into Bedrock, Microsoft Foundry, or Google's Vertex AI, and ships with a Claude Code plugin so an engineer can wire it up directly inside their editor instead of starting from a blank page. Anthropic's launch partners include Accenture, Mastercard, and Visa, and the early numbers being cited are baskets up to thirty-five per cent larger and purchase completion up sixty per cent higher, though those are the vendor's own launch-partner figures and worth treating as a marketing claim rather than an independently measured result. Why you care: this isn't a chatbot bolted onto the corner of a website as an afterthought. It's a full working pattern for letting a customer shop by conversation and letting a manager run the shop by conversation too, and because the whole thing is open source, a competent developer doesn't need Anthropic's permission, a partnership, or a large budget to go and try it this weekend. If PlayFundWin ever moves toward letting a supporter buy raffle tickets or top up an entry by chatting instead of clicking through a form, this is the reference pattern that would already exist to build from.

Google shipped Gemini three point eight Flash on the same day, the second of September, and are calling it their best reasoning and coding model yet, at the same speed and the same price as the version it replaces. That price is being held at seventy-five cents per million tokens in and three dollars seventy-five per million tokens out, locked through the end of this year. Alongside it, Google launched a specialised sibling called Flash Cyber that hunts for software vulnerabilities entirely on its own and can generate a validated fix once it finds one, but that model is restricted to a new vetted-partner scheme aimed squarely at governments and critical infrastructure operators, so it won't be reaching a small business any time soon. Why you care: the standard Flash model is the one that actually matters here. If you or a developer you work with builds anything on the Gemini API, you now get a meaningfully better model at exactly the cost you were already paying, held steady through the rest of the year, which is one less thing to budget around.

xAI opened up something called Grok Bot to enterprise customers on the third of September. These are described as always-on AI teammates that run inside their own isolated cloud environment, keep working when nobody's watching them, and can message each other and share context on a shared task without a human relaying information between them. xAI is offering two weeks completely free to existing Grok or Cursor Enterprise customers, including inviting people at those organisations who don't already have a seat. The use cases xAI is showing off are things like monitoring a sales pipeline and drafting outreach, sourcing recruiting candidates, chasing up webinar leads automatically, and watching vendor spending for anything that looks off. Why you care: if any one of those sounds like a job currently being done, badly, by a spreadsheet and a Friday-afternoon scramble, a free two-week trial is a cheap way to find out whether an always-on agent actually earns its keep before anyone has to pay for a seat. The catch is the same catch every "always-on" agent has right now: someone still has to set the guardrails, decide what it's allowed to send without asking first, and check its work often enough in that first fortnight to actually trust it by week three.

OpenAI put out the full safety documentation behind GPT-six Astra on the third of September, two days after first announcing it as their earliest model to cross what they call the critical threshold for cyber capability. The system card covers jailbreak resistance testing, alignment evaluations, and a new layer of misalignment monitoring rolled out to real users, and it includes an unusually candid admission that the model's internal reasoning has actually become harder to monitor from the outside than in earlier versions, not easier. This one is more a trust and transparency story than a new tool you'd reach for on a Tuesday. Why you care: it's a reminder that the most capable models are also the ones getting the tightest access controls wrapped around them, so if any part of your business plan leans on frontier-model access down the line, read the access terms before you build a roadmap around an assumption. For PlayFundWin specifically, a critical-capability model existing anywhere in the world, even one you'll never touch, is as good a prompt as any to spend ten minutes this week checking that admin accounts and payment access have proper two-factor authentication switched on, given the platform handles real money moving through real bank accounts.

And OpenAI committed a billion dollars, also on the third of September, to subsidised access to its cyber-defence tooling for operators who couldn't otherwise afford it, water utilities, electricity networks, local government bodies, under a programme branded Daybreak for Frontline Defenders. It starts with a pilot alongside an American body called the Multi-State Information Sharing and Analysis Centre, backed by a network of more than thirty-five partner products that embed the underlying models. Why you care: it isn't aimed at a small UK business directly, and I wouldn't build a plan around it, but if any of your suppliers or partners sit inside critical infrastructure, this is the kind of programme that quietly raises the floor on how well-defended the whole chain around you actually is.

One smaller item worth a single line. Lovable, the AI app-building tool, added a section to its settings on the second of September where you can scan a QR code to install its companion app on your phone. Not a headline in itself, but if you or anyone at LeaguePages is prototyping something in Lovable, you can now check on a build from your pocket rather than being stuck at a laptop.

I also went and checked the usual quiet corners directly rather than assuming nothing happened there: Cursor, ElevenLabs, Replit, Perplexity, and Microsoft Copilot. All four had nothing dated in the last two days, and Bolt's own changelog blocked the page from being read at all, so I'm leaving that one out rather than guessing at what it might say. A quiet forty-eight hours from five different vendors at once is unusual enough on its own to be worth a mention, even without a headline attached to it.

Now, the build.

There's a founder in North Macedonia named Ivan Nedelkovski who used to run a development agency called MVP Masters. He grew it to fifteen full-time staff and seven-figure revenue, then sold his stake and, with one other person, built something called Lancer. It's an AI agent that connects directly to a freelancer's Upwork account, reads every incoming job listing, filters those listings against that freelancer's actual niche and skill set, writes a fully customised proposal in their own voice for each one that fits, and sends it without the freelancer lifting a finger. Once replies start coming back, it helps manage and triage that inbox too.

According to a post the founder wrote himself on Indie Hackers, published in July this year, Lancer hit ten thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue within sixty days of launching back in July last year, and by the time of that write-up it had grown to twenty thousand dollars a month, with the company information box on the same post noting revenue had pushed past twenty-five thousand. I read that post directly, in full, rather than going off a summary of it. It's self-reported by the founder on a public forum, not independently audited by anyone, so take the precise figure as a claim rather than a settled fact. But it's consistent across two separate check-ins months apart rather than one lucky screenshot, which counts for something even if it isn't proof.

Pricing is two tiers. A pay-per-lead plan at around a hundred and forty-nine dollars a month, or ninety-nine dollars a month if you pay quarterly, and an unlimited plan at roughly four hundred and ninety-nine dollars a month, or three hundred and thirty-three a month paid quarterly. There's also a smaller side income stream where Lancer refers clients to independent freelance coaches and takes a cut of what those coaches go on to earn.

Now the honest problem with the idea exactly as it's built. Upwork's own terms of service explicitly ban the automated submission of proposals on a freelancer's behalf. A tool that logs in as you and fires off applications automatically is a tool that can get your account suspended, and for a working freelancer, their Upwork history is very often their entire client pipeline. Losing it isn't a minor inconvenience, it's losing the business. So I wouldn't copy Lancer exactly, and I'd be cautious recommending anyone else does either. I'd build the safer half of the same idea instead: an AI proposal co-pilot that reads a job listing, drafts a proposal that's properly tailored and actually sounds like the person it's written for, and then hands it straight back for a human to read and hit send themselves. Same value, the same voice-matching trick that's doing all the real work, none of the account-ban risk, because a human stays the one who submits.

That's also, by the way, exactly the sort of build where Steve's three existing businesses have a head start that Ivan Nedelkovski never had. He had to find his first users cold, one Upwork freelancer at a time, with no existing audience to draw on. PlayFundWin already has relationships with hundreds of clubs and charities who all face the identical sponsor-outreach problem every season. LeaguePages already sits inside the inbox of grassroots clubs chasing kit sponsors. EV-Partnerships already has a shortlist of venue managers worth pitching. None of that guarantees a single sale, but it means the distribution problem, usually the hardest part of any of these ideas, is mostly already solved before a line of code gets written. That's the edge worth weighing against the risks above.

That's realistically a few days of work for a competent AI coding assistant to help build, not a few months. You'd need a simple way to bring in job listings, which without an official Upwork API for this specific purpose probably means the freelancer pastes in a link or a screenshot rather than anything scraping the site behind the scenes. You'd need a prompt pipeline trained on a handful of that person's past proposals and finished work so the tone actually matches how they write, not a generic AI voice. A simple dashboard to review drafts before they go anywhere. And Stripe for billing once anyone's actually paying you. Domain registration runs around ten pounds a year. Hosting on something like Vercel or Railway is free at small scale and maybe ten pounds a month once you've got real, paying users on it. API credits while you're testing and building will run somewhere around twenty to forty pounds a month. All in, a working first version, live and usable, comes in well under the five hundred pounds we set as the ceiling for this segment.

The build order matters more than the tool list. Start with the prompt pipeline and test it on your own writing before anything else exists, because if the drafts don't sound right, nothing else you build matters. Only once that's properly working would I bother wiring up a dashboard, and only once someone other than you wants to pay would I bother wiring up Stripe. Building the payment page before the product works is the classic way these projects burn a weekend on the wrong problem.

The money maths, and I'll keep this deliberately conservative rather than exciting. If you charged forty pounds a month and picked up thirty freelancers across your first few months, that's twelve hundred pounds a month recurring, against running costs still sitting in the tens of pounds. Get to a hundred paying customers, which would take real, sustained marketing effort, and you're looking at four thousand pounds a month before you've done anything clever with tiered pricing or an annual discount to improve cash flow. None of that is Lancer's own twenty-five thousand dollars a month. It's a believable first year for a solo builder, not a fantasy projection dressed up to sound like one.

The risks stretch beyond the terms-of-service problem too. You're building an entire product on top of a platform you don't control, so if Upwork changes its page layout, your paste-in workflow breaks overnight, and if Upwork's own matching algorithm shifts, the quality of the leads your users are seeing shifts right along with it, through no fault of your own code. And a self-reported revenue number from one founder on one public post is exactly that, a single data point from an interested party, not independent proof of a repeatable market.

One more thing worth being honest about before this idea sounds too easy: the freelancer market is crowded with people who've been burned by tools promising to write their proposals for them, so the pitch that will actually land isn't "AI writes your proposals," it's "AI writes a first draft that sounds like you, in under a minute, and you decide whether it's good enough to send." That's a smaller, less exciting promise than Lancer makes, and it's also the honest one, which tends to be the version that survives contact with sceptical customers.

Here's the part that's actually interesting for Steve specifically, more than the platform Lancer happens to be built for. Strip Upwork out of the picture entirely, and what Lancer is really doing underneath is turning a small pile of someone's past writing into personalised, voice-matched outreach at scale. That mechanic doesn't care in the slightest whether the "job listing" going in is a freelance gig on a marketplace or a local business you're trying to persuade to sponsor a prize draw.

Instantly AI, a cold-email outbound platform built by Nils Schneider and Raul Kaevand, reportedly reached two hundred thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue within six months of launch and twenty million dollars in annual revenue by December twenty twenty-four, according to a detailed third-party write-up I read directly. I haven't been able to cross-check that specific figure against Instantly's own published numbers, so treat it as secondary-sourced, not founder-confirmed. Rytr, an AI writing and copy assistant from founder Abhi Godara, was reported in a Starter Story interview at around eighty-three thousand dollars a month with over three hundred thousand users signed up, though that same article's own headline number oddly undersells the figures stated in its body text, so I'm quoting what the interview itself says instead of repeating the misleading headline. Both of those are older, more established products than Lancer, and I'm including them mainly as evidence that this style of AI-writing business tends to compound rather than plateau once it finds its footing. And Spellbook, an AI contract-review tool built for law firms and led by chief executive Scott Stevenson, says it tripled its revenue across twenty twenty-five and is now targeting a hundred million dollars in annual revenue this year, following a fifty-million-dollar funding round last October. That hundred-million target is the company's own forward-looking claim, not an audited trailing figure, so hold it a little more loosely than the others. It's also the one runner-up that's raised real venture money rather than staying bootstrapped, which is worth keeping in mind before comparing its trajectory too directly to anything a solo builder could realistically pull off on a five-hundred-pound budget.

The Lancer mechanic, a matched voice and a matched pitch, still sent by an actual human being, maps almost exactly onto the unglamorous, repetitive work of getting local businesses to back a club's prize draw. A club treasurer with zero marketing background could paste in a target business's website and get back a first-draft sponsorship ask that actually sounds like them rather than like a template downloaded from somewhere. Separately, the DCMS call for evidence on National Lottery good causes closes at midday on the twenty-third of September, which is nineteen days from today. That deadline hasn't moved this week and nothing new has been published against it, so the useful thing isn't another reminder that it exists on the calendar, it's actually opening the real submission questions this week and getting a rough draft down on paper, even a bad one, so the final version doesn't get written in a rush in the last forty-eight hours before it's due.

For LeaguePages, it was a properly quiet week in grassroots sports tech specifically, nothing dated in the last seven days worth reporting on air, so I'm not going to manufacture a story where there plainly isn't one to tell. One action for today, and it should take you comfortably under thirty minutes. Pick one of the three businesses. Gather three or four pieces of writing you've actually sent in the past, an email, a sponsor pitch, a message to a club chair, whatever's closest to hand right now, and paste them into Claude along with one specific, concrete ask, for example a first-draft sponsorship email aimed at a fictional local gym. Read what comes back and mark it up honestly: too formal, too long, doesn't sound like you at all, whatever's actually wrong with it. That small exercise is the entire test of whether the voice-matching trick behind today's build idea is worth building into something real, and it costs you nothing but twenty minutes and a handful of examples of your own writing. If the draft is close, that's your signal the idea's worth an afternoon of real building. If it's miles off, better to find that out for free today than after a week spent building the wrong thing.
