Now to the part of the show where I go looking for a small business a determined person could actually build, and today I found something doing a properly different job from anything I've picked on this show before.

It's called Jobric, built by a man named Erik Chavez, who by day is a senior solutions architect at Microsoft and builds this on the side, in the hours most people spend watching television. Here's what makes it interesting rather than just another entry in an already crowded field: nearly every AI tool built for the recruitment space is built to serve whoever's paying for it, and that's almost always the employer or the recruiter — sourcing candidates for them, screening resumes for them, ranking applicants for them. Jobric flips that whole relationship on its head. It's built for the person actually looking for the job, the one nobody in this market usually builds software for.

The pitch, in Erik's own words from an Indie Hackers interview: every other tool in this space works for whoever's paying, and that's the recruiter, not you. Jobric takes your CV, works out what your actual skills and experience really are, then matches you against live job postings and tells you exactly where you fit and where you don't — instead of you scrolling a job board for an hour hoping the right keyword happens to land in the right place.

The evidence, and I'll be precise about what kind of evidence this is: Erik reports three thousand three hundred dollars in monthly recurring revenue within two months of launching on the first of May. That figure is self-reported by the founder himself — there's no independent verification service standing behind it the way there was behind a previous episode's pick, so take the number as his own honest account rather than an audited figure from a third party. He runs three pricing tiers: a free level to get people through the door and using the product, a Candidate tier priced at twenty-nine dollars a month, and a Contender tier at forty-nine dollars a month for people who want the full matching engine along with the extras that come with it.

Here's why this one's actually buildable, and buildable fast, in a way that wouldn't have been true even three years ago. The hard part of a tool like this used to be building a resume parser and a matching algorithm entirely from scratch — expensive, slow, and it needed an actual data science team to do properly. Now Claude does the parsing and the scoring inside a well-constructed prompt: feed it a CV, feed it a job description, ask it to score the fit between them and explain precisely where the gap sits. Ask it to be specific rather than vague, and it will tell a candidate exactly which three skills are missing rather than a generic "moderate match" score nobody can act on. The part you actually still have to build yourself is the plumbing around that intelligence — pulling job postings from a jobs data API or an aggregator's feed rather than writing and maintaining your own web crawler, a simple front end where someone uploads a CV and sees their ranked matches, and Stripe wired up cleanly for the subscription billing. None of that requires a team, or even much time. A capable solo builder could plausibly have a working version live inside a week, and the running costs — API calls to Claude, a jobs-data API subscription, basic hosting for a lightweight web app — sit comfortably under five hundred pounds to get to a sellable first version that real people can sign up for.

The honest maths, worked through conservatively rather than optimistically: at those price points, covering a modest hosting and API bill takes a surprisingly small number of paying subscribers — call it a few dozen at the lower tier before you're covering your own running costs, not the thousands you might assume a subscription business needs before it breaks even. Getting from a working product to your first fifty paying users is the actual hard part of this whole exercise, same as it always is with ideas like this one — the building itself has become the easy half thanks to tools like Claude, but the customer acquisition is the part no AI tool solves for you, and it's where most attempts like this one actually stall.

Walk through what building the first version would actually look like, step by step, because that's more useful to you than a vague description. Day one: sign up for a jobs-data API — there are several aimed at exactly this, indexing postings from major boards so you're not scraping anything yourself. Day two and three: build the upload flow and wire Claude into it with a carefully written prompt that takes a CV and a job posting and returns a structured fit score along with a plain-English explanation of the gap, tested against a stack of real CVs and real postings until the explanations actually sound useful rather than generic. Day four: a basic front end — upload your CV, see your top matches, click through to apply. Day five: Stripe, the three pricing tiers, and a landing page that says plainly who this is for and why it's different from a normal job board. None of those five days require specialist skills beyond ordinary web development and a willingness to iterate on the prompt until the output is genuinely good rather than merely functional. That's the whole shape of why this kind of business has become buildable by one determined person in a way it simply wasn't a few years back.

The risks, stated plainly rather than glossed over: job-matching lives or dies entirely on trust, and if your matching engine sends people toward jobs wildly outside their actual fit, they'll churn fast and say so publicly on the same forums where you found this idea in the first place. You're also handling people's CVs and career histories, which in the UK means treating that data properly under data protection law from day one rather than as an afterthought you'll get to once you've got paying customers. And it's a crowded space in the sense that recruitment technology attracts a huge number of AI wrapper products right now, all making similar claims — the differentiation has to be real and demonstrable, not just marketing copy about being "built for candidates" while doing the same thing as everyone else underneath.
