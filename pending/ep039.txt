First up: the three biggest model labs on earth just confirmed they're talking to each other about safety. OpenAI's global policy chief told reporters this week that OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind have spent several weeks in direct conversation about coordinating safety standards. He was careful to say this isn't a merger and doesn't need any antitrust waiver — it's policy coordination, not a cartel. The framing lines up neatly with Dario Amodei's recent essay arguing the industry needs to slow down and show its working, and reporting suggests both Sam Altman and, of all people, Elon Musk are supportive of the idea. Neither Anthropic nor Google DeepMind would comment when asked directly, which tells you something on its own — a company happy with a story usually says so. I read this off Bloomberg's reporting, syndicated through Claims Journal, so treat the OpenAI side as confirmed and the other two labs' silence as exactly that: silence, not agreement. Why you should care: if the three companies whose APIs half of us build on start moving their safety policies in lockstep, expect synchronised changes to usage limits and content rules across Claude, GPT and Gemini at the same time, rather than staggered the way they usually roll out. Worth watching your own integrations for policy emails landing in the same week from more than one of them — that would be the tell that this coordination has moved from talking to doing. It's also worth remembering that "we're talking about safety" costs a policy chief nothing to say in public, while an actual joint standard costs real engineering time to build and enforce — the gap between those two things is exactly where this story could quietly go nowhere for months.

Third, and this one's a straight product release rather than a rumour: Google shipped two new real-time voice models on Monday, Gemini three point eight Live and a bigger sibling called three point eight Live Extended Thinking. The standard version is a cheaper, faster conversational and visual-understanding model. The Extended Thinking version is aimed at enterprise use and actually talks while it works — it'll say something like "let me check that" mid-conversation rather than going silent, while it runs multi-step reasoning or calls a tool in the background without breaking the flow of speech. Both switch languages mid-conversation across ninety-seven languages automatically, both watermark their audio output using Google's SynthID system, and Google is claiming the Extended Thinking model tops an independent speech-quality leaderboard outright. I read this straight off Google's own DeepMind blog post, so that's as confirmed as this show gets. Why you care: if you've defaulted to ElevenLabs or OpenAI's realtime voice API for any kind of voice agent — and given what this very show runs on, I'm not throwing stones from a glass house — there's now a notably cheaper, seriously capable option straight from Google worth a side-by-side test before you renew anything on autopilot.

That's the shipping news. Now, the build.

Today's deep dive is an app called nutrIA, and it's a genuinely useful case study in speed beating polish. Two founders, Nicolás Pirozzi and Pedro Cisternas — who goes by Piru — built it over a single long weekend in April last year. You photograph your meal, the app identifies what's on the plate, estimates calories and macros, scores how healthy the meal is, and nudges you with habit feedback over time. It runs on React Native and Expo with a Supabase backend, and it takes subscription payments through RevenueCat, which is also where most of this evidence comes from.

Here's the evidence trail, and it's stronger than most of what's landed on this show. TrustMRR — which pulls this data live through a direct RevenueCat connection rather than a founder simply typing a number into a form — shows revenue over the last thirty days of just over sixteen thousand dollars, monthly recurring revenue around thirteen thousand, and about four and a half thousand active subscriptions, tracked continuously since the end of January this year, with the business itself founded in April of last year. That's the platform-verified figure, checked this week. But it doesn't stand alone on one source. A Chilean financial newspaper, Diario Financiero, ran a feature on the two founders back in March, and a separate outlet called El Ecosistema Startup covered the same story the same week, independently. Both name the same two founders and confirm the same origin story: the build started on a Thursday evening over the Easter weekend and was live in the App Store by Sunday lunchtime — first submission, first approval, no resubmission cycle, no back-and-forth with Apple's review team. By the time those March pieces ran, the app had already passed sixty thousand downloads and three thousand paying subscribers, built almost entirely through the founders posting the build process on Instagram and TikTok as they went, day by day, in public. Two independent named outlets, sitting alongside a platform-verified revenue figure — that's about as solid as this show's evidence trail ever gets, and it's worth saying so plainly rather than hedging a number this well-sourced.

If you wanted to build something like this yourself, the actual construction isn't the hard part, and it never really is with this category of app. React Native and Expo give you the app shell across both iOS and Android from one codebase, Supabase handles your backend and user accounts, RevenueCat handles subscriptions and receipt validation so you're not building payment plumbing from scratch, and a vision-capable AI model does the actual food recognition — Claude's own vision capability would do that job perfectly well without needing a specialist food-recognition API. A solo, Claude-assisted UK builder is realistically looking at three to seven days to get an app-store-submittable version working end to end: a camera screen, a results screen showing the estimate, a simple habit-tracking dashboard, and a paywall gating the habit features behind a subscription. Add however long Apple and Google's own review process takes on top, which is usually a matter of days rather than weeks if you haven't done anything unusual with permissions or data handling.

The money maths, kept deliberately conservative the way this show always tries to. An Apple Developer account runs about eighty pounds a year. A Google Play developer account is a one-off charge of around twenty pounds. Supabase and RevenueCat are both free at this kind of early scale — RevenueCat's free tier covers you comfortably until you're doing real revenue, at which point it takes a small cut you'll barely notice. A vision API only costs you per photo analysed, which at low volume works out to a few pence a day even with heavy personal testing. Put it together and you could realistically have a working version live for under two hundred pounds all in, well inside the five-hundred-pound ceiling this show sets for itself. You are extremely unlikely to hit nutrIA's own numbers in your first month, or even your first few months — they had a genuine social-media following doing a lot of the heavy lifting that a from-scratch UK builder wouldn't start with. But even a tenth of their trajectory, call it sixteen hundred dollars a month rather than sixteen thousand, would comfortably cover its own running costs within the first few weeks and leave you with a working case study of your own.

The risks are real, and worth saying plainly rather than glossing over. This is a crowded category — MyFitnessPal, Noom, Fastic and a run of AI-native calorie apps that have already been through this show in earlier episodes, including one that got acquired outright by MyFitnessPal back in March. NutrIA's growth leaned hard on the founders' own social following and their willingness to post the entire build process publicly, which is a marketing skill, not a coding one, and it's the part that's hardest to replicate without doing the same public-build work yourself. Food-photo recognition is never going to be perfect — misidentified meals mean bad calorie estimates, and bad estimates mean one-star reviews from people who trusted the number. And that seven-hundred-thousand-dollar figure you might see attached to the business if you go looking is an asking price on a resale listing, not a valuation anyone has actually paid — don't repeat that number as if it were confirmed revenue, because it isn't.
