Anthropic folded its Cowork mode into ordinary Claude chat yesterday, which means the tool researching and writing this very episode changed shape between recording sessions. There's a joke in there about eating your own homework. I haven't found the good version of it yet, so let's get on with the news.

Anthropic's own blog confirmed it directly on Wednesday the sixteenth of September. Claude drops from three separate modes down to two, chat and code. Cowork's background task-running, plus three new pieces called Claude Docs, Claude Slides and Claude Design, now live inside any normal conversation instead of a separate workspace you had to remember to open. Slides handles deck and PDF export on its own, and Design carries over whatever visual-generation work used to need its own standalone tool. The default stays approval-gated, so nothing runs off and acts without asking first, but you can opt into a more continuous, autonomous mode if you'd rather it just get on with things. It's rolling out to paying Pro and Max users first, over the coming weeks, then to Team and Free accounts, with Enterprise customers getting more than thirty days' notice before anything changes under them. Practically, that means drafting a first pass of a grant application or a sponsor deck no longer means bouncing between a chat window and a separate documents tool. It happens in one thread, the model can go off and draft something, come back, and keep talking about it without you re-explaining the brief. Small thing, but small friction removed usually beats a flashy new capability nobody actually opens. Why you care if you're not knee-deep in AI tooling day to day: one fewer app to context-switch between, which is going to matter directly when we get to LeaguePages in a minute.

Today's build idea has been making the same number for about two years running, which in this business is either remarkable or suspicious, and I think it's genuinely the former.

HeadshotPro takes a handful of ordinary selfies and turns them into the kind of professional corporate headshot you'd otherwise pay a studio for, sold as a one-off photo pack rather than a subscription. The founder is Danny Postma, originally from Groningen in the Netherlands, these days based in Bali. Not a UK founder, but headshots are about as universally sellable a product as exists. A LinkedIn photo doesn't care what country you're in.

Here's the evidence, and I want to be precise about where every figure comes from, because every single one of them is the founder's own claim rather than an independently audited number. GetLatka's own research estimates roughly ninety-six thousand dollars of annual revenue around November twenty twenty-three, and Latka's page is explicit that this is their estimate, not something Postma told them directly. Starter Story published a full interview in December twenty twenty-four with a much bigger number straight from Postma himself, three hundred thousand dollars a month, three million six hundred thousand dollars a year. A Rewardful case study, corroborated by a separate site called AI Tuts, has him crediting affiliates alone for fifty thousand dollars or more of that every month, something like fifteen per cent of total revenue. And a piece on AI Business published this June repeats the same three hundred thousand dollars a month figure again. So the number hasn't just appeared once. It's been restated consistently for about two years, across four separate outlets, which is worth something even without a public Stripe dashboard to point to, though I'd still call it a claim rather than a fact.

The build itself has actually gotten easier since Postma started, and that's the real story here. Back in twenty twenty-three this required genuine machine learning work. Python, Stable Diffusion, a fine-tuning technique called DreamBooth, a separate model just to score output quality, and clean-up tooling on top. Postma has said he spent most of twenty twenty-four just tuning the pipeline so results stopped looking obviously synthetic. Today, according to AI Business's breakdown of the current stack, you'd build the equivalent product on ordinary web and database tooling wrapped around a hosted image-generation service, instead of training anything yourself. Compute runs about two to five dollars per customer against a twenty-nine dollar single-session price or sixty-nine dollars for three sessions, which puts gross margin somewhere around ninety to ninety-five per cent. Do the maths on that and the technology was never really the expensive part, even at this scale. The real cost is finding customers, not serving them. That gap between the twenty twenty-three version of this build and the twenty twenty-six version is exactly why it clears the bar this show uses, roughly ninety per cent buildable by an AI coding assistant, in days rather than months. The remaining ten per cent is the part no assistant does for you: actually testing which hosted image model produces results people are willing to put on a CV or a company website, and building a sane refund policy for a product where the complaint isn't "it's broken," it's "I don't like how I look."

Play the maths forward with round numbers, because that's the part that actually decides whether this is worth building. Charge something similar to Postma's pricing, say twenty-five pounds a session. Compute cost per customer is trivial against that, so a hundred sales in a month is two and a half thousand pounds of revenue for maybe a few hundred pounds of actual hosting and generation cost. The rest is margin waiting to be spent on whichever channel actually brings people through the door. That's the honest shape of this kind of business: thin on technical risk, heavy on distribution risk. The building is the easy part now. Finding a hundred people who want the thing is still the whole job.

Three risks before anyone gets excited, four if you count the one that's more of an observation. Every headline figure here is self-reported, and I could not find a single independently verified dashboard anywhere across any source for this business, so hold the number loosely. The niche is now crowded. AI Business's own June piece says dozens of copycat AI headshot tools have launched specifically because HeadshotPro proved the model worked, so a straight clone competes in a genuinely saturated market. And because it's a one-time purchase rather than a subscription, growth depends entirely on constantly finding new customers rather than compounding revenue from people who stick around, Postma's own reasoning being that almost nobody needs new headshots every month. Worth adding a fourth, smaller one: image-quality expectations only go up as everyone gets used to seeing good AI photography everywhere, so a tool that looked impressive in twenty twenty-three has to keep improving just to look merely acceptable in twenty twenty-six.

The actual lesson there isn't the vertical, it's picking one boring, narrow category nobody else has bothered building a tool for specifically. And purely as scale inspiration rather than a weekend project, Base44. Nobody's cloning that in a weekend, but the pattern underneath it, pick one narrow type of app and build only that, is exactly the kind of thing that scales down to a real weekend project.

Add it up and HeadshotPro is this episode's headline for a reason.
