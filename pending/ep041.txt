Why you care about any of this if you're not running a frontier lab: if the company building the tool you use every day is measuring how fast that tool is replacing its own engineers, that tells you something about the pace you should expect on your own side too. Now, the build.

Today's deep dive is an instructive one, because it's a success story and a cautionary tale in the same breath, and I think that combination will serve you better than another straightforward "here's a tool, go build it" segment. Most weeks I hand you a business that's still standing. This week I'm handing you one that isn't, and I think you'll get more out of understanding why than you would from one more everything-worked-out story.

The business is called GummySearch, built by a solo founder who goes by Fed — that's the only name he uses across every interview I found, no surname on the public record anywhere. What it did: it scanned Reddit communities for recurring complaints, questions and pain points, then used AI to summarise the patterns for founders and marketers doing product research before they built anything. If you've ever wanted to know what a thousand strangers in a niche are actually frustrated about, without reading a thousand posts yourself, that's the itch it scratched. Mechanically, that's a Reddit API call, some keyword and theme clustering, and a language model doing the summarising on top of all of it. That's not a research-lab build. That's the kind of thing Claude Code could put together for you in a matter of days, not months, and it's exactly why I picked it for this segment over something requiring real machine-learning infrastructure.

Here's the evidence, and I'll flag upfront that all of it is self-reported by Fed himself, not third-party verified through something like Stripe or RevenueCat, so take the exact figures with a pinch of salt even though the story is consistent across multiple tellings over several years. A Starter Story piece from twenty twenty-two has him at sixty thousand dollars in the first year. An Indie Hackers post from the following year puts cumulative revenue at around one hundred thousand dollars over two years, with monthly recurring revenue sitting near three thousand dollars at the time of writing. He's also mentioned, on his own social account, fifty thousand dollars raised from a single lifetime-deal promotion run over two months, a launch tactic a lot of solo founders lean on early to fund development before recurring revenue catches up. And Getlatka, which does its own independent modelling rather than just repeating founder quotes back at you, estimated the business at around ninety-six thousand dollars a year in annual recurring revenue. Four sources, all broadly consistent with each other over a multi-year span, none of them independently audited.

Now here's the twist, and it's the reason I picked this one over something safer. GummySearch shut down. New signups stopped at the end of November last year, and the whole thing closed for good on the first of December this year. Fed's own farewell post states plainly that he couldn't reach an agreement with Reddit that fit within Reddit's data API usage policies at his scale, which by then was over one hundred and thirty-five thousand users. Reddit tightened its commercial API terms significantly a couple of years back, in the same wave that famously blew up a lot of third-party Reddit apps overnight, and a business built entirely on top of someone else's platform data eventually ran straight into that wall, no matter how good the product itself was.

That's not a reason to skip this segment, it's the whole reason to run it. The mechanic here, mining a public community for pain points and turning that into a product, is sound, evidenced, and still buildable in days with today's tools. What killed GummySearch wasn't the idea, it was betting the entire business on a single third party's API terms, with no fallback plan for what happens if those terms ever change. In a year where every platform from Reddit to X to LinkedIn is tightening its data access to fund its own AI ambitions, that's not a one-off story, it's a pattern you should assume will keep repeating, and it should shape how you architect anything you build on somebody else's platform.

So if you wanted to build the same kind of tool today, the sensible version doesn't scrape Reddit at industrial scale and hope for the best. There are two safer paths, and they're not mutually exclusive. One: build it for a narrower, less contested data source where the terms of use are clearer and less likely to shift under you without warning — think a specific public forum, a niche Facebook group, or a trade body's own discussion board, rather than a platform-wide crawl that puts you on some trust-and-safety team's radar. Two, and this is the stronger option: point the exact same mining technique at data you already own outright, where no third party can ever revoke your access. A rough build sequence, if you wanted to try this yourself: pick one narrow community or one owned dataset, pull the last six to twelve months of posts or messages, run them through Claude for theme clustering and a weekly digest of what keeps coming up, then wrap that output in a simple login and a subscription so people pay to see it regularly rather than getting it as a one-off report. Realistic build time for a solo, non-technical founder using Claude Code as the developer doing the actual coding: three to five days for a working first version, closer to a fortnight if you want it polished enough to charge for with a straight face.

On the money, some deliberately conservative, round-number maths, and I'll say clearly upfront that this is illustrative, not a forecast built on anything real. If you charged twenty pounds a month for a niche version of this and picked up thirty paying customers in your first three months, that's six hundred pounds a month in revenue before hosting and API costs, which for a tool doing this kind of light processing should stay well under fifty pounds a month even at that scale. That's not a living on its own, and I wouldn't pretend otherwise, but it's a real, working proof that the mechanic earns money, and testing it costs you nothing close to the five-hundred-pound ceiling we set at the top of the show.

Risks, stated in full rather than glossed over. Platform dependency is the headline one, and we've just watched it kill the exact business we're discussing, so it's not theoretical. Positioning is hard, Fed says so himself in more than one of his own interviews, because there's no clean existing category to slot a tool like this into, no obvious shelf to put it on when you're trying to explain it to a stranger in one sentence. Recurring revenue was also thinner than the headline totals suggest, because a meaningful chunk of that hundred thousand dollars came from one-off lifetime deals rather than steady monthly income, which flatters the top-line figure more than it reflects the ongoing health of the business. And there's a UK-specific wrinkle worth naming if you're mining any public platform for user-generated content: you're still processing what could be personal data under UK data protection law, even if it's technically public, so any version of this needs a proper look at what you're allowed to store and for how long before you build past a prototype.

Where's the edge for you specifically, since that's the part I actually want you to walk away with. Both PlayFundWin and LeaguePages already have something Fed had to build entirely from scratch: an owned, permission-based audience. You don't need to scrape a public forum and hope Reddit doesn't change its mind about you next year — you already have donor feedback, entrant queries, and club committee messages sitting in your own systems, generated by people who've already agreed to hear from you and, in most cases, already trust you with far more sensitive information than a Reddit post ever contained. Point the same clustering-and-summarising technique inward at that data instead of outward at someone else's platform, and you sidestep the exact risk that ended GummySearch entirely, while getting a running start Fed never had and never could have had.
