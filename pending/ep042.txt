Today's build idea comes with a distinctly different flavour to most of what we've covered, because the founder behind it has already put a hard number on how many people he's helped: fifteen thousand seven hundred authors, by his own count, as of a post he put up back in February.

The product is called AIWriteBook, and the person behind it is Mykyta Chernenko, building under the name Paradox Builders. Like a good few of the founders we've featured on this show, there's no venture funding here and no large team — this is squarely a solo-builder story, which is exactly the kind of evidence this segment exists to surface rather than the well-funded, venture-backed version of the same idea. Here's the mechanic, and it's worth walking through properly because the whole idea rests on it: an author uploads a sample of their own writing, so the tool can match their voice and tone, along with either a book outline or an existing manuscript in whatever format they've got it in — Word document, PDF, ePub, whatever's lying around. The tool then drafts chapters that sound like the author wrote them, generates a cover and any interior illustrations you want, and exports files ready to upload straight to Amazon's self-publishing platform. It's not a generic "write me a book" chatbot prompt — the voice-matching step is doing real work, and it's the difference between a tool authors actually finish books with and one they abandon after a chapter because it sounds like nobody they recognise.

On the money, and I want to be careful here because this is exactly the kind of figure that's easy to overstate: his own post on Indie Hackers, forty days after launch, claims five thousand dollars in revenue over a twenty-eight-day window, with seventy-five paying subscribers. That's a snapshot from early in the product's life, not a confirmed steady monthly number, and there's no independent audit of it anywhere I could find — this one's self-reported, full stop, and I'd rather say that up front than let it sound more solid than it is. What I trust more is the growth story that follows it: from those seventy-five early subscribers to more than fifteen thousand authors served and over fifty thousand books generated across more than thirty languages, according to his own writeup from this February. And it's not purely his word that a real business exists underneath the claims — the product is listed on AppSumo with a lifetime deal priced at seventy-nine dollars against a normal price of a hundred and forty-four dollars a year, which is exactly the kind of detail that only shows up around an actual functioning product with actual paying customers, not vapourware somebody's talking up on a forum.

How would you build something like this in days rather than months? The hard part — and it really is the hard part, not a rounding error — is getting a model to draft convincingly in someone else's voice from a short sample, chapter after chapter, without drifting into generic AI-speak by page thirty. That's squarely the kind of task Claude is good at, and it's the entire core of what this product sells. Everything else around it is comparatively straightforward: an image-generation API for covers and any illustrations, a free open-source tool called Pandoc to handle the unglamorous but essential job of converting a finished draft into the exact file formats a self-publishing platform wants, a thin website to handle uploads and let someone iterate chapter by chapter, and Stripe sitting behind it for payment. None of that is exotic engineering. A working version, even a rough one, is realistic in a small number of days, not weeks, which is exactly the bar this show holds ideas to.

On honest money maths — and I want to flag clearly that these are illustrative round numbers I'm making up to give you a sense of scale, not anyone's real figures, and definitely not a forecast: domain, hosting, API credits and payment processing to get something live would run you somewhere around one hundred and fifty to three hundred pounds. If a narrow version of this sold thirty books at ninety-nine pounds each in its first year, that's just under three thousand pounds gross, against maybe fifty pounds of actual compute cost per finished book once you account for drafting, cover generation, and a few rounds of revision. That's a made-up scenario, built to be conservative rather than exciting, and I'd treat it exactly that way rather than as a plan.

Worth a word on how a product like this finds its first customers in practice, since that's usually the part these deep dives skip over. Chernenko's own growth story leans heavily on writer communities — self-publishing forums, author Facebook groups, the kind of places where someone who's stuck on chapter four of a novel they've been "meaning to finish" for two years is already hanging out and already primed to try something that promises to get unstuck. That's a cheap, low-friction way to find early users for a general writing tool, and it's worth noting because it's exactly the same playbook a narrower version would use, just in a smaller pond — grassroots club committees already talk to each other, across leagues and across regions, and a handful of good centenary books doing the rounds at committee meetings would likely do more for word of mouth than any advertising spend could.

The risks are real, and worth naming plainly rather than glossing over. Amazon's self-publishing platform now requires AI-content disclosure and has tightened its quality bar, so a further policy change there could choke off the main distribution channel with very little warning — that's the single biggest structural risk sitting under this whole category, and it's not hypothetical; it's already tightened once. The market's crowded too; there are several well-funded competitors chasing the same general idea from slightly different angles. And Chernenko's own stated lesson, in his words, is to treat the AI as an accelerator rather than a full replacement for the author — meaning there's real ongoing editorial and support overhead here that a purely passive product wouldn't have. Quality control matters more than it might look from outside, because the fastest way to kill a product like this is a reputation for generic, obviously-machine-written output that readers can smell from the first page.

Here's where it gets properly interesting for you specifically, Steve. The generic version of this tool is fighting in a crowded global market against well-resourced competitors, and that's not a fight worth picking from a standing start. But point the exact same underlying mechanic somewhere narrower — call it Club Legacy Books — and the whole shape of the opportunity changes. Grassroots sports clubs sit on decades of oral history that nobody's ever done anything with: old matchday programmes gathering dust in a cupboard, committee minutes going back generations, player interviews nobody wrote down properly, boxes of photographs nobody's ever digitised. Turn that raw material into a properly formatted club history or centenary book, sold to members and supporters as a one-off fundraising product, and you're not competing with AIWriteBook's target market at all — you're selling into an audience you already have direct relationships with, through PlayFundWin's existing club and treasurer contacts, using distribution you didn't have to go out and build from scratch. It becomes another fundraising product line for clubs you already work with, rather than a stranger's gamble thrown into a crowded global market. Picture the actual workflow: a club committee hands over a shoebox of old photos and a few members willing to be interviewed, someone transcribes an hour or two of conversation with a cheap transcription tool — maybe even the Grok tool from earlier in this episode — feeds that plus any old programmes into a voice-matched drafting tool, and inside a couple of weeks the club has a real, sellable centenary book they'd never have produced on their own. That's the whole edge here: not a better AI writing tool than the next one, but a smaller, defensible market for the same underlying mechanic, sold through a channel that already trusts you. On pricing, a sensible starting point would sit somewhere between a nicely printed matchday programme and a proper coffee-table book — think forty to sixty pounds a copy for a hardback run, sold as pre-orders to members before you commit to printing a single one, which keeps your actual capital risk close to zero while you test whether a given club's supporters will really pay for it.
