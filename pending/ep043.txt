Today's business idea is a proper solo-builder story, the kind where one person with a laptop and ten days turned a properly annoying task into real income. It also happens to be a useful case study in something this show doesn't talk about often enough: what happens when the founder's own numbers and an outside estimate of the same business don't quite agree with each other, and how you should think about that gap rather than just picking whichever figure flatters the story more.

The product is called aiCarousels, and the founder is Fernando Pessagno, an Argentine product designer based in Malmö, Sweden, self-taught as a coder with no formal computer science background. The idea solves a specific, recognisable pain: making those slide-by-slide "carousel" posts you see all over LinkedIn and Instagram is fiddly and slow if you're doing it properly in a design tool, and looks amateurish if you're not. Pessagno built a web app where you paste in a topic, some freeform text, a website link, or even a YouTube link, and the tool turns it into carousel copy, drops that copy into a set of locked-position design templates with fonts that automatically resize to fit, lets you reorder the slides, swap colours and fonts, and exports the finished images or a PDF ready to post. No design skill required from the user at all — that's the entire value proposition in one sentence. Worth sitting with why that particular pain point was even there to be solved: anyone who's tried to build one of these carousel posts by hand in a general-purpose design tool knows the actual friction isn't coming up with what to say, it's getting ten slides of text to look consistent, sized correctly, and on-brand without spending an hour nudging text boxes around. Pessagno didn't invent a new kind of content, he just removed the one part of making it that nobody enjoys, which is exactly the pattern worth training your eye to spot in your own businesses — not "what new thing could AI do," but "what existing, annoying, repetitive step is quietly costing someone an hour every time."

On the money, and this one's a good example of why it pays to check more than one source. His own Starter Story interview, headlined "I Accidentally Built A SaaS in Ten Days and Quit My Nine to Five," claims fifty dollars in monthly recurring revenue on day one, from five paying users, after launching on Product Hunt in April twenty twenty-three. By his own account, six months later he'd reached five thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue, against an original twelve-month target of just two and a half thousand — so a business that beat its own year-one goal inside half the time, if you take the founder's number at face value. Getlatka's independent research, though, tells a noticeably more modest story: around two and a half thousand dollars in annual revenue as of November twenty twenty-three, growing to roughly fifteen thousand dollars a year by twenty twenty-four. That's a real gap between the founder's self-reported monthly figure and an independent estimate of the annual one, and I think the honest thing to do is give you both rather than pick whichever sounds better. Treat Getlatka's number as the more credible floor, and Pessagno's own claim as the optimistic ceiling, and the truth is very likely somewhere in between, run by one person, with no outside funding.

I want to spend a moment on why that gap exists at all, because it's a pattern you'll see again on this show and it's worth knowing how to read it rather than just shrugging at it. A founder's own self-reported monthly recurring revenue is usually a snapshot taken at a specific, often favourable moment — sometimes right after a lifetime-deal promotion, sometimes counting figures that don't survive into the following month. An independent research firm like Getlatka, by contrast, tends to build its estimate from a mix of public signals, so it can lag behind reality or miss a genuine spike entirely, but it's less prone to cherry-picking a good week and presenting it as the steady state. Neither source is lying to you, necessarily — they're just measuring different things, at different times, with different incentives. The discipline this show tries to hold itself to is giving you both numbers rather than the one that makes a better headline, and letting you decide how much weight to put on each.

How would you build this? The bulk of it is squarely in Claude's wheelhouse: a web app with a canvas-style editor for the slide templates, fonts that resize themselves to fit whatever text goes in, an AI content step that turns a topic or a link into six to ten slides' worth of copy, a login and billing layer, and an export step that turns the finished carousel into images or a PDF. None of that requires exotic engineering; a working version is realistic inside a small number of days, which is exactly this show's usual bar. The part that isn't code at all, and where Pessagno's own account gives real credit, is the design work — a template pack that actually looks professional — and the early marketing grind: he built this one in public on LinkedIn, posted on Reddit, wrote on Medium, and did the unglamorous work of engaging with early users personally. That's the ten per cent of this that AI simply can't do for you, and it's worth naming honestly rather than pretending the whole thing runs itself.

Worth being specific about the actual build order too, since "a small number of days" can mean very different things depending on how you sequence it. Day one is the template system and the export pipeline — get one genuinely good-looking carousel format working end to end, from blank page to a downloadable image, before touching the AI content generation at all, because that's the part a user actually judges the product on in the first ten seconds. Day two or three is wiring in the content generation itself, which is the easy part technically but the part that needs the most iteration to sound like a person rather than a generic AI summary. Whatever's left of the week goes on billing, login, and enough polish that the first users you show it to don't bounce off a rough edge before they see what it actually does. That ordering — interface and output quality first, AI wiring second, admin plumbing last — is worth remembering for any build this show covers, not just this one.

On honest money maths, using only the real cited figures as the basis and flagging clearly where I'm extrapolating: a domain, a no-code backend for login and billing along the lines of what Pessagno actually used, modest AI-API costs at low volume, and payment processing that only charges on transactions, would get you live for comfortably under one hundred and fifty pounds — well inside this show's usual five-hundred-pound ceiling. From there, scaling from Getlatka's more conservative trajectory alone, a UK builder reaching even that lower estimate within a year would have a genuinely profitable one-person side income for a tiny outlay. I want to say clearly that's a scaled illustration built from real cited numbers, not a promise, and definitely not a guarantee anyone matches even the conservative case.

The risks are real and worth naming without softening them. Canva and Adobe Express both already offer carousel templates and are actively adding their own AI content generation on top, so the competitive moat here is thin — a nicely designed template is not hard for a well-resourced rival to copy. A meaningful share of the growth in the actual case study came from the founder's own sustained, personal community-building effort over many months, not from the product doing the selling by itself, so anyone expecting a purely passive outcome is setting themselves up to be disappointed. And the gap between the self-reported and independently-estimated revenue figures is itself a reminder to treat any single-source success story with a healthy amount of scepticism, this one included.

None of that makes the idea a bad one — plenty of solid businesses live in crowded, thin-moat categories and win on execution rather than a defensible secret. It does mean the version worth building isn't a straight clone aimed at the same generic LinkedIn-creator audience Pessagno went after, since that fight now includes two design giants with far bigger budgets than his ever was. The version worth building is one aimed at an audience with a specific, recurring need the giants haven't bothered to template for, which is exactly what the match-day angle below is.

Here's the angle that actually matters for you, Steve. A generic carousel tool is fighting Canva and Adobe on their own turf, which is not a fight worth picking from scratch. But you're not starting from scratch — you already have relationships with grassroots sports clubs and, through them, their sponsors. Point the exact same mechanic at "match-day carousel packs": a fixture result, a player-of-the-match shout-out, a sponsor mention, automatically turned into a ready-to-post Instagram or LinkedIn carousel the moment the final whistle blows. That's not a cold-start product looking for an audience from nothing — it's a retention add-on for customers you already have, sold into a workflow clubs already do badly or not at all, using distribution that took you years to build rather than a Product Hunt launch and a lot of hope.

Picture it concretely: Saturday afternoon, a club's first team wins three-nil, and right now that result probably ends up as a single sentence in a WhatsApp group and maybe a bare scoreline on a league table somewhere, and nothing else happens with it. With this mechanic sitting behind PlayFundWin or LeaguePages, the same result becomes a five-slide carousel within minutes of full time — the scoreline, the standout performer, a thank-you to the match sponsor, a note about the next fixture, a call to action for the next fundraiser — ready to post to the club's own channels and easy for the sponsor to reshare on theirs. Multiply that by every club you already work with and every weekend of the season, and you've got a steady stream of properly useful content that costs those clubs nothing extra to produce and gives their sponsors a reason to keep paying for visibility they can actually point to. That's the kind of feature that doesn't win you new customers on its own, but makes the customers you already have considerably harder to lose to a competitor, and it's worth remembering that retention features are usually cheaper to build and easier to sell internally than anything aimed at brand-new customer acquisition, precisely because the audience already trusts you enough to say yes to something small before they'd ever say yes to something big.
