Let's get into what shipped.

xAI put out Grok four point seven yesterday, the twenty-first of September, and the pitch is the usual one for a new coding model: faster, and about half the price of comparable rivals. I read the announcement myself, so that part of the claim is solid. Standard pricing lands at two dollars in and six dollars out per million tokens, with a faster tier running double that. On paper, genuinely cheap.

Here's the catch, and it's actually the more interesting story than the launch itself. Independent testing from VentureBeat found Grok four point seven burns through roughly twice the output tokens its own predecessor needed to finish the same tasks, and nearly three times what a comparable OpenAI model used on the identical job. Run the real maths on an actual task and their test landed at three dollars seventy-four for that one job, more than some of the models it's supposedly undercutting on price. I'll flag that this particular cost comparison comes from a third party's own benchmark, not from xAI's own figures, so hold the exact number loosely. But the shape of the finding is the one worth carrying with you: a cheap sticker price per token tells you almost nothing about what a task will actually cost, because a model that reasons more verbosely can quietly spend its way past a pricier, more efficient rival without anyone noticing until the invoice lands. If you're choosing between models for anything you run at real volume, benchmark the finished job end to end, not the headline price per token.

Grok four point seven landed inside GitHub Copilot on the very same day, which I also read directly off GitHub's own changelog rather than a summary of it. It's now selectable across every Copilot tier, in VS Code, Visual Studio, the command line tool, the cloud coding agent, JetBrains and Xcode. Billing follows the same list pricing as everywhere else, so the token-efficiency point from a moment ago applies just as much here if you're the kind of person who switches models inside Copilot to try and save money.

Perplexity published a piece of research worth a mention even though it reads more like an engineering paper than a product launch. They described a training technique that learns from real users' actual agent sessions, the ones that worked and the ones that failed, and used it to cut tool-call failures in their Comet and Computer agent products by just over twenty-one per cent in live testing. The offline error rate dropped from around two and three-quarter per cent down to under one per cent. I read the post myself and the numbers are laid out plainly enough to trust. If you're one of the people actually letting an AI agent click through web forms and book things on your behalf rather than just chatting with it, this is exactly the unglamorous kind of improvement that decides whether you trust it enough to leave it running unattended while you do something else.

OpenAI opened up four new structured learning tracks inside OpenAI Academy, aimed separately at people applying AI at work, developers building with it, leaders adopting it across a team, and educators teaching it. It's free, and you get a completion credential at the end of each track. I read the announcement directly rather than a write-up of it. If you're bringing on a first hire and want them productive with these tools fast without building the onboarding material yourself from scratch, this is exactly the kind of free structure worth pointing them at instead of a paid course that covers the same ground.

And a smaller item, more governance than product, but worth thirty seconds. OpenAI has set up an independent advisory group on mathematics, nine working mathematicians including some properly serious names in the field, to help vet and communicate any mathematical claims that come out of its models, after reports of an internal model resolving more than a hundred open problems including work touching on Navier-Stokes. The members are unpaid and explicitly free to disagree with the company in public. I only have this from OpenAI's own post announcing it, so take it as their framing of their own oversight rather than independent confirmation that the underlying mathematical claims all hold up under scrutiny. It's worth knowing this body now exists. It isn't worth building your morning around.

Now, the build.

Today's pick is Sleek, a chat-based tool for generating mobile app screens, built by a solo founder called Mattia Pomelli. You describe the screen you want in plain English, something like a clean onboarding flow for a fitness app, and it generates production-ready mobile user interface designs back in minutes, which you can then export straight to Figma or directly to code. It's deliberately narrow: mobile app screens only, not general graphic design work, not marketing images, not full websites.

The evidence here is unusually well corroborated for this kind of story, which is a big part of why I picked it over some louder candidates. Pomelli wrote up hitting ten thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue within six weeks of launch, on an Indie Hackers interview post published back in January. The climb was documented stage by stage: two and a half thousand dollars in week two, six thousand by week three, eight thousand by week four, ten thousand by week six. He posted the same story himself on Hacker News under a Show HN post that's refreshingly honest about the fact that this was his third product attempt, and he called it his last real shot before he'd have gone looking for an ordinary job. A separate independent write-up on Substack, by someone with no obvious connection to him, lands on the same ten thousand dollar figure, and the product is also listed in its own right on Product Hunt. Three separate angles on the same claim, from the founder himself and from people writing about him independently, is about as corroborated as these founder stories tend to get. I'd still call the underlying number founder-reported rather than platform-verified, so hold it with a reasonable pinch of salt, but it's a genuinely well-sourced founder-reported number rather than one screenshot posted once and never mentioned again.

Here's the part of the story that matters more than the headline figure, and it's worth slowing down on. Pomelli didn't build this from nothing in three weeks. He'd already built and abandoned two earlier products, and by his own account, a meaningful chunk of Sleek's actual design-generation engine was reused from one of those earlier attempts. That's not a knock on what he achieved, it's the real mechanism behind it. A fast launch that looks like it appeared out of thin air almost always has a slower, less successful attempt sitting underneath it, doing the expensive work of teaching the founder what to build and leaving behind code and lessons he could reuse. If you ever compare your own week one against someone else's six-week success story and feel like you're already behind, there's a decent chance you're comparing your first attempt to someone else's third.

How buildable is this with the tools you've already got. Very. Underneath the chat interface it's a fairly standard shape once you break it down. Step one, a language model turns your plain-English brief into a structured layout description rather than a finished image. Step two, that structure gets rendered against a library of pre-built mobile interface components and templates, so the model is filling in a scaffold rather than painting from a blank canvas every time. Step three, the result exports to something usable, either a Figma file a designer can refine or working front-end code a developer can ship. Step four is the boring but necessary wrapper: accounts, billing, a simple usage limit on the free tier. Claude Code or an equivalent coding agent could realistically get you a working version of that core loop in days rather than weeks, especially if you start from an existing open-source component library instead of designing your own from a blank page. Costs to get something live and testable are refreshingly small: a domain name, free tiers on hosting and a database to start, Stripe for billing with no monthly fee, and a modest pay-as-you-go buffer for API credits while you're testing. All in, comfortably under a hundred and fifty pounds to get a working demo in front of real people.

Now the honest, conservative money maths, and I mean conservative. Nobody building this next week should expect Pomelli's numbers, because Pomelli didn't start from zero. If a first version found even a small fraction of what Sleek achieved, say fifty paying users at around twenty-five pounds a month, that's twelve hundred fifty pounds a month in recurring revenue. That's a real, useful side income, comfortably covering the setup cost within the first month and then some, and it's a sensible floor to plan around rather than a promise of anything higher. Getting anywhere near ten thousand dollars a month would need either Pomelli's reused code and existing audience, which you won't have on day one, or a sharply differentiated angle that makes people choose you over the half dozen well-funded alternatives already in this space.

Which brings me to the honest risks, because there are real ones here. This is a crowded lane. Uizard, Galileo, Vercel's own v-zero tool, Lovable and several others already do some version of describe it and get a design back, and some of them are free or bundled into platforms people already pay for. The technical moat is thin. This is largely clever prompting sitting on top of a well-built template library and a foundation model everyone else has access to too, which means a well-resourced competitor could replicate the mechanics without enormous difficulty if they decided it was worth their time. And Pomelli's specific speed had two real advantages behind it that you shouldn't assume you'll get for free: reusable code from two earlier attempts, and an existing audience from building in public, which is most of why he needed to spend nothing on advertising to get that first wave of paying users through the door.

So where does this actually help Steve specifically. Not as a general SaaS play chasing Sleek's own market, that's a crowded fight against better-resourced competitors. The interesting angle is the underlying mechanic, redirected at sales rather than at a new product line. Picture using that same chat-to-mockup approach to generate a branded prize draw landing page or a club team site in minutes, live, in front of a prospect, while you're actually on the call with them. Describe the club, its colours, its badge, and get back something that looks close to finished before the call even ends, instead of promising to send something over next week once you've found time for it. That's a genuine edge for PlayFundWin and especially for LeaguePages, because unlike Pomelli starting from a completely blank slate, there's already a library of real branded prize draw pages and club sites sitting in the existing customer base that could train or template the thing from day one, and an actual pipeline of live prospects to test it on immediately rather than waiting for cold traffic to stumble across it.

Three runner-ups from today's research, all backed by real published numbers, none of them niched for the ventures, just worth knowing about.

Studr, an AI notes and flashcards tool built by a founder called Mustafa, turns recorded lectures into structured notes and spaced-repetition flashcards aimed specifically at exam revision, not general note-taking for its own sake. It sits on a verified revenue tracker at two hundred and eighteen dollars a month recurring. That's modest, clearly, but it's actually platform-verified rather than self-reported, which is worth something on its own even at a small scale.

AI Podcasting, run by a founder called Adithyan Ilangovan, isn't really a self-serve product at all, it's a small service business. It uses AI to automate roughly eighty per cent of podcast editing, show notes and distribution, and sells the result as a flat monthly retainer somewhere around two and a half to three thousand dollars. He put the business at fourteen and a half thousand dollars a month in an Indie Hackers post from March, grown entirely through referrals with no advertising spend at all. It's a useful reminder that not every small AI business has to be a self-serve tool. Sometimes the product is you, doing the boring, repetitive bit with AI doing the heavy lifting behind the scenes, and charging a proper price for the finished result rather than a subscription for the raw tool.

And a pair of niche speech-writing tools from a founder called Stefan Wirth, an Eulogy Generator and a Maid of Honor Speech Generator, both AI tools that turn a description of a person and an occasion into a fully personalised speech. He's reported them combined at around twelve thousand dollars a month, sourced from his own published figures rather than a third party's estimate. It's a good reminder that some of the best small AI businesses aren't chasing a huge addressable market at all. They're picking one specific, emotionally loaded moment in someone's life and doing that one thing properly, rather than trying to be useful to everyone.
