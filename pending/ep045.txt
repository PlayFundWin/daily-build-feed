Part one. What shipped.

Anthropic broke a four-day silence yesterday with the kind of release that actually matters if you're paying by the token. Claude Opus five point five landed on the twenty-second, and the headline claim, straight from Anthropic's own announcement, is that it performs at roughly the level of the previous Opus on most work while costing forty per cent less to run. I read that on Anthropic's news page, and I cross-checked it against the Claude Code changelog and the API release notes, and both agree on the mechanics: it's now the default model in Claude Code, it carries a full million tokens of context, and it's priced at four dollars in and twenty dollars out per million tokens. There's a catch worth flagging before you get too excited: thinking can no longer be switched off. Every call now runs with adaptive reasoning turned on, which is good news for accuracy and slightly annoying news for anyone who liked dashing off a trivial request without paying the thinking tax. Why you care: if you're running long agentic coding sessions, a forty per cent cost cut on a model that holds a million tokens of context is real money back in your pocket, but budget for the fact that even your simple one-line asks now come with a bit of built-in deliberation.

Same day, over at OpenAI, two new models turned up with almost cosmic names: GPT-six Sol and GPT-six Luna, alongside an update to prompt caching for the GPT-six family. OpenAI's own announcement is light on the fine detail of what Sol and Luna are each optimised for, and I'll be upfront about that rather than guess at a split I can't confirm — I've read the announcement itself, so the existence and the date are solid, but the deeper positioning is still thin from OpenAI's side, and I'd treat any confident explainer you see elsewhere of "Sol does this, Luna does that" as search-only until OpenAI actually spells it out. Part two. The build.

Today's pick is a category this show hasn't touched in forty-four episodes: an AI tool that turns a plain-English request into a working spreadsheet formula.

The founder is David Bressler, and the product is Excel Formula Bot. He built the first version in Bubble, a no-code platform, over the summer of twenty twenty-two, with no coding background at all. You type something like "sum column B if column A says paid," and it hands back the exact formula, plus a short explanation of what it does. The pitch is almost insultingly simple, which is usually a good sign in this kind of business — nobody has to be taught what the product does.

The evidence here is unusually well corroborated for a solo-founder story, because Bressler wrote it up himself, more than once, on Indie Hackers, at different stages of the business, and the numbers move consistently between posts rather than mysteriously improving with each retelling, which itself deserves a moment's notice — plenty of founder case studies you hear about on this show only exist as one triumphant post, and this one has a paper trail. In his first detailed write-up, he described hitting three thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue and twenty thousand dollars in total revenue within two months of switching the product from free to paid. A later post picks up the story after a repricing — he moved from a flat two dollars ninety-nine to a tiered structure topping out around thirty dollars a month — and reports thirty thousand dollars in revenue over the following three months, with lifetime customer value roughly quadrupling, from about twenty-seven dollars to over a hundred. That repricing detail matters more than it sounds: he didn't grow by finding more customers, he grew by charging the ones he already had more, once he'd proven the tiered value was there. A third post, a straightforward case study rather than a founder retelling, projects a hundred and sixty-four thousand, eight hundred and fifty dollars in profit over twelve months and notes he had a hundred and eighty thousand monthly visitors at that point. He's also said, in more than one of these posts, that he turned down both a partnership approach from Microsoft and investment interest from well-known venture firms to keep the business independent — which tells you something about the margins on a product like this once it's past the awkward early months, since that's not a decision you make on a business barely covering its server bill. None of these figures are audited by a third party, so take them as founder-reported rather than externally verified, but three separate posts from the same person telling a consistent, escalating story is about as solid as founder-reported evidence gets on this show.

How you'd actually build this. Start by wrapping an API call to Claude or GPT behind one very specific prompt: take a plain-English description of a spreadsheet task and return the exact formula plus a one-line explanation of what it does and why. That's the entire technical core, and it's an afternoon's work, not a sprint — the hard part of this business was never the code, it was everything after the code. Ship it three ways if you want the full funnel: a bare web page for people who land on it from a Google search; a Google Sheets add-on built with Apps Script, which gets you free discovery through the Workspace Marketplace without spending a penny on ads; and, later, once you've proven demand, an Excel add-in through Microsoft's own AppSource store, which is a slower approval process and worth leaving until you know the idea works. Price it the way Bressler eventually did, not the way he started: a handful of free formulas a month as a taster, then somewhere around five to eight pounds a month for unlimited use, with a higher tier — call it twenty to thirty pounds — for the harder stuff like VBA macros, SQL queries and regular expressions, which is exactly the upsell path that quadrupled his customer value. Resist the urge to launch at that higher tier straight away; he didn't, and the free-to-cheap-to-expensive ladder is doing real work in his numbers. For distribution, the audience already exists and already complains loudly about formulas in public: r slash excel, r slash googlesheets, a short video showing a properly fiddly formula solved in four seconds, and a Product Hunt launch once you've got a working version people can actually try rather than a landing page promising one.

Now the honest money maths, because this show doesn't do fantasy numbers. Getting started costs you API credits, a domain, a Stripe account and probably a no-code subscription if you go the Bubble route like Bressler did — call it twenty-five to thirty pounds a month in running costs, comfortably inside the five-hundred-pound ceiling this segment works to. There's a real risk cost worth naming rather than glossing over: Bressler has described an unexpected bill in the low thousands of dollars from unmetered API usage during a viral traffic spike, before he'd put a paywall up. Build in hard usage caps from day one — this is the one mistake in his story that's genuinely avoidable, and there's no excuse for repeating it now that rate limiting is a solved, boring, five-minute problem. A conservative, no-virality base case looks like this: fifty paying users at six pounds a month gets you to roughly three hundred pounds a month in recurring revenue within your first month or two of posting consistently on Reddit and short-form video, which is a real number, not Bressler's outlier trajectory. Getting to something that actually changes your month — fifteen hundred to two thousand pounds a month — realistically needs somewhere between two and three hundred paying users, or a smaller number of people paying for that higher-tier power version. Put another way: this is not a get-rich-quick pitch, it's a genuinely small, boring, defensible side business if you execute the distribution properly, and it stops being interesting the moment you expect it to replace a salary in month one.

The risks, stated plainly rather than buried. The moat here is thin — Microsoft and Google are both actively adding native AI formula help directly into Excel and Sheets, so the entire premise of a third-party tool doing this could get squeezed by the platforms themselves over time, and speed to a defensible niche matters more than usual; the smart move is probably to specialise into something the platforms won't bother building, like the charity-specific angle below, rather than compete head-on with a general formula bot. The uncapped-API-bill trap is real and documented, as above. Marketplace approval for both the Google Workspace and Microsoft AppSource stores can take weeks, which is longer than the build itself, so start that paperwork the same week you start coding rather than after. And the base price point is properly low, which means this only works at volume unless you find a niche willing to pay for the power tier.

Which brings me to the one place this connects to something you're already running, Steve. There's a specific, narrow niche version of this worth a passing thought rather than a pivot: a charity-and-prize-draw spreadsheet assistant, built for the exact formulas a fundraising operator actually needs — Gift Aid calculations, raffle-odds and payout tables, ticket-sales reconciliation against a bank statement. It's not a product PlayFundWin needs to build today, but it's the kind of small, free value-add tool that costs almost nothing to knock together over a weekend and gives a club or charity customer one more reason to trust you with their books rather than a rival's. It also happens to sidestep the exact competitive risk I just flagged, since neither Microsoft nor Google has any reason to build Gift Aid formulas into their own products. Filed as an idea, not a to-do.

And Kleo, from Cameron Whiteside, is a browser extension that ghostwrites LinkedIn posts in your own voice for personal-brand building — a write-up on Medium, citing the founder's own Indie Hackers numbers, has it reaching sixty-two thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue within three months, built mostly through a waitlist and paid webinars rather than organic growth, which tells you the launch mechanics mattered as much as the product. None of the three have an obvious fit to any of your ventures, and I'd rather say that plainly than force one just to give this segment a tidier ending.

Part four. One action today.

Pick a single spreadsheet task you or someone on your team does by hand every week — raffle payout reconciliation, a Gift Aid calculation, matching ticket sales against a bank statement, whatever it is — and spend no more than thirty minutes today asking Claude to write you the exact formula or script for it. Don't build a product. Don't even think about a product yet. Just solve your own actual problem once, today, with the tool you already have access to, and pay attention to how long it took and how much friction there was getting from the request to something you could actually paste into a cell. That thirty minutes is the entire feasibility test for today's build idea, run on a real task with real stakes, and it costs you nothing but half an hour. If it works cleanly, you've just proven the core mechanic on your own data before anyone else's money is involved.
