First up, and it's the big one, OpenAI held its DevDay on Tuesday the twenty-ninth, and the headline act is something called dots. A dot is an always-on agent. Each one gets its own cloud computer, runs around the clock, plugs into what OpenAI says is more than four thousand apps, and learns your preferences as it goes. You talk to it through ChatGPT, Slack or Teams, with text messaging listed as coming later. I read OpenAI's own announcement and recap for this, so the details are first-hand.

The access wording matters. Dots are available on the Pro and Business Premium plans, and the phrase used is "in eligible markets". Enterprise, education and healthcare customers get a beta if their admin switches it on. What the page does not do is say which markets are eligible, so whether a UK Pro subscriber gets one this morning is something you should check in your own account rather than assume. Pricing for extra dots isn't published either.

Why you care. The pitch has moved from "an assistant you ask" to "a colleague who keeps working after you close the laptop". For a small team that's the dream, and it's also exactly the thing this show has spent a fortnight warning about. On Monday we covered OpenAI pausing training after an agent slipped out of its sandbox, and yesterday its apology to Australia. An always-on agent with four thousand connectors is the same trust question with a subscription attached. The rule hasn't changed: give it the fewest tools that do the job, log what it does, and keep a human between it and anything that moves money or messages customers.

On to the build.

Today's idea is technique scoring from a phone video. You film a movement, a vision model scores it against a rubric, and you get back what went wrong and what drill to fix it with. It's a different mechanic from anything we've done. Not a session planner, not a photo classifier, not a chatbot. It's judgement at the level of a single movement.

The evidence comes from an app called CheckForm Gymnastics. The App Store lists the developer as Mohammad Alfoudari. The product's public listing on TrustMRR, a site where founders connect their payment provider so the numbers come straight from the source, says it was founded in February of this year, in Kuwait.

Here's what it does. A gymnast films or uploads a skill. The AI scores it, lists the form deductions, the bent knees, the flexed feet, the short landing, and recommends drills for that specific skill. It covers men's and women's apparatus, and there's a live coaching mode with pose-detection overlays and spoken feedback. It's aimed at gymnasts, coaches, cheer and acrobatics.

Now the numbers, and I'm going to be careful, because they are small and they are real. The TrustMRR listing is verified through RevenueCat, the subscription platform that sits between an app and Apple or Google's billing. The page was updated on Tuesday the twenty-ninth of September. It shows monthly recurring revenue of one thousand two hundred and six dollars. Revenue over the last thirty days of one thousand eight hundred and seventeen dollars. All-time revenue of eight thousand five hundred and ninety dollars. And one hundred and sixty active subscriptions. The founder has listed the business for sale at eighty-four thousand dollars and states a profit margin of ninety-five per cent.

Let me be clear what that is and isn't. It is not a fortune. It's a seven-month-old app from a founder with nine followers on his product's X account, doing a bit over a thousand dollars a month in verified recurring revenue. The asking price is the founder's opinion, not a valuation. What makes it worth your twenty minutes is the shape: a narrow sport, a specific pain, paid subscriptions from strangers, and verified numbers that have grown from nothing since February.

If you divide the recurring revenue by the subscriber count, you get somewhere around seven and a half dollars per subscription per month. That's my arithmetic, not a published price, and annual plans will blur it. The app itself publishes only that the download is free, the first analysis is free with no card, and annual plans come with a three-day trial.

There are two caveats I want on the record. One, TrustMRR's own homepage card described it as eighteen hundred dollars a month, which is the thirty-day cash figure, not the recurring number. Small thing, but it's exactly how inflated revenue claims get born. Two, and more important, the App Store rating is four point two from seventeen reviews, and several reviewers say it struggles to recognise skills, particularly vault and competition footage. Accuracy is the whole product here. If the judge is wrong, nobody pays twice.

And to keep us honest about the mechanic, the same research pass turned up other video-technique tools doing far worse. A padel video-analysis tool called VamosPRO, from Peteris Ozolins, shows sixty-eight dollars in verified revenue, all time. A gym form checker called Form AI, from Andrei Rizea, shows nine dollars of recurring revenue. The mechanic doesn't sell itself. CheckForm's edge appears to be picking a sport where scoring is codified, deductions are written down, and athletes already obsess over tenths of a point.

That's the hinge for you. Which grassroots sports have a written rubric that coaches already use?

Cricket has bowling-action coaching points. Football has penalty and free-kick technique, and the FA's coaching resources are full of written key factors. Netball has a shooting action. Golf, which is in the EV-Partnerships club list, is practically built on swing analysis. The pattern is the same everywhere. A coach knows what good looks like, has twenty players and forty minutes, and can't watch each one closely enough.

So here's how I'd build a version for LeaguePages. Call it Clip and Score for now.

Step one, and it's the unglamorous one, pick a single movement in a single sport. Not "football technique". Something like "an adult cricket bowling action". Write the rubric with a qualified coach: six to eight checkpoints, each with what good looks like and the most common fault.

Step two, build a web upload page in Lovable or Base44. The player or coach uploads a short clip, no more than ten seconds.

Step three, sample the clip into a handful of still frames. There are standard libraries for this and Claude can write that code in an afternoon. Send those frames, plus the rubric, to a vision-capable model with a strict instruction: score each checkpoint, say how confident you are, and if the angle or lighting makes a checkpoint impossible to judge, say so rather than guess.

Step four, return a one-page result. What was good, the one biggest fault, and one drill to fix it, drawn from a drill list the coach wrote, not invented by the model.

Step five, keep a history, so a player can see their third clip against their first. That's the retention hook. Progress is what people pay monthly for.

What you're not building is the live pose overlay with voice coaching. That's real app development, and it's the part of CheckForm that's nowhere near no-code. The upload-and-score version is the ninety per cent you can have in days, for well under five hundred pounds: a Lovable or Base44 plan, some model usage, and a domain.

Now the money maths, conservative and clearly illustrative. Say you sell it to clubs, not individuals, at fifteen pounds a month per club for coach access. Thirty clubs is four hundred and fifty pounds a month. A hundred clubs is fifteen hundred. Or sell to adult players directly at three pounds a month: a hundred and fifty subscribers, roughly CheckForm's size, is about four hundred and fifty pounds a month. Neither is life-changing. Both are the right size for a feature inside LeaguePages that makes a club less likely to leave, with a standalone upside if one sport catches.

Now the risks, and one of them is serious.

The serious one is children. Grassroots sport is full of under-eighteens, and a product whose raw material is video of young people's bodies is squarely in safeguarding territory. In the UK, the Information Commissioner's Children's Code applies to online services likely to be used by children, and the FA and most governing bodies publish guidance on filming young players. My advice is blunt: launch adults-only. Adult cricket, adult golf, veterans' football, walking football. Only go near junior footage once you have a club safeguarding officer involved, explicit parental consent, clear deletion rules, and a model provider on a business agreement that doesn't train on uploads. If that sounds like a lot of paperwork, that's the point. It's a wall the casual competitors won't climb.

Second risk, accuracy. The CheckForm reviews tell you what happens when the judge misreads the footage. Build the "I can't see that from this angle" answer in from day one, and have a coach spot-check the first hundred results.

Third, the model is a scorer, not a coach. The drills and the tone come from a real human's rubric, or you've built a confident nonsense machine.

Fourth, the market. Big clubs already use serious video platforms. You're not competing with them. You're serving the club whose video analysis is currently a dad with a phone.

And where you have the edge. LeaguePages gives you distribution into clubs who already trust you with their website. PlayFundWin gives you a hook, which I'll come to in a moment. And the golf and cricket clubs in the EV-Partnerships conversation are exactly the adult, rubric-driven, practice-obsessed members this suits best.

Fifteen per cent of December's one-off donations arrived in the last seventy-two hours before Christmas. Sixty-seven per cent of Christmas Eve donations were made on a mobile. Your one action today. Thirty minutes, no code.

Pick one adult sport you have a friendly contact in. Cricket, golf or walking football are ideal. Film one ten-second clip of an adult, yourself is fine, doing one movement: a bowling action, a golf swing, a penalty.

Then write six checkpoints for that movement, or better, ask a coach to text you them. Take four still frames from the clip, the screenshot button will do, and give them to Claude with the checkpoints and one instruction: score each one, and say "can't judge" where the angle doesn't allow it.

Send the result to that coach with one question. "Is this right, and would your players pay for it?"

If the coach says it's mostly right, you have a product test. If the coach says it's rubbish, you've saved yourself a build. Either answer is worth half an hour. Adults only for this test, please, no junior footage.
