Right. What actually came out of the pipe since yesterday.

First up, Anthropic. On Friday the second of October, Anthropic announced something called the Claude Frontier Academy, backed by a hundred million dollars. I read the announcement itself on this one. The goal is to train ten thousand engineers by the end of twenty twenty-seven. They are calling the graduates Frontier Deployed Engineers, which sounds like a job title invented by someone who has never had to put it on a business card.

The format is borrowed from medicine. A residency, not a lecture. Engineers do a multi-day, in-person course with Anthropic's own engineers, then go back to their own organisation and spend twelve weeks leading a real Claude project. Training happens in San Francisco, New York and London.

The first cohorts come from Accenture, Bain, Capgemini, Deloitte, McKinsey, Morgan Stanley, Commonwealth Bank of Australia and Novo Nordisk. Places are by nomination, through an Anthropic account team or a partner manager. There is no published price and no route in for a two-person company in Bedfordshire.

Why you care. Two reasons, pulling in opposite directions. The first is competition. The big consultancies are building armies of people who can ship Claude projects, and in a year's time a lot of the "AI implementation" work small firms are picking up will be bid for by Deloitte with a residency certificate. The second is opportunity. Those armies are pointed at banks and pharma companies. They are not pointed at a rugby club treasurer or a charity raffle. The bottom of the market stays wide open, and the person who already knows how a grassroots club works has an edge no residency will teach. Remember Barclays from yesterday's episode. That is the customer this academy is built for. You are not competing for that customer, and you should not try.

Second, Lovable. On Thursday the first of October, Lovable's changelog added three things. The one that matters is called GPT Live. It puts live voice conversation into apps you build on Lovable. Your users can talk to the app, interrupt it mid-sentence, and have it act on what they said. You describe the conversation you want, say a voice tutor or a booking assistant, and Lovable wires it up. The catch, and there is always a catch, is that it needs a specific framework called TanStack Start, and it burns workspace credits. The changelog did not say which languages it supports.

The other two changes are for bigger customers. EU inference, on the Enterprise plan only, runs AI requests through European providers, both while you build and inside the published app. It costs more credits and some models are not available. And Business and Enterprise admins can now lock an API key to an approved list of IP addresses.

Why you care. Voice used to be the part of an app you paid a developer for. Now it is a sentence in a prompt box. That changes what a no-code build can be. A club app that answers "what time is kick-off on Sunday" out loud is now a weekend job, not a quote. The EU inference option also gives you a cleaner answer when a charity trustee asks where their supporters' data goes, though only if you are paying Enterprise money.

Third, a housekeeping item that will bite someone. On Friday the second, GitHub retired four models from Copilot. Gemini three point five Flash and three point six Flash are replaced by Gemini three point eight Flash. Kimi K two point seven Code is replaced by Kimi K three. And Claude Opus four point seven is replaced by Claude Opus five point five. That applies across chat, inline edits, agent mode and code completions. Enterprise admins have to switch the replacements on in their policy settings, so if nobody does, the team loses the model they were using.

Why you care. Pair this with the Claude Sonnet four point five retirement we covered on Thursday, which lands on the thirtieth of November. Every automation you run is pinned to a model somewhere, and models now have shelf lives measured in months. Ten minutes this week to list which model each of your scripts and tools calls is cheaper than finding out from an error message at nine o'clock on a draw night.

While we are on developer tools, a quick one. Claude Code version two point one point two eight eight, also out on Friday, now compacts a long conversation automatically instead of falling over with a "prompt is too long" error, and unattended runs carry on from a partial answer after an API timeout. Small, but it is exactly the sort of fix that stops overnight jobs dying at three in the morning.

Fourth, security. Microsoft published its Digital Defense Report for twenty twenty-six. The page shows it last updated on Friday the second. Some numbers from it, which I checked against Microsoft's own page. Microsoft processes more than a hundred and sixty-five trillion security signals a day. It screens five point two billion emails a day. Eighty-eight per cent of enterprises are experimenting with AI agents, and Microsoft projects around one point three billion agents deployed by twenty twenty-eight. The UK had the highest observed event counts in its regional category.

The line to remember is this one. Malicious browser extensions harvested AI chat histories from more than six hundred thousand installs. That is people's conversations with ChatGPT and Claude, scooped up by an add-on they installed for something else entirely. A news roundup also reported that the time from a vulnerability being found to it being weaponised has dropped below twenty-four hours, but I could not find that figure in the report itself, so treat it as unconfirmed.

Why you care. Everything you paste into an AI chat, a supporter list, a draw spreadsheet, a club's bank details, sits in a browser tab. Any extension that can read that tab can read that data. The fix costs nothing. Open your browser's extensions page, and remove everything you cannot name a reason for. Do the same on whichever laptop runs PlayFundWin's admin.

Fifth, and slightly outside the window, because it slipped past us earlier in the week. On Wednesday the thirtieth of September, the voice AI company Inworld announced it has acquired Ultravox, a platform people use to build phone and voice agents. I read Inworld's own post. Existing Ultravox customers do not need to migrate or change code, their voice IDs keep working, and built-in voices get upgraded to Inworld's newer text-to-speech model at no extra cost. No price was disclosed.

Why you care. Voice agents are consolidating. If you build a phone answering service on a small vendor, ask the boring question up front. What happens if they get bought? This time the answer is a free upgrade. Next time it might be a sunset notice with ninety days on the clock.

That is the news. Now for the part where somebody is actually making money.

Today's build is old photo colourisation and restoration. Take a faded black-and-white photograph, upload it, and AI adds natural colour, removes scratches, tears and stains, and sharpens it up. It sounds like a party trick. It turns out to be a business.

The evidence. The product is called colorize dot cc. The founder is Alexander Kozhevin, who posts as Alex Polymath, and the site says he created it in twenty eighteen. TrustMRR, the verified revenue directory, lists the business in Thailand, so this is not a UK operation. TrustMRR pulls its figures straight from Stripe, and the page was updated on Friday the second of October. The page says this. Revenue over the last thirty days, five thousand four hundred and sixty-eight dollars. All-time revenue, a hundred and thirteen thousand and fifty-six dollars. Monthly recurring revenue, zero, because there are no subscriptions. People buy packs of credits. Growth month on month was shown at around thirteen to fifteen per cent; the page refreshed between two reads and the number moved.

Two caveats before you get excited. First, TrustMRR says the business was founded in December twenty twenty-two, while the site says twenty eighteen, so the Stripe total may only cover part of its life. Second, the site claims more than five hundred thousand users and three million photos processed, but its own enterprise page says two hundred thousand photos. One of those pages is out of date, and I would not quote either user number with a straight face.

The pricing is simple and clever. Credit packs that last a year. Fifty credits for twelve dollars. Two hundred and fifty for forty-nine dollars. Twelve hundred and fifty for a hundred and ninety-nine dollars, aimed at studios. A photo costs between one and six credits depending on which AI model you use. And there is a free, low-quality preview, which is the whole sales funnel. You see your grandmother in colour, you want the sharp version, you pay twelve dollars. The emotional hook does the selling.

Now the money, done on the back of an envelope. Five and a half thousand dollars a month in revenue is roughly a hundred and ten of the forty-nine-dollar packs, if everybody bought that one. That is my sum, not a published figure, and real buyers will be a mix. It is gross revenue, before the cost of running the AI models. No margin is published. So this is a solid one-person income, not a fortune, and it has taken years to get there.

Now the counter-evidence, because this is not an empty field. There are free tools. There is an open-source restore project on GitHub. MyHeritage has a colouriser. Remini does photo enhancement on your phone. Photoshop has a neural filter for it. Palette and Hotpot do the same. Any general image AI will have a go if you ask. I could not find a named founder who failed with this exact model, but the risk is obvious. The model is a commodity. Anybody can wire up colourisation in an afternoon. If you build a generic colouriser and put it on the internet, you are the six hundred and first one, and you will lose.

So the value cannot be the model. It has to be the niche, the service and the physical object at the end. Which brings us to you.

So, the version for Steve. Call it the Club Heritage Wall.

Every grassroots club has a box. A cardboard box, usually in a cupboard behind the bar, full of old team photos. The nineteen sixty-three cup-winning side. The first women's team. The year the clubhouse was built by members with their own hands. Those photos are black and white, creased, and nobody under fifty has looked at them in decades.

Clubs care about this more than almost anything, especially around centenaries, anniversaries and presentation nights. And this is the edge most colourisation tools do not have. The AI guesses colours. It does not know that your club plays in claret and sky blue. The club does. So the heritage version asks for the kit colours, the club colours, the colour of the old stand, and feeds them into the restoration. A generic tool gives you a nice guess. Yours gives the club its own colours back. That is the difference between a novelty and something a committee will pay for.

How you would build it, in rough order. Step one, a simple upload page. Lovable or Base44 would do it in an afternoon. The club secretary uploads a batch of scans and fills in a short form: kit colours, rough decade, anything known about the photo. Step two, hosted restoration and colourisation models through an API, Replicate or fal are the usual places, called with those details in the prompt. Step three, a human check. Every image gets looked at by a person before it goes anywhere, because AI will occasionally give a goalkeeper three hands or turn a pint of bitter green. Step four, a private gallery page per club, which could live on LeaguePages. Step five, a print-on-demand partner for framed prints and canvases, with Stripe on the front. All of that comes in well under five hundred pounds to start, most of it in model credits and a print sample or two.

Label everything clearly as colourised by AI, with the colours as an interpretation, not a historical record. Club historians are a passionate group, and you want them on your side, not writing to the local paper.

Now the club-side money, and this is illustration, not evidence. Assumptions to check, not facts. Say a club pays a hundred and forty-nine pounds for a heritage pack, thirty photos restored, gallery included. Ten clubs is roughly fifteen hundred pounds. Then the prints. Say a framed A three print sells for twenty-five pounds, and the print and frame cost you somewhere around ten, which you would need to confirm with a real print partner. Forty prints sold at a centenary dinner leaves about six hundred pounds of margin, which you split with the club. The club makes money from its own history. You make money from the club. Nobody is asking a supporter for a donation; they are buying a picture of their dad.

And the PlayFundWin angle writes itself. A framed, colourised photo of a legendary team is a cheap prize with enormous emotional value. Put one up as a draw prize and you have a prize that costs you about ten pounds to produce and that every older member of the club wants on their wall.

The risks, without the varnish. One, copyright. Old team photos were often taken by a local newspaper or a professional photographer, and copyright can last a long time. Before selling prints, the club needs to own the photo, or the rights holder needs to agree, or the photo needs to be old enough to be out of copyright. Build a simple "who took this and do we have permission" checkbox into the upload form and do not skip it. Two, people. Some of the players in those photos are alive and some have families who might feel strongly. Selling a print of a named individual for profit is a different thing from hanging it in the clubhouse. Start with team photos, ask before selling anything that features one person, and steer well clear of recent junior team photos. Three, quality. AI colourisation gets skin tones and old fabrics wrong sometimes. The human check is not optional. Four, the free competition. If a club secretary can do it for nothing on their phone, why pay you? Because they will not. They do not have time to do thirty photos, fix the colours, build the gallery and organise the printing. You are selling the afternoon they do not have.

Right. How does today's news land on the three businesses?

PlayFundWin first, and I owe you a correction. On yesterday's episode I said Flutter's Cash4Clubs fund was something any club could stack alongside a draw. That was wrong. I have now read Sported's own page in full. It confirms the fund opened at nine in the morning on Thursday the first of October and closes at five in the afternoon on Friday the first of January twenty twenty-seven. So it is a much longer window than the December date one trade site gave. But one line changes everything. Groups must only support adult members, eighteen and over. If a club works with anyone under eighteen, it cannot apply, even if the money would only go to the adults. That rules out most junior and mixed-age grassroots clubs, which is a big chunk of PlayFundWin's audience. The two thousand pound grants and the five ten thousand pound grants are real, but only point the walking football section, the veterans' cricket side or the adults-only club at it. Not the under-twelves.

Second, the giving picture. Civil Society reported on Thursday that the Charities Aid Foundation distributed a record one point four three billion pounds in twenty twenty-five to twenty-six, up from one point two billion. The same article gives the wider context, and it is not pretty. UK giving fell ten per cent to fourteen billion pounds in twenty twenty-five, and only fifty-five per cent of adults gave money or sponsored someone, down from sixty-nine per cent in twenty sixteen. My reading, not the article's: fewer people are giving for nothing. A draw, where the supporter gets a chance at something back, is a different ask from a straight donation, and that difference matters more when the pool of habitual givers is shrinking. Treat that as an argument for the pitch, not as evidence about draws.

Third, the security point from the Microsoft report applies directly. PlayFundWin handles supporter details and payment flows. Ask whoever has admin access to strip their browser extensions back to the essentials. It is the cheapest risk reduction you will do all month.

LeaguePages next, and there is a proper opening here. On Thursday the first of October, the FA published its grassroots behaviour and discipline report for twenty twenty-five to twenty-six. It covers more than one point three million grassroots fixtures. Ninety point five per cent of matches were rated good on respect marks. Serious misconduct happened in fewer than nought point nought nine per cent of fixtures. Registered referees hit a record forty-one and a half thousand, with eighty-two per cent staying on. Serious offences against officials fell. The worrying number: discrimination reports rose, and the minimum ban for a first discrimination offence goes up from an average of seven matches to ten this season.

The part LeaguePages should care about is the admin. For twenty twenty-six to twenty-seven, the FA is bringing in mandatory fines and education, so-called Positivity Champions, national cooling-off periods, and monthly publication of disciplinary sanctions. Monthly publication means somebody at every league has to produce and post that list, every month, accurately, without naming the wrong person. That is a job begging for a tool. A sanctions page template on LeaguePages, fed from a simple form, with a human sign-off before anything goes live, would save league secretaries real time. Check the exact FA requirements before building, because the full report is a large document and I only have the summary page.

And the heritage idea fits LeaguePages naturally. Every club page could have a history tab. Restored photos make it worth visiting.

Lovable's voice feature is the other LeaguePages thought. A "what time is my match" voice assistant on a league site is now a small build rather than a project. Keep it to fixtures and venues, and keep it away from anything to do with discipline or children.

EV-Partnerships. Nothing new on the charging front since yesterday's Sunderland and Marston's stories; the trade press was quiet and the government statistics have not moved since late August. So rather than invent news, one practical link to today's build. The community-hub pitch depends on dwell time: people staying at the club long enough for the charger to earn its keep. A wall of colourised club history in the bar is exactly the sort of thing people stop and look at while they wait. It will not sell a charger on its own, but it helps make the clubhouse a place worth sitting in, which is the whole thesis. Meridian FC would be a natural first test, if they have a box of old photos behind the bar.

Your one thing for today. Thirty minutes, no code, and very Saturday-friendly.

Ask one partner club for a single old team photo. An adult team, a photo the club owns, ideally from before nineteen eighty. Snap it on your phone or scan it. Then, two options. Run it through a free colourisation preview on one of the tools I mentioned, or give it to Claude with the club's kit colours and ask how it would restore it, then try a hosted model. Either way, get one decent colour version.

Then send the before and after to the club secretary with one question. "If we did thirty of these and sold framed prints at your next big night, would members buy them?" Do not mention a price. Watch for the reaction. If they forward it to three committee members within the hour, you have your answer.
