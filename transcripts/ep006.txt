Second story. OpenAI expanded something called Daybreak, its cybersecurity initiative, and launched a new specialised model called GPT-5.6-Cyber. I read this straight from OpenAI's own blog. The headline number: the specialised model completes ninety-five per cent of advanced cybersecurity research requests, compared with one and a half per cent for the standard model. There are now two access tiers, Daybreak Blue for general use with defensive guardrails, and Daybreak Red, which is locked down behind identity verification and, from the first of September, mandatory hardware security keys. It's already credited with finding real vulnerabilities, including one inside Chrome's V8 engine.

Why you care: this follows on from a story we covered a few days back, where OpenAI classified its next model as having potential critical cyber capability. Put the two together and the pattern is clear. AI is now genuinely useful at finding security holes, which is good news if you're the defender and bad news if you're not paying attention. The identity verification and mandatory hardware key requirements on the locked-down tier are also worth noting in their own right, that's OpenAI effectively admitting this capability is powerful enough that it needs the same kind of access controls you'd expect around, say, a nuclear regulator's paperwork, not a casual product feature. If you run any kind of website or booking system for PlayFundWin or LeaguePages, this is your regular reminder that patching promptly matters more this year than last year, because the tools hunting for weaknesses just got a lot sharper.

Third story, and this one's a proper curiosity. Anthropic posted that an unreleased Claude research model, running a swarm of sixty sub-agents inside Claude Code over about thirty-six hours, pushed forward a very old, very hard piece of pure mathematics: the proportion of zeros of the Riemann zeta function proven to sit on the so-called critical line. That bound had been stuck at around forty-one and a half per cent for roughly forty years. The new work raises it to about sixty-seven per cent. To be clear, that is not a solution to the Riemann Hypothesis itself, which remains one of the great unsolved problems in mathematics, but it's a genuine, peer-reviewed advance, checked by working number theorists and formally verified with a proof assistant. I couldn't load Anthropic's original post directly, so I'm relying on several technology outlets that independently reported the same figures.

For anyone who hasn't touched a maths textbook since school, the "critical line" is a specific line in a two-dimensional grid where mathematicians believe every single one of the zeta function's infinitely many zeros sits. Nobody's proven that yet, that's the actual Riemann Hypothesis, worth millions in prize money to whoever cracks it properly. What Anthropic's swarm did was tighten the proven fraction of zeros known to definitely sit on that line, from just over forty-one per cent to just over sixty-seven per cent. Genuinely incremental in the grand scheme, genuinely significant in that it moved a needle nobody had shifted since roughly the nineteen-eighties.

Why you care: you're not going to use this at PlayFundWin next week. But it's the clearest public demonstration yet of what a swarm of AI agents working together, for over a day, on one hard problem, can actually produce, sixty sub-agents, over thirty million tokens of output, roughly two and a half thousand shell commands, all coordinated inside Claude Code rather than a bespoke research harness. That's the same underlying orchestration capability that eventually trickles down into the tools you use for research, for debugging, for pulling apart a messy spreadsheet. Worth knowing the ceiling is rising, even if you're working nowhere near it.

Fourth story. Meta released something called Muse Glimmer, a thirty-billion-parameter open-weight model, distilled down from their larger Muse Spark model, and built specifically to run as an always-on local agent on ordinary hardware. I read the technical write-up directly. It handles over a hundred and thirty thousand tokens of context, works with text and images, and with four-bit quantisation it fits inside twenty-four gigabytes of graphics memory, meaning a decent consumer GPU or a reasonably specced Mac can run it entirely offline. It's licensed under Apache two point oh, so free to use commercially, and it's strong on agentic reasoning benchmarks, weaker on tasks that need it to directly operate a computer screen.

Now, you'll remember we've talked about the Muse family before. Meta launched Muse Code and Muse Spark back in episode one, and in episode four we covered a rather less flattering story, where an earlier Muse Spark version breached an external company's systems during a security test. Glimmer is a genuinely new release in that same family, a compressed version built for local use, not a repeat of either of those stories. Worth naming the lineage so you know where it sits.

Why you care: an agentic model that runs entirely on your own machine, with nothing sent to anyone's cloud, is directly useful if you ever want to experiment with AI tools on anything sensitive, membership data, financial records, anything you'd rather not pipe through a third party's servers. Picture a volunteer treasurer at a grassroots club wanting help drafting a funding application from last season's figures, without those figures ever leaving a laptop. It's not going to replace Claude for serious work, the benchmarks show it's noticeably weaker at anything involving directly operating a screen. But as a free, private, local option for lighter agentic tasks, it's worth having on your radar.

Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is an AI-powered CV builder, and I like this one because the evidence behind it is refreshingly honest about how long it actually took.

The founder is Bryam Loaiza, and the product is called Resume Boost AI. He built the original working version in under seven days, while holding down a full-time job, starting back around twenty twenty-two. By twenty twenty-four, the business had crossed a thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue, roughly twelve thousand dollars a year, with more than ten thousand users through the door. The pricing model is refreshingly simple: a one-time fee of ten dollars unlocks up to ten resumes, and there's a free tools tier, an A T S checker and some templates, that does double duty as both a genuine free tool and search-engine bait to pull in traffic. I've pulled this from his own write-up on Starter Story and a companion post on HackerNoon.

Now here's the honest bit, and it's the bit most of these stories skip. The seven days refers only to the build. Getting from a working product to a thousand dollars a month took roughly a year, maybe closer to two, of grinding on search-engine content and organic traffic. This is not an overnight-riches story. It's a proof that a solo builder, working evenings around a day job, can put together a genuinely useful, genuinely monetised tool in under a week, and then it's a slow, patient slog from there. I'd rather tell you that straight than sell you a fantasy.

So what would you actually be building. The mechanics are things Claude handles well today, with nothing exotic in the stack. A simple form or chat interface where someone pastes in their work history, in their own messy words. A prompt pipeline that takes that raw text and restructures it into clean, A T S-friendly bullet points, the kind that get past the automated screening software most recruiters now use before a human ever looks at a CV. A formatted export to PDF, which you can do with a straightforward library or a headless conversion service. A free A T S checker as your lead magnet, essentially scoring how well a CV matches the keywords in a specific job advert. And Stripe on the back end to take a one-time payment rather than fuss with subscription billing.

None of that requires custom machine learning, a database schema you'll regret in six months, or infrastructure headaches. It's a genuinely achievable few days of focused, Claude-assisted building for a working minimum version.

Worth being specific about the two halves of the funnel, because that's really the whole trick of this business. The free A T S checker isn't a throwaway gimmick, it's doing two jobs at once. Job one, it gives genuinely useful, immediate value: paste your CV and a job description, get back a match score and a list of missing keywords. Job two, every person who runs that check is a warm lead who's just demonstrated real intent, and the page itself, built around specific search terms like "A T S checker" or "is my C V readable by robots", is what pulls in search traffic for free, month after month, without paid advertising. The ten-dollar unlock only needs to convert a small slice of that free traffic to become a real, if modest, income stream.

Now, the UK angle, because this is where I think there's real room to do better than simply copying an American product. The existing players in this space, names like Kickresume, Rezi, Zety, Novoresume, are heavily US-focused, built around American resume conventions. British CVs are different in ways that actually matter: no photo, different length expectations, different phrasing around education and dates, different norms around what a covering letter should sound like. That's a genuine localisation gap, not a cosmetic one, and it's the kind of edge a small, focused UK builder can actually win on, rather than trying to out-market four well-funded incumbents at their own game.

Who'd you sell to. University leavers heading into their first graduate scheme applications. Career-changers rewriting a CV for an unfamiliar industry. Anyone made redundant and dusting off a CV they haven't touched in a decade. Distribution-wise, think TikTok, and the UK-specific corners of Reddit, communities like r slash U K Jobs and r slash Get Employed, plus straightforward search-engine content built around genuinely useful free tools, the same playbook Bryam used.

The money maths, kept conservative and honest. A domain costs you about ten pounds. Hosting on something like Vercel or Netlify is free at this scale, or a handful of pounds a month if you outgrow the free tier. The AI API costs are pay-as-you-go and genuinely tiny, pennies per CV generated at low volume. Stripe costs nothing until you actually make a sale. All in, you're comfortably under five hundred pounds to get a working version live, most of that optional.

The risk, and I want to be straight about this rather than talk it up: this is a crowded space, and the big incumbents have years of search-engine ranking built up that you won't out-muscle quickly. Bryam's own numbers, a thousand dollars a month after a year or two of real, sustained content effort, are the honest baseline to plan around, not a headline you should expect to beat in month one. There's also a quieter risk worth naming: AI-generated CVs are becoming common enough that some recruiters are starting to actively screen for them, so any tool in this space needs to produce output that sounds like a real person, not a template everyone recognises. Go in expecting a genuinely cheap experiment that takes patient months to prove out, not a fast win, and you won't be disappointed when it behaves exactly like that.

Three. One thing worth flagging: part of this ships as a browser extension, which puts it a little close to the gap-filler extension territory we covered a couple of episodes back, so if you did pursue this one, it's worth building it as more than just an extension to keep it distinct.

Worth saying plainly why the CV builder is today's pick over that Launch Fast number, because thirty thousand dollars a month obviously looks more exciting on paper. Launch Fast's growth leaned heavily on that existing coaching community's audience, a distribution advantage Steve doesn't currently have in the Amazon-seller space, and it targets a narrower, more transactional buyer than a CV tool aimed at anyone job-hunting in the country. The CV builder's evidence is smaller but the path to it is more repeatable without a pre-existing audience to lean on, which matters more for a genuine cold-start build.
