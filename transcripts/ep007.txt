Part one. What shipped.

First, and this one's confirmed, I read the release notes myself. Anthropic extended its Compliance API so it now covers Claude Cowork and Claude Code sessions running locally, on your own desktop or laptop, not just server-side API calls. That landed on the eleventh of August. Practically, it means an organisation on Claude Enterprise can now pull a unified transcript and metadata from those local sessions through a new set of endpoints, using the compliance access keys they already hold. It doesn't reach Claude Code running in a browser, or sessions through Bedrock, Vertex or Foundry, but for anyone doing real work inside Cowork or the Claude Code command line, there is now an audit trail a compliance or legal team can actually pull, rather than a shrug and a screenshot.

Why you care: if any part of your business runs through Claude, and if you're listening to this it probably does, this is the difference between "we think an AI touched that file" and "here's the record." I'll come back to this one in Apply It, because it lands directly on PlayFundWin.

Worth sitting with for a second, because it's easy to skim past a compliance feature as boring plumbing. If you're a small operator handling anyone's personal data, supporters, donors, players, parents, "boring plumbing" is exactly what a regulator or an insurer wants to see when something goes wrong. This kind of feature usually shows up first for enterprise customers with big compliance teams, and trickles down later. The fact that it's arriving now, aimed at exactly the kind of local, on-device work smaller teams actually do, tells you Anthropic is building for the long tail of businesses, not just the logos on their homepage.

Second, also confirmed, straight from Anthropic's own release notes. The introductory pricing for Claude Sonnet five, two dollars per million input tokens, ten dollars per million output tokens, was due to jump to three and fifteen on the first of September. Anthropic has now made the introductory price permanent instead. No hike coming.

Why you care: if you've built anything on Sonnet five, an internal tool, a customer-facing feature, anything, your unit economics just got locked in at the cheaper rate indefinitely. That's one less line item to worry about when you're doing your own conservative money maths later in this episode.

It's also a small but telling signal about where the model providers think the real competition is. Cancelling a scheduled price rise instead of quietly letting it happen is a company choosing predictability over squeezing a bit more margin out of existing customers. For anyone building a small AI-powered product on top of someone else's model, that kind of stability matters more than it sounds like it should, because the single biggest risk to a thin-margin subscription business is your own costs moving under your feet without warning.

Third. Yelp's reservation and waitlist tools are now callable directly inside ChatGPT, across every plan except the enterprise Work tier, on mobile, web and desktop. Ask ChatGPT to book a table and it can pull live availability from OpenTable, Resy and Yelp itself, then complete the booking without you leaving the chat window. I read Yelp's own announcement of this one myself, so that's confirmed too.

Why you care: this is agentic commerce showing up somewhere ordinary people already are, not a demo at a developer conference. Any local, bookable business, a club running pay-and-play sessions, a venue that takes reservations, anyone with a diary, is a candidate for the same treatment before too long. Worth keeping an eye on whether that kind of booking integration extends into sports and leisure platforms next, because that's closer to home than it looks.

The mechanism matters here too. This isn't OpenAI scraping Yelp's listings and guessing at availability, it's a proper integration pulling live data from the actual booking systems, which means the information ChatGPT gives you is as accurate as what you'd get on Yelp's own app. That's the bar any small business now needs to clear if it wants to show up correctly when a customer asks an AI assistant to sort something out on their behalf, rather than typing it into a search box themselves. If your booking system doesn't expose clean, structured availability data somewhere, you're invisible to this entire layer of commerce, no matter how good your actual service is.

Fourth, and I want to flag the sourcing on this one honestly. I have it from secondary reporting, not Google's own blog post directly, because that page returned a dead link when I tried to load it myself. With that caveat: Gemini has reportedly crossed one billion monthly active users, up from nine hundred and fifty million just two weeks earlier, apparently the fastest product to hit that number in Google's history. The same reporting says sixty-three per cent of Gemini users now talk to it by voice rather than type, with dozens more regional dialects and wider Android task automation said to be coming.

Why you care: even allowing for some scepticism on an unconfirmed growth figure, the direction isn't in doubt. Voice is turning into the default way people talk to AI, not a novelty feature bolted onto the side. If any part of your customer journey is still text-only, that gap is going to feel dated faster than you'd expect.

Put the number in context. A billion monthly users puts Gemini in the same rough territory as WhatsApp and Instagram, which is a genuinely strange thing to type about a product that most people were still calling "the also-ran assistant" a year or two ago. Whatever you make of Google's underlying strategy, that kind of reach means whatever interface habits Gemini trains into a billion people, voice-first, conversational, assume-the-context-is-remembered, become the baseline expectation your own customers quietly start bringing to every other product they touch, including yours.

That's what shipped. Two stories I read straight from the company's own release notes, one from Yelp's own announcement, and one number I'm taking on secondary authority because Google's page wouldn't load for me. I'd rather tell you that than pretend I checked something I didn't.

Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is an AI mockup generator for merch and print-on-demand sellers, and I want to start with the evidence because that's the whole point of this segment.

The founder is Pranay Agarwal, working with co-founder Mofid Ansari, and the product is called Mockey. He wrote about it himself on Indie Hackers, and a separate case-study writeup corroborates the numbers: twelve thousand dollars a month in subscription revenue, roughly eleven months after they switched on paid plans in April of last year, plus around seven hundred dollars a month from advertising, and about a hundred thousand monthly visitors to the site. It's freemium, three free mockups a day, then seven dollars a month or a one hundred and ninety-nine dollar lifetime deal. Bootstrapped since twenty twenty-two, no outside investment. I'll flag the data honestly: it's about seventeen months old at this point, so treat it as a proven pattern, not a fresh case, but the pattern itself is solid and the category is one this show hasn't touched before.

What it actually does is simple to describe and genuinely useful. You upload a logo or a design, and it places that design realistically onto photos of real products, t-shirts, hoodies, mugs, tote bags, so you get professional-looking product photography for your online shop without hiring a photographer or owning any stock. If you've ever tried to sell merch and given up because the product photos looked amateurish, this is the tool that fixes exactly that problem.

The reason this category works as a business at all is that print-on-demand and merch selling has exploded over the last few years, but the actual production side, printing a hoodie, shipping a mug, has been commoditised down to almost nothing by companies like Printful and Printify. The bit that's still hard, and still visibly bad on most small sellers' shops, is making the product look like something you'd actually want to buy. Mockey didn't invent print-on-demand, it found the one unglamorous, badly-served step in an already-large market and built a tool that does just that step, well. That's a pattern worth noticing on its own, independent of the specific numbers.

Here's how you'd build something in the same category, and this is about ninety per cent doable with Claude doing the heavy lifting. Start with a straightforward web app, Claude can scaffold the whole thing, a simple frontend, a database for user accounts and uploads, and hosting that doesn't cost you anything until you have real traffic. Next you need a library of blank product photos to place designs onto, t-shirts, hoodies, water bottles, tote bags, either sourced cheaply or generated with an image model. Then you need the actual placement logic, an image API can do realistic perspective warping and lighting so a flat logo looks like it's actually printed on the fabric, rather than just pasted flat and obviously fake. Add Stripe for the subscription billing and a daily free-use limit to drive conversions, and you have a working product. None of that requires you to be a computer vision engineer. It requires you to be patient enough to iterate on the bit that actually matters, which is making the mockups look convincing.

Now the honest money maths, and I mean honest, not the version that makes it sound easy. To get something live, you're looking at hosting costs that are close to nothing at low volume, an image-generation API charging fractions of a penny per mockup, Stripe's standard fees, and a starter set of product template photos that might cost you somewhere between fifty and one hundred and fifty pounds if you don't generate them yourself. Call it two hundred to four hundred pounds to get a working version live, comfortably under the five-hundred-pound ceiling this segment works to. The revenue side is the slow part. Mockey took the best part of a year of consistent effort to reach twelve thousand dollars a month, and that's after they'd already built an audience through free template packs and organic search. A realistic first target for someone starting from nothing is a handful of paying customers at five to seven pounds a month within three to six months of steady content and outreach work, not a headline number by Friday. If anyone tells you this kind of thing is an overnight win, they haven't built one.

The risks are worth naming plainly too. This is a crowded space, Placeit and Smartmockups have been doing versions of this for years, Canva has mockup templates built in, and Mockey itself is now a real competitor rather than a gap in the market. The price point is low, so you need real volume to make meaningful money, and cheap freemium products tend to churn hard, people sign up, get their one mockup, and leave. The mockups themselves need to actually look good, fabric folds, shadows and lighting are where AI-generated placements fall apart if you don't put real care into that part. And there's a distribution problem underneath all of it, a beautifully built tool nobody's heard of makes nothing, so whatever time you don't spend building, you need to spend finding the first hundred people who actually need this.

Here's where Steve's existing position gives a genuine edge rather than a theoretical one. PlayFundWin already has trusted relationships with sports clubs and charities, and a huge number of those clubs sell kit, hoodies and merchandise to raise money, usually with photography that looks like it was taken on someone's kitchen table, because it probably was. A version of this tool aimed squarely at club treasurers and volunteer fundraisers, instant, professional-looking merch mockups, no design skills required, sidesteps a head-on price war with Mockey and Placeit entirely, because you're not fighting for cold organic traffic, you're reaching an audience you can already talk to directly.

Think about the actual wedge product. A club running a prize draw or a fundraiser through PlayFundWin already has a logo and a cause, what it doesn't have is a design team. Offer it a free set of merch mockups the moment it sets up a campaign, a hoodie, a mug, a tote bag with its own badge on it, and you've turned a nice add-on into a reason the club upgrades to a paid tier, or simply a reason it tells the next club down the road to use PlayFundWin instead of a rival. That's a genuinely different acquisition channel to what Mockey has, which is cold organic search and template give-aways. You'd be starting with warm distribution most bootstrapped founders spend years trying to build.

Three runner-ups, briefly, and why none of them got the deep dive. Sebastian Volkis built TrendFeed, a tool that spots trending articles and auto-generates short video content from them, documented in his own Indie Hackers post, seven thousand one hundred dollars on launch day and twelve thousand dollars in the first four weeks. Genuinely impressive, but that's launch-day spike revenue with no proof of what happens after the initial wave, and it edges into content-repurposing territory that's a less clean fit for a subscription business. Yasser Elsaid's Chatbase, an AI chatbot and customer-support widget builder, is sitting around three hundred and ninety thousand dollars a month by his own account on social media, but that's a company-scale business now, competing with Intercom's Fin and Crisp, not something you're realistically replicating as a days-long side project under five hundred pounds. And Jan Hammer's 3D AI Studio, a text-to-three-D-model generator, reportedly reached three hundred thousand dollars a year within six months according to a secondary blog syndicating his original story, but three-D generation leans on more specialised and less stable APIs than flat image placement, and the sourcing on that one is thinner than I'd like for a deep dive.

What all four of these have in common, worth pulling out explicitly, is that none of them started as a technically novel idea. Trend-spotting tools, chatbot builders, three-D generators and mockup placement have all existed for years. What each founder actually did was take an unglamorous, specific, already-proven need and build a fast, cheap, AI-powered version of it before anyone else bothered. That's a far more repeatable playbook than waiting around for a genuinely new idea nobody's had yet, and it's the reason this segment keeps finding fresh evidence every single week without running out of categories to cover.

Part three. Apply it.

Starting with PlayFundWin, and this one connects directly to today's top story. The new Compliance API coverage for Cowork and Claude Code sessions is worth a real look if PlayFundWin's team is using Claude anywhere near payment processing or supporter data, which, given the nature of the business, it almost certainly is. Turning that logging on costs you very little and gives you something concrete to point to if a payment processor, an auditor, or a worried trustee ever asks how AI tools are being used around sensitive data. This also picks up a thread from Ep six, when the one action was to check when your payment and supporter-data tooling was last security-reviewed, this is effectively the next step in that same conversation, and it's a cleaner answer than anything you'd have had available even a week ago.

It's also worth thinking about this alongside the still-open Fundraising Regulator thread, which I'll get to properly in a moment. Whatever the eventual ruling on minimum contributions looks like, a regulator paying closer attention to the prize-draw sector is a regulator more likely to ask operators how they handle supporter data generally, not just contribution percentages. Being able to answer that question calmly, with an actual audit trail, rather than scrambling for one, is worth having in place well before anyone asks.

For LeaguePages, two things from today's news line up naturally with what the platform actually does. The mockup-generator build idea isn't just a hypothetical side project, it's a genuine feature LeaguePages could offer grassroots clubs directly, professional merch mockups as part of the club-website toolkit, which turns a nice-to-have into a reason for a club to upgrade from the free tier. Separately, if voice really is becoming the default way people interact with Gemini for hundreds of millions of users, it's worth asking whether parents and coaches checking fixtures and results on a league site would rather just ask than tap through menus, particularly on a phone, standing on a touchline, in the rain.

Neither of those is a today problem, to be clear. But it's worth logging both as things to prototype cheaply rather than build properly, a rough merch-mockup feature behind a single club as a test, a simple voice-friendly results lookup as a weekend experiment, before committing real engineering time. The value of watching a platform cross a billion users isn't the number itself, it's noticing early which interface habits are about to become expected everywhere, including on a grassroots league site nobody would normally think of as cutting edge.

For EV-Partnerships, I want to be careful here rather than overreach. The OpenAI and Yelp reservations story is an early sign that AI agents booking real-world services is moving from novelty to normal, and in time that could plausibly extend to finding and booking a charging session at a venue. But EV-Partnerships doesn't own or operate any chargers itself, it's a lead-generation gateway connecting venues to installers, and there are zero units actually installed as of today. So the honest framing is: this is a trend worth watching for the eventual installer partners, not something EV-Partnerships itself is positioned to benefit from yet. Getting ahead of that story before there's anything installed to talk about would be getting ahead of yourself.

Where it is genuinely useful, though, is as a talking point with prospective installer partners. If you're pitching a charge-point operator on why a sports venue is worth their capital, "AI assistants are already booking real-world reservations for people, and charging availability is the obvious next category" is a forward-looking argument worth having in your back pocket, even with zero chargers on the ground today. Just keep it framed as market context for the pitch, not as a claim about anything EV-Partnerships itself has built or operates.
