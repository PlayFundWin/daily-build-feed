Today's deep dive is a document-chat tool, but built narrow, not broad, and I want to explain why that distinction is the entire pitch. The generic version of this, upload any PDF and chat with it, is basically a solved, commoditised problem now; ChatGPT and Claude both do it natively inside their own apps, so nobody is paying you a subscription for that anymore, full stop. The opportunity left on the table is picking one paperwork-heavy niche you actually understand from the inside and building a tool that only does that one job, done properly, for people who would genuinely rather pay a small monthly fee than read forty pages of small print themselves every time a question comes up.

Here's the evidence, and I want to be upfront about its age before I get into the numbers. Damon Chen built a product called PDF dot AI, and posted his own figures on Indie Hackers back in October twenty twenty-three: twenty-five thousand and seventy dollars in monthly recurring revenue, seventeen thousand six hundred dollars of that as profit in a single month, and the product had passed three hundred and fifty thousand users within four months of launch. He followed that up on LinkedIn in November of the same year, saying the product had crossed sixty thousand dollars in revenue for that month alone. A Starter Story interview published in October last year confirmed he'd scaled it to roughly sixty thousand dollars a month, somewhere near one and a half million dollars a year, after acquiring the underlying product for a modest sum and recouping that acquisition cost within six days of relaunching it on Product Hunt. So yes, this is two to three year old data, and I'll say it plainly on air rather than gloss past it: the generic chat-with-any-PDF space is more crowded now precisely because the big labs ate the generic version whole. That's exactly why niching down hard is the actual pitch here, not cloning what Chen did back in twenty twenty-three.

Here's what niching it down actually means for you specifically, because a generic pep talk isn't much use. You already sit inside three paperwork-heavy worlds without having to go looking for one. Fundraising compliance documents: grant agreements, funding terms and conditions, the paperwork a charity or club has to wade through and understand correctly before they can take your product live with confidence. Grassroots sports club admin: safeguarding policies, affiliation paperwork, league regulations that unpaid volunteers are expected to read and correctly apply in their spare time, usually under deadline pressure. And EV charging wayleave and connection agreements: the dense legal documents that sit between a landowner and a charge point actually going into the ground, full of clauses that matter enormously and get skim-read anyway. Pick one of the three. Build a tool that lets someone upload their pile of documents and ask it plain, ordinary questions: what does clause four actually require of us, does this agreement let us terminate early if the installer underperforms, what deadline are we about to walk straight into without noticing. Have it flag genuinely risky or unusual clauses automatically rather than waiting to be asked. That's the whole product, and it's deliberately not more than that.

The build itself is genuinely within reach in days, not months, and this is where an AI coding tool actually earns its keep rather than just being a novelty. Document upload, text extraction, a vector store for the chunked content, and a chat interface wired to Claude for the actual answering: that's a well-worn, thoroughly documented pattern at this point, and Claude Code will happily build most of the plumbing for you across an afternoon or two rather than a sprint. Add Stripe for billing, a simple hosting setup, and maybe a basic admin view so you can see what people are actually asking, and you're comfortably under five hundred pounds to get a working first version live and in front of real users. The honest, conservative money maths looks like this: even ten small charities or clubs paying twenty pounds a month each to stop dreading their compliance paperwork gets you two hundred pounds a month recurring, which won't change your life on its own, but it is a real, working business you built and shipped in about a week, and the ceiling moves upward fast if the niche turns out to be painful enough that people happily upgrade to a higher tier with more documents or faster turnaround.

The risks are worth saying out loud rather than skating past them, because this isn't a toy category. Getting an answer wrong on a legal or compliance document is a genuine liability question, not a minor product bug you patch next sprint, so you'd want clear disclaimers baked in from day one that this is a reading aid rather than legal advice, and quite possibly a human-review step designed into the product from the start rather than bolted on later once someone complains. Trust also builds more slowly in compliance-adjacent tools than it does in, say, a fun consumer app that people try on a whim, so expect a longer sales cycle than PDF dot AI's headline numbers might suggest to you at first glance. And the market here is genuinely narrower by design, which is the entire point of niching down, but it does mean you shouldn't expect Chen-style overnight virality from three hundred and fifty thousand users in four months; this is a steady, defensible, slightly boring niche business, not a lottery ticket, and that's actually a feature, not a bug, for someone who already has two other ventures to run.

One more risk worth naming plainly: data sensitivity. Grant agreements, safeguarding policies and charging contracts all contain information people would rather not see leaked or misused, so wherever you land on hosting and on which model provider handles the actual document content, you'll want a clear, honestly-worded answer ready for the first prospective customer who asks where their documents go and who can see them. That's not a reason to avoid the idea, it's a reason to have the answer ready before anyone asks, rather than fumbling it in the room.

Your edge here, and I mean this specifically rather than as a motivational aside: you are not guessing at a niche from the outside the way most indie hackers building this kind of tool are forced to. You already have the network and the built-in credibility inside fundraising and grassroots sports to get your first ten paying users without a single cold email, which is consistently the single hardest part of this exact business model for someone starting from nothing.

On go-to-market specifically, since a good idea with no distribution plan is just a hobby: don't launch this on Product Hunt or wait for organic search the way Chen eventually scaled PDF dot AI. Go straight to three or four charity or club contacts you already have a working relationship with through PlayFundWin, and offer to run their next grant agreement or funding terms document through a rough prototype for free, in exchange for ten minutes of honest feedback about whether the answers were actually useful. Worth a structural note on the tech choices too, since "just wire it to Claude" undersells how much judgement still goes into a build like this. You'll want to decide fairly early whether you're chunking documents by fixed size or by structure, clause by clause for legal-style paperwork tends to give noticeably better answers than blindly splitting every five hundred words, because a clause cut in half mid-sentence produces a worse answer than a slightly larger, cleanly bounded chunk. It's a small decision, but it's the difference between a tool that feels sharp on day one and one that needs an awkward round of user complaints to fix.
