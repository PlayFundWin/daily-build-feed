Part one. What shipped.

First up, and this is the one worth sitting with: OpenAI previewed something it's calling Private Safety Processing, announced Wednesday the nineteenth of August, and I read the announcement myself. Here's the problem it's solving. OpenAI already offers Zero Data Retention to enterprise customers, meaning your prompts and outputs aren't stored after the call completes. Good for privacy, but it created a blind spot: if a customer misused the API in that mode, OpenAI's safety systems couldn't see the pattern, because there was nothing to see. Private Safety Processing tries to have it both ways. Automated tooling flags harmful patterns across related interactions while your actual content stays encrypted with keys you control, so OpenAI's own staff can't read it. If your account gets flagged, you get a narrow signal, not a human reading your prompts. It's rolling out in September, with a technical paper to follow. Why you care: if you've been keeping client contracts, financial records or anything remotely sensitive out of ChatGPT or the API because you didn't trust where it was going, this is OpenAI trying to close that exact gap. For a UK business specifically, that matters a bit more than it might elsewhere, because you're already sitting under UK data protection law on anything involving client or member personal data. A credible technical answer to "who can actually see this" isn't just nice to have, it's the kind of thing you'd want to point to if a client or a trustee ever asked how their information was being handled. Worth revisiting properly once it actually ships, not just previews.

Second, Cursor pushed a genuinely bigger update to its agent harness on Tuesday the eighteenth, and again, I read the changelog directly. The headline feature is a slash-goal command, which lets you hand the agent an open-ended objective, something like keep the build green, rather than a single task, and it'll keep working at it. They've also added subscriptions, so an agent can wake itself up when a pull request lands or a message arrives in Slack, isolated subagents that run on their own virtual machines so several pieces of work happen in parallel without stepping on each other, and custom modes that let you pin a particular skill as the agent's default behaviour. Why you care: this is squarely aimed at people without a dedicated engineering team. If you don't have anyone whose job is to babysit a build pipeline, handing an agent a standing goal and letting it manage itself is exactly the kind of leverage a two or three person operation needs.

Third, and a nice companion story to the OpenAI one: Replit launched what it's calling Free Mode on Monday the seventeenth, powered by OpenAI's cheaper Luna model, which had its price cut by around eighty per cent back in July. Free Mode covers the everyday stuff, chatting, ideating, routine edits, at no credit cost, and automatically upgrades to a stronger model only when the task actually needs it, then steps back down. Paying subscribers reportedly get around thirty times more usage out of their existing plan. Why you care: if you've been putting off prototyping an internal tool because you didn't want to burn through paid credits just exploring an idea, that friction has now mostly gone. The cost of "let's just try it" keeps dropping.

Part two. The build.

Right, today's deep dive, and I want to spend real time on this one because the shape of it is different from anything we've covered so far on this show.

The business is Coachvox AI, and the founder is Jodie Cook, a London-based entrepreneur who'd already built and sold a social media agency before starting this one. Here's the pitch: if you're a coach, a consultant, an author, anyone who's built up a body of knowledge, a tone of voice, a set of frameworks, Coachvox lets you upload your books, your blog posts, your podcast transcripts, your course material, and turns all of it into a branded AI chatbot that talks as you. Not a generic assistant wearing your logo. Something trained specifically on how you actually think and phrase things, that can coach your clients and your audience around the clock without eating any more of your actual hours.

And critically, this is sold business to business, to the expert themselves, who then either offers it as a value-add to existing clients or resells access to their wider audience.

Now, the numbers, and I want to be precise about where each one comes from, because that matters more than the number itself. Jodie Cook told Hampton, the founder community, that Coachvox hit six figures in annual recurring revenue before the product had even properly launched, while they were still building the waitlist to open more spaces, back in 2023. Separately, the business directory Latka reports Coachvox reached one hundred and eighty thousand dollars in annual recurring revenue with one hundred and fifty customers by November of 2023, an average contract value of around twelve hundred dollars, fully bootstrapped, no outside funding, one employee at the time. Pricing, according to Starter Story's breakdown, sits at ninety-nine dollars a month for the creator, who in turn often charges their own audience something like ten dollars a month for access to their AI coach. I'll be straight with you: the most recent verified figure I could find is that November 2023 number. There may well be a more current one out there, but I'm not going to invent it, so treat that as the last confirmed data point rather than where the business stands today.

Here's the part that should genuinely interest you if you're thinking about building something like this yourself. Coachvox itself wasn't built by a team of engineers from scratch. Per Starter Story's own tools breakdown, the actual stack was Bubble for the no-code front end, Vercel for hosting, OpenAI for the model, and Google Cloud Storage underneath. That's a strong signal about what's achievable here. The underlying mechanics are what's called retrieval-augmented generation: you take someone's written and spoken content, break it into chunks, store it in a vector database, wrap the whole thing in a system prompt that captures their tone and their frameworks, and serve it through a simple chat widget with Stripe on the front for payment. That's a standard, well-understood pattern at this point. A solo builder using Claude to scaffold a proper coded version, rather than relying purely on no-code tools, could realistically have a working demo live in a matter of days.

Why does this suit the UK specifically? There's a genuinely large pool of business coaches, executive coaches, financial advisers, fitness and performance coaches, and authors, all of whom already have a back-catalogue of content and a real incentive to scale their one-to-one time without simply working more hours. Coachvox's own traction, and the fact it got picked up by UK tech press, tells you the demand is real, not hypothetical. The obvious catch is that Coachvox is already established and reasonably well known in this space, so going head to head with them directly is a losing game. The move is to niche down hard. Only for sports and performance coaches. Only for accountants. Only for financial advisers who need something compliance-aware. Pick a lane a generalist tool hasn't bothered to specialise into.

Let's talk about how you'd actually build it, because that's the part that makes this different from a lot of ideas we cover here. Step one is content collection: pull together everything the expert has written or said, the more the better, and clean it into plain text. Step two is chunking and embedding that text into a vector database, which is a solved problem now, there are libraries that do the hard part for you. Step three is the system prompt, and this is genuinely where the value lives: you're not just telling the model to sound friendly, you're encoding the expert's actual frameworks, their pet phrases, the questions they always ask back, the opinions they hold that most people in their field don't. That's the bit a generalist chatbot can't fake. Step four is the interface, a simple embeddable chat widget on the expert's own website or a branded standalone page. Step five is metering and billing through Stripe, so the expert pays for their tier and, if they're reselling access, their audience pays them. None of these five steps requires anything exotic. It's the kind of build where the challenge isn't the technology, it's getting the tone right, and that's a product problem, not an engineering one.

On cost, this is genuinely a sub-five-hundred-pound build. A domain costs about ten pounds a year. Hosting on something like Vercel is free to start, maybe twenty pounds a month once you've got real usage. A vector database on a free or low tier runs zero to twenty pounds a month. Budget fifty to a hundred pounds for API usage while you're building and testing the thing on yourself. Stripe is free to integrate, they just take a cut per transaction. If you want a voice-cloned version so it actually sounds like the person, a service like ElevenLabs has a usable free tier or sits around twenty pounds a month. Add in fifty pounds or so for a proper terms-of-service and consent template, because you genuinely need one here. All in, comfortably under five hundred pounds to get a first version live and onboard your first few paying experts.

Now the risks, and I'm not going to soft-pedal these, because they're real. The biggest one is trust. If an audience feels like they've been talking to a real, named expert and it turns out to be an unlabelled AI clone, that backlash lands hard, and it lands on the expert's name, not yours. Disclosure has to be upfront and clear, every time. Second, hallucination risk is not abstract here: if the bot gives bad advice under someone's real name, particularly in anything financial, health-adjacent, or legal-adjacent, that's a liability problem, not just an embarrassing one, so you'd want strict guardrails on scope. Third, as I said, Coachvox itself is already the established, press-covered player in this exact space, so this only works if you pick a vertical they haven't bothered to specialise into. And fourth, your ongoing costs scale with how much each creator's audience actually uses the bot, so your pricing needs to build in real margin from day one, not just cover the demo phase. None of these risks are reasons not to build it. They're reasons to build it narrower and more carefully than the pitch makes it sound: strict scope on what the bot will and won't answer, a visible disclosure on every conversation, and a pricing model that assumes real usage rather than the quiet demo traffic you'll see in week one.

Three runner-ups, quickly, because they show how wide this category actually is.

SkillSoniq is an AI-powered recruiting marketplace built by founder Abhinav Verma, letting companies trial freelance or domestic talent before converting them to payroll, with claimed savings of over sixty per cent on hiring costs. Cal AI is a photo-based calorie tracker built by teenage founder Zach Yadegari: point your phone at your meal, get an instant AI estimate of calories and macros. Multiple outlets, including TechCrunch, Inc. and CNBC, reported it grew to somewhere between forty and fifty million dollars in annual recurring revenue before being acquired by MyFitnessPal back in March of this year. And DoNotPay, founded by Joshua Browder, uses AI to help ordinary people fight parking tickets, cancel subscriptions and cut through bureaucratic admin. Fast Company reported it had over two hundred thousand subscribers paying eighteen dollars a month, roughly seventy per cent one-year retention, and had recently turned profitable enough to pay shareholders a dividend north of one million dollars. Good evidence for AI in consumer legal and admin work, a category most people overlook, though again, this is a funded, staffed operation rather than something you'd knock out solo. What all three runner-ups share with the main pick is a founder who started by solving one specific, personally understood annoyance, not by trying to build a platform for everyone. That's the filter worth applying to your own list of frustrations before you copy any of these outright.

Line those three runner-ups up against the main pick and a pattern shows itself. Coachvox sells expertise-as-a-service to the expert. SkillSoniq sells reduced hiring risk to the employer. Cal AI sells effortless tracking to the individual consumer. DoNotPay sells relief from bureaucracy to the individual consumer. Four different buyers, four different price points, all riding the same underlying shift: AI has made it cheap enough to build a narrow tool for a specific frustration that, five years ago, would only have justified a much bigger, much more general piece of software. That's the real takeaway from today's build section, more than any single number. The opportunity isn't "build an AI chatbot." It's "find the specific, narrow frustration you already understand better than a venture-backed team in San Francisco does, and build only for that."
