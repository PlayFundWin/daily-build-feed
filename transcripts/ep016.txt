Part one. What shipped.

First up, Anthropic launched something called Claude Academy on Thursday. It's a free educational platform, built around courses and tutorials rather than a chat window, and it's structured around five stated principles: keeping human agency in the loop, favouring mindsets over rote mechanics, active learning over passive reading, and a couple of others in the same spirit. Why you care: if you've been learning prompting by trial and error, which, let's be honest, most of us have, this is a free, structured on-ramp built by the people who actually make the model, rather than a course some marketer threw together last month. Fifteen minutes with it before bed beats another evening of guessing, and it costs you nothing but the time.

Second, and this is the bigger one. Anthropic took its Computer Use tool out of beta and into full general availability, and launched a brand new Browser Use tool alongside it. Computer Use now supports batch actions, meaning an agent can fire off several actions in a single turn instead of clicking once, waiting, clicking again. It's also now cleared for HIPAA-regulated workloads under a business associate agreement, which is a fairly strong signal that Anthropic considers it stable enough for genuinely sensitive, regulated use, not just demos.

Browser Use is the properly new piece here. Up until now, an agent driving a browser had to squint at a screenshot and guess where the button probably was, the same way you'd navigate a website with your eyes half shut. Browser Use instead reads the actual page structure, the accessibility tree underneath the pixels, so it can find and click the correct element with far more consistency. I read the platform release notes and the accompanying blog post directly, both confirmed.

Why you care: this is the difference between an agent that sort of clicks around a webpage and hopes for the best, and one you can actually trust to run a real back-office task while you're not watching it. Think data entry, form filling, chasing information across a website that's never going to offer you a proper export button or an API. That used to be a job for a virtual assistant on an hourly rate. It just got a lot more plausible to hand to software instead, and a lot more reliable than it was even a month ago.

Picture the actual use case rather than the abstraction: a club treasurer's membership platform that only lets you view one record at a time, no bulk export anywhere in sight. Last month, that was an hour of tedious clicking for a volunteer on a Sunday evening. With a properly configured Browser Use agent, it's a five-minute setup once, and then it runs itself every month after that. That's the entire pitch for this release in one sentence.

Part two. The build.

Today's evidence-first idea is Jenni AI, an academic writing assistant. And I want to walk through this one properly, because the story behind it is a genuinely useful lesson on its own, quite apart from the tool itself.

Jenni launched back in August twenty nineteen as a general-purpose AI writing tool, no particular audience in mind, write-anything-for-anyone. It went nowhere fast. Two thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue, and stuck there. Then in twenty twenty-two the founder, David Park, and his team did the one thing that actually moved the needle, and it's the same lesson every one of these build segments keeps circling back to: they repositioned, hard, at one specific, narrow audience. Students and postgraduate researchers, specifically that exact moment of staring at a blank document with a citation format you're inevitably, eventually, going to get marked down for getting wrong.

It went semi-viral on social media in July twenty twenty-two, and the numbers from that point onward are the kind you don't often get to see in this much detail, because Jenni's team has been unusually open about sharing them publicly.

An Indie Hackers post, quoting the founder's own stated figures, records that in a single month, August twenty twenty-three, Jenni brought in one hundred and fifty thousand dollars in revenue, with seventy-two thousand seven hundred dollars of that landing as profit. Over the full year following that July twenty twenty-two relaunch, the same post states one point two million dollars in total revenue, and one point six million users accumulated across fifteen months.

A separate interview, published by the research firm Sacra, has Park saying directly, quote, we added one hundred thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue with influencer marketing, end quote, and separately, that the business was, quote, still hovering around sixteen per cent churn, end quote. Hold onto that churn figure, I'll come back to it in a moment.

And a more recent piece, from the outlet TMTPost, reports that annual recurring revenue rose from around one point eight million dollars in twenty twenty-three, to eight million dollars in twenty twenty-four, and past ten million dollars by early this year. The team, according to that same report, stayed at just nine people through late last year before expanding to twenty-three. Bootstrapped throughout, with no pressure to raise outside money, because, in Park's own words, they were having trouble spending all the revenue they already had.

So that's three separate named sources, at three separate moments in the company's life, all pointing in exactly the same direction. That's about as solid as published evidence gets in this space, and precisely why it's today's pick over anything shakier.

Here's why it's genuinely buildable, not just impressive. Strip away the polish and Jenni is, at its core, a rich text editor wired up to a strong language model. You write, it autocompletes in your voice, it paraphrases on request, and it attaches citations pulled from real papers in the correct academic format. None of that requires training your own model from scratch. A text editor library for the interface, calls out to Claude for the actual writing and citation work, Stripe for handling payments, and perfectly ordinary web hosting underneath it all. Nothing there that most solo builders working with Claude haven't already touched in some other project.

Now, here's the part I actually want you to sit with, because a straight copy of Jenni aimed at the UK student market is a losing fight against an incumbent with a two-year head start, a war chest of testimonials, and a brand name students already trust. That's not the edge worth chasing. The edge is the niche Jenni never took, and you happen to already own the audience for it.

You already have a direct line to several thousand grassroots sports club treasurers and secretaries through LeaguePages. Every single one of those people, at some point in the club's year, has to sit down and write something formal and slightly dreaded: a grant application to a funding body, a safeguarding policy document, a funding bid to a local authority, an annual report back to a national governing body. It's the exact same blank-page, exact-format-required problem Jenni solved for students, except this audience only does it once a quarter, dreads it every single time, and has absolutely nobody building anything specifically for them.

Worth being honest about the competitive picture too, rather than pretending this is empty ground. There are general-purpose UK grant-writing consultants who charge several hundred pounds per application, and a handful of broad "AI grant writer" tools aimed at charities generally, mostly American-built and priced in dollars. None of them, as far as this research turned up, are built specifically for grassroots sports clubs, and none of them arrive with an existing audience of exactly the right people already checking a website every week. That last part is the whole advantage. The tool doesn't have to be better than every alternative in the world, it just has to be the one sitting in front of the right person at the right moment.

Build steps, roughly, in three phases. Phase one, a week or so: a simple rich-text editor, Claude doing the drafting and rewriting behind it, and a small starter library of the two or three formats grassroots clubs actually get asked for most often, safeguarding policy, grant application, funding bid. Phase two: an autocomplete tuned to write in a slightly formal, trustworthy-committee-volunteer voice rather than an academic one, since the tone that reassures a funding panel is quite different from the tone that reassures a dissertation supervisor. Phase three, and this is the bit that actually sells it: a handful of pre-filled templates specific to grassroots sport, so a treasurer opens the tool and sees a Sport England grant template sitting there ready to go, rather than a blank page and a vague prompt box. You are not inventing a new category of software here. You're taking a proven wrapper and pointing it at an audience nobody else has bothered to serve, because nobody else has that audience already sitting in their inbox the way you do.

Honest money maths, kept deliberately conservative throughout. A domain costs roughly ten pounds a year. Claude API usage while you're building and testing, call it thirty to fifty pounds. Hosting on a free tier to start with, and Stripe costs you nothing at all until you actually take a payment. Call the entire build somewhere between fifty and one hundred and fifty pounds all in, comfortably inside the five-hundred-pound ceiling for this kind of project. Price it modestly, say eight to twelve pounds a month per club, and twenty clubs signed up gets you somewhere around two hundred pounds a month in recurring revenue before you've done any marketing beyond your own existing audience. Fifty clubs, a genuinely achievable number given how many are already inside the LeaguePages world, and you're looking at four to five hundred pounds a month. That's not retirement money, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. It's proof the wrapper works, on an audience you already own, at a cost that barely registers against anything else you're running.

Before any of those three phases, though, spend a day validating rather than building blind. Post a short, plain description of the idea into the LeaguePages community, or email a handful of club treasurers directly, and ask whether they'd pay eight to ten pounds a month for it, today, before a single line of code exists. If five of them say yes, and better still, if two of them will pay upfront for early access, you've de-risked the whole project for the price of an afternoon. If nobody bites, you've saved yourself two weeks of building something nobody actually wanted, which is exactly the mistake Jenni's own founders made the first time round, back in twenty nineteen, before they found their audience.

Risks, honestly, and there are a few worth naming rather than glossing over. Jenni's own churn, sixteen per cent as stated directly by the founder, is a reminder that writing tools like this get used seasonally rather than daily, so expect cancellations between funding rounds and people quietly coming back the moment the next grant deadline looms. If you ever add anything resembling plagiarism detection, tread carefully, because that edges into a liability question rather than a simple feature question, and I'd leave it out of a first version entirely.

There's a second risk that's specific to this particular niche, and it's worth taking seriously rather than skating past. Safeguarding policy documents, by their nature, sometimes reference children, vulnerable adults, and sensitive incidents. That means proper data handling matters here in a way it wouldn't for a general essay tool: encrypt documents at rest, don't retain anything longer than necessary, and be explicit with clubs about exactly where their data sits and who can see it. That's not a reason to avoid the idea, plenty of small UK software vendors handle exactly this kind of information responsibly every day, but it is a reason to bake proper data hygiene into phase one rather than bolting it on afterwards once a club has already asked an awkward question.

And there's a slower-burn risk too: if this works, it's genuinely easy for someone else to copy once they see it, so the actual moat here isn't the software, it's the distribution you already have through LeaguePages that a copycat would have to build from zero. Move on it while that head start is still real.
