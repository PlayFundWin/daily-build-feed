Part two. The build.

Today's idea comes from a founder called Piotr Obidowski, and it's a genuinely good lesson in what "niching down" actually looks like when you follow it all the way through, rather than half-doing it and wondering why it didn't work.

His product is called Visualizee. The pitch is simple: an architect or an interior designer describes a room or a building in plain English, in a chat window, and the tool generates a hyper-realistic render of it. There's a friendly assistant inside called Vizzy who handles all the prompt engineering behind the scenes, so the architect never has to learn the specific magic words that get a good result out of an image model, they just describe what's in their head. It supports over a hundred and forty languages, so it isn't even locked to English-speaking markets, which matters more than it sounds like it should.

Here's the part I actually want you to sit with, because it's the honest bit most of these stories skip. For the first two years, Visualizee made about a hundred and fifty dollars a month. One-time payments, no subscription, and barely any traction. That's not a success story, that's a founder quietly grinding on something that mostly wasn't working, for two full years, while probably wondering whether to give up on it. Piotr wrote about this himself in a post republished on Indie Hackers, and the honesty in it is the genuinely useful part, not the eventual numbers everyone quotes.

Then he changed two things, and only two things. He moved from one-time payments to a proper monthly subscription, and he rebuilt the entire interface around that conversational chat experience with Vizzy, so the product stopped feeling like a form with some dropdown settings on it and started feeling like talking to a capable assistant who happened to understand architecture specifically. Six months after that pivot, Visualizee was doing eight thousand six hundred dollars a month in recurring revenue. That's roughly a fifty seven times increase, in half a year, built on exactly the same underlying image-generation technology he'd had the whole time, just repositioned and re-priced around how the actual customer wanted to use it.

Where did that growth actually come from, because this is the bit people skip past to get to the exciting number. By his own account, around half of it came from organic search, built on blog content that specifically targeted the phrases architects and interior designers actually type into Google, not generic phrases like "AI image generator" that every tool in the category is fighting over. Another ten to fifteen per cent came from what he calls AI-citation traffic, meaning tools like ChatGPT surfacing Visualizee directly when someone asks it for architectural rendering software, which is a channel that essentially didn't exist a couple of years ago and is quietly becoming a real acquisition source for small, well-niched tools. He was seeing something like a hundred and eighty to two hundred visitors a day after the pivot, converting from a free trial to a paid plan at somewhere between twenty and thirty five per cent, which, for a chat product running a card-required seven-day trial, is a genuinely strong conversion number, most subscription products would take that.

I want to be precise about what's actually novel here, because it isn't the technology, and that's the whole point. Underneath the hood, this is a chat interface sitting on top of an existing image generation model, with a prompt-engineering layer translating plain English into good prompts, plus standard subscription billing. There is no proprietary model, no fine-tuning breakthrough, nothing here that an AI coding assistant couldn't help you sketch the bones of in days rather than months. The entire value sits in the packaging: picking exactly one trade, understanding precisely how that trade describes what it wants out loud, and removing every scrap of friction between "I have an idea in my head" and "I have a picture of it on my screen."

That is a directly copyable playbook, so let's be honest about what building it would actually take, rather than romantic about it. On the technical side: a chat interface, calls to an existing image generation API rather than anything you'd train yourself, a prompt-translation layer that takes plain English and turns it into a good image prompt behind the scenes, and standard subscription billing through something like Stripe. None of that is exotic, and a solo builder with Claude doing the heavy lifting on the code could plausibly have a working version live inside a couple of weeks, not months.

On the money maths, be conservative rather than hopeful. Image generation API calls cost real money per render, so model your unit economics properly before you set a price, don't just guess a subscription number and hope the margin works out, work backwards from what a render actually costs you at your expected usage per customer. Hosting and the chat interface itself are genuinely cheap, you could have a working prototype live for well under five hundred pounds all in. The real cost isn't the infrastructure, it's the unglamorous work of writing blog content that actually ranks for what your specific target trade searches for, because that's where roughly half of Visualizee's growth came from by the founder's own account, and unglamorous, specific, patient content writing takes weeks of steady effort, not a single weekend sprint.

On risk, be honest too. Image generation models can and do change under you, a provider updating their model or their pricing overnight is entirely outside your control, and it can quietly wreck your margins or your output quality with no warning. Trade-specific tools also live or die on trust, one bad render sent to a client is a much bigger deal for a professional architect than a slightly wrong blog post is for a general writer, so quality control matters more here than it does in most AI wrapper businesses. And the category itself, while currently underserved for any one specific trade, is not secret, other niches will get targeted the same way as this playbook gets more well known, which is exactly why speed and genuine depth in your one chosen niche both matter.

And Tour Estate AI generates automated video tours and marketing content for property listings, sitting at a hundred and twenty nine dollars a month recurring, according to the same TrustMRR tracking. What all three runner-ups share with Visualizee, even at their much smaller scale, is the same underlying shape: someone looked at a generic AI capability everyone already has access to, and instead of competing head-on in the crowded general version of it, they asked who specifically needs this and what specifically do they need it to look like. Kjeldaas didn't build a better general receipt scanner, he built the one that worked properly for Swedish businesses. Terekhov didn't build a better general video tool, he built one that speaks recruiter, one-way interviews, automatic scoring, nothing else. That's a repeatable question you can ask about almost any generic AI tool you've already seen and dismissed as "not for me": not for you generally, maybe, but is there a version of it that's specifically for one trade you already understand well.

The Visualizee story is the one to actually learn from today, and the lesson generalises cleanly beyond architecture entirely. Most of you listening aren't sitting on some completely unbuilt idea waiting to be discovered, you're far more likely sitting on a generic tool, or a generic idea, that simply hasn't been niched down hard enough yet, or hasn't been re-priced to match what people are actually, genuinely willing to pay once you've done that work properly.

Here's where you specifically have an edge that Piotr didn't have for his first two years. He spent two years grinding blog content from a standing start, with no audience, no trust, and no distribution, just hoping architects would eventually find him through search. You don't have that problem. Between PlayFundWin, LeaguePages and EV-Partnerships, you already sit in front of thousands of grassroots clubs, committee volunteers and small operators who already trust you enough to use your other tools. That's the single hardest part of Piotr's journey, the two flat years, and you'd be starting several steps past it. The version of this playbook that matters for you isn't "build a generic wrapper and hope for organic search," it's "build a wrapper niched to a trade you already have a direct line into," which is a fundamentally shorter, cheaper path to the same eight thousand six hundred dollars a month, or whatever the LeaguePages-specific number turns out to be.

That edge cuts both ways though, so don't skip the boring caution. An existing audience forgives a mediocre generic tool far less than a cold-start audience does, because they already have an opinion of you, so a clumsy first version does more reputational damage inside an existing base than it would to strangers finding you through a search result. Ship the boring, reliable version first, kit colours and sponsor logos and nothing fancier, before you reach for anything more ambitious.
