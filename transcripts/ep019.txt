Part one. What shipped.

After two consecutive rounds of "everyone went quiet," today actually broke the streak. Barely.

First: OpenAI. On Monday the twenty-fourth of August, OpenAI made its GPT five point six model family, that's Sol, Terra and Luna, available inside Kiro, which is Amazon Web Services' own coding-agent platform. I read OpenAI's own announcement directly for this one, not a write-up of it.

Here's the number that actually matters. On a benchmark called Terminal-Bench two point one, GPT five point six Terra completed the same coding tasks at roughly eighty-two per cent lower cost than earlier benchmarked runs, with fewer wasted iterations along the way. That's not a speed claim, it's a cost claim. The model gets to a correct answer with less wasted computation, and on a metered API bill, wasted computation is the number that shows up on your invoice at the end of the month.

Why you care: if anyone on your team leans on AI coding agents to prototype tools, and by episode nineteen you probably do, this is a second cost cut stacked on top of the one we covered back on the twenty-first. Coding-agent time is getting cheap enough, fast enough, that "let's just get Claude to knock up a rough version and see if it's worth building properly" is turning into a five-pound experiment rather than a fifty-pound one. Worth remembering next time an idea feels too small to bother prototyping. It probably isn't.

Worth being precise about what this isn't, too. It's not a smarter model, OpenAI isn't claiming GPT five point six got better at reasoning this week, it's claiming the same capability now costs meaningfully less to run, specifically inside a third-party agent platform rather than OpenAI's own products. That's a slightly unusual distribution move as well: a model lab making its models cheaper and more available inside a rival's coding tool, rather than only pushing people toward its own. It's a sign that the competition between these platforms is shifting from "whose model is smartest" toward "whose model is cheapest to run at scale," which, if you're on the buying side rather than the building side, is good news either way.

Second, same day: Google. On Monday the twenty-fourth, Google rolled out a genuinely new capability inside the Gemini app, and it's not another text answer, it's an interactive one. Ask it something like "show me how a combustion engine works," and instead of a paragraph, it now generates an actual rotatable three-dee model you can spin around inside the chat window. Ask it to model a pendulum, and it builds you a working simulation. Ask it to lay out a cash-burn scenario, and it builds an interactive table you can poke at, not a screenshot of one. I read Google's own Workspace Updates post, published that same day, confirming the rollout to both consumer Gemini accounts and Workspace domains, usage limits notwithstanding.

Why you care: Google is aiming this at students doing homework right now, but the underlying trick, turning a natural-language prompt into something interactive rather than something you just read, is exactly the kind of thing that turns a static slide in a sponsor pitch deck into something a funder can actually play with instead of skim past. Hold that thought, we'll come back to it in Part Three.

It's also worth noting what this quietly signals about where the big labs think the next fight is. Text answers are basically a solved, commoditised product at this point, every major assistant gives you a competent paragraph. Interactive, generated artefacts, a working simulation, a live model, a tool you can actually manipulate rather than read, are a much harder thing to fake and a much stickier thing to use once you've tried it. If Google's betting its consumer product on that, expect Anthropic and OpenAI to start pushing the same direction before too long.

And that's it for what shipped today. I checked Anthropic's newsroom directly, Cursor's changelog, Replit, Lovable, Bolt: all four still sitting on whatever they last posted before the weekend. Two labs moved, four stayed dark. After Sunday's "quietest stretch yet" and yesterday's second verse of the same song, I will happily take two confirmed, dated, sourced items over four invented ones.

Part two. The build.

Today's deep dive is a category we haven't touched yet on this show: AI-assisted video screening for hiring.

The idea, in one sentence: an employer writes a handful of screening questions, sends a candidate a link with no login required, and the candidate records video, audio or text answers on their own time. An AI layer transcribes every answer and builds a scorecard, so the employer can rank a stack of candidates without sitting through hours of footage.

The evidence behind it is real and it's named. The product is called Hirevire, built by a founder called Sanat Hegde, based in India, launched back in December twenty twenty-one, bootstrapped, run by a team of two to five people. According to TrustMRR, which verifies revenue through a connected Stripe account rather than a screenshot someone typed a number into, Hirevire is currently sitting at somewhere between thirteen thousand one hundred and fourteen thousand two hundred dollars in trailing thirty-day revenue, call it thirteen and a half thousand dollars a month, across a hundred and sixty-four active subscriptions and around five hundred total users, at roughly eighty per cent profit margin. I want to flag the honesty of that evidence properly: TrustMRR's Stripe verification is stronger than a self-reported number, but it's still the founder's own connected account, not an independent audit, so treat it as corroborated, not certified.

And it is corroborated, from more than one angle. The founder wrote his own origin story on Medium, explaining he built the tool after personally interviewing three hundred candidates the hard way and getting fed up with how much of his week disappeared into first-round calls that went nowhere. The company's own site claims over five hundred customers, naming logos including McDonald's, Hertz and an investment firm called PPFAS. It carries a four point nine out of five rating on AppSumo and won Capterra's Best Ease of Use award in twenty twenty-four. None of that is proof on its own, a customer logo on a website is marketing, not an audited client list, but stacked together, a Stripe-verified revenue figure, an independently awarded ease-of-use badge, a multi-year AppSumo rating from actual buyers, and a founder who's told the same origin story consistently since launch, it's a lot more than one man's word for it. That's the bar I want every deep dive on this show to clear: not a single screenshot, several independent things pointing the same direction.

Here's why this is worth four years of someone else's grinding rather than an overnight miracle, and I say that deliberately, because I'd rather give you the honest shape of the curve than a highlight reel. Hirevire didn't rocket to thirteen and a half thousand dollars a month. It took roughly four years, a small team, and presumably a lot of unglamorous customer support to get there. If you build a version of this alone, in the UK, targeting one narrow niche, a realistic first outcome inside three to six months of consistent outreach is five to fifteen paying customers at somewhere between twenty-nine and seventy-nine pounds a month each. That's a few hundred pounds to perhaps a thousand pounds a month, not a headline. The value of Hirevire's number isn't a timeline to copy, it's proof that people will actually pay for this category, which is the harder thing to prove from scratch.

Build steps, and this is genuinely close to a weekend's work with Claude doing the heavy lifting. One: an employer dashboard where someone creates a job, writes a handful of text questions, and gets a unique link with no login required for candidates, built on something like Next dot js with Supabase or Clerk handling the database and authentication. Two: a candidate-facing page using the browser's built-in media recorder capability to capture webcam or microphone answers directly, with a text fallback for anyone who'd rather type. Three: uploading those clips straight to cheap object storage, Cloudflare R two or Bunny Stream both work, to keep hosting costs sane from day one. Four, and this is the actual AI hook: pipe the audio to a speech-to-text service, then feed the transcript alongside the job requirements to Claude, and have it generate a scorecard and a short written summary for each candidate. This is the single easiest piece of the whole build. Five: an employer dashboard sortable by that AI score, so someone can skim the top five candidates instead of watching forty video clips end to end. Six: Stripe Checkout across a few pricing tiers, with a short free trial and no card required up front, mirroring exactly the funnel Hirevire already proved works.

On getting your first customers, worth being specific rather than waving at "marketing" as if it happens by itself. Hirevire's own growth leaned on being findable when someone searches for a specific alternative to an established, expensive competitor, so the same playbook applies here: a handful of comparison pages, "video screening tool for small business," "affordable alternative to enterprise hiring software," built once and left to earn search traffic quietly over months rather than requiring constant attention. A Product Hunt launch on the day you go live, timed for a Tuesday or Wednesday when the site sees the most traffic, gets you an initial wave of curious early users for free. And direct outreach, fifteen or twenty personal emails a week to UK recruitment agencies, hospitality groups and care providers with high-volume hiring, will get you your first paying customers faster than any of the passive channels, because a founder who's clearly used the product themselves and can speak to a specific pain point beats a cold advert every time at this stage.

Honest money maths on getting started: a domain costs around ten pounds, hosting on something like Vercel or Railway is free to near-free at low volume, object storage is usage-based and only a few pounds a month early on, Claude API calls plus speech-to-text realistically run twenty to fifty pounds a month at low volume, and Stripe charges nothing upfront. Total to get a working product live and taking its first customer: somewhere between a hundred and fifty and three hundred pounds. Comfortably inside the five-hundred-pound ceiling we set ourselves on this show.

Now, here's the bit I actually got excited about while researching this one, and it's specific to you. Generic hiring-screening tools compete on being the best generic hiring-screening tool, which is a crowded, noisy fight against well-funded competitors. You don't have to fight that fight. LeaguePages already sits in front of thousands of grassroots sports clubs, and every single one of those clubs has to vet new volunteer coaches before they're allowed anywhere near a team. Safeguarding is not optional for them, it's a legal and reputational necessity, and right now it's mostly done through paper forms, DBS checks processed slowly, and word-of-mouth trust. A video-screening add-on, built specifically for club committees vetting a new adult volunteer coach, with structured questions a safeguarding officer actually wants asked, consistently, on the record, isn't a cold start into a crowded market. It's a feature extension to an audience you already have, solving a problem they already have to solve anyway. That's a genuinely different starting position from Sanat Hegde's four-year grind, and it's worth building the MVP around that one specific customer before you ever try to sell it as a generic hiring tool.

Risks, plainly. Video of job candidates is personal data under UK GDPR, so retention periods, consent language and deletion policies need to be right from day one, not bolted on later. Storage and bandwidth costs scale with usage in a way flat subscription pricing doesn't always keep up with, so watch your margins as volume grows. And "safeguarding officer at a grassroots club" is a genuinely different buyer than "hiring manager at a call centre," with different budget, different urgency and a much smaller total addressable market, so the LeaguePages angle is a strong wedge, not necessarily the whole business on its own.

One more risk worth naming directly, because glossing over it would be dishonest: any product touching volunteer vetting for youth sports sits close to child-safeguarding territory even though the people actually being screened and recorded on video are adult applicants, not children. That means the standard you should be building to isn't "good enough for a generic hiring tool," it's "good enough that a club's safeguarding officer, and if it ever came to it, an actual regulator, would be comfortable with how the data's handled." Practically: short, clearly-stated retention windows, straightforward deletion on request, no retaining footage longer than a club's own vetting process requires, and language throughout that treats this as a safeguarding tool first and a hiring tool second. Get that right early and it becomes a genuine trust advantage over generic competitors; get it wrong and it becomes the reason a club committee says no.

Three runners-up, briefly, all with their own real named evidence, in case the main pick doesn't grab you.

One: a tool called Mentions, which tracks how a brand actually gets mentioned, or ignored, inside ChatGPT and other AI answer engines rather than traditional search results. Founder Cameron Trew posted on Indie Hackers back in January that it was doing twenty thousand dollars a month, part of a wider portfolio he built alongside a companion tool called Kleo. Founder-stated, single source, but a genuinely fresh category, because as more people ask an AI assistant for a recommendation instead of googling one, "does the AI even mention us" becomes a real, measurable business question, and right now almost nobody's answering it well.

Two: a tool called Launch Fast, AI-powered product research and listing help for Amazon sellers. Founder Hasaam Bhatti, self-described as non-technical, built the first version in forty-eight hours using Cursor, and posted on Indie Hackers that it hit ten thousand dollars a month by day thirty, twenty-one thousand eight hundred by day ninety, and thirty thousand dollars a month a few months after that. Also useful as raw proof that an AI-coding-tool minimum viable product can go from nothing to real revenue inside days, not months, if the underlying pain point is sharp enough and the seller audience is already there waiting.

Three: Photo AI, from the well-known indie developer Pieter Levels, an AI headshot and photoshoot generator. This is the best-corroborated figure of the four, cross-confirmed across his own public posts, his building journal, Indie Hackers, and a Lex Fridman podcast appearance: roughly five thousand four hundred dollars in week one, sixty-one thousand eight hundred by month five, past one hundred thousand dollars a month by month eighteen, and somewhere around a hundred and thirty-five thousand dollars a month as of late last year. I'll flag honestly that this one is more technically involved to build, it needs image-generation infrastructure rather than a straightforward transcription pipeline, so it's less of a weekend build than the other three, but it's the strongest revenue proof in the batch, and the clearest evidence that speed of first launch, Pieter Levels shipped the original version in two to three weeks, matters more than polish.

What ties all four of today's picks together, main idea and runners-up alike, is that none of them are technically clever. Video transcription, brand-mention tracking, product research, image generation, these are all AI capabilities that already exist off the shelf. What made the money in every single case was picking a specific, underserved buyer and building the smallest possible thing that buyer would actually pay for, not building the most impressive possible thing and hoping a buyer turns up. That's the pattern worth internalising more than any individual number.

Part three. Apply it.

Start with today's Google story, because it maps onto PlayFundWin almost exactly. An interactive, pokeable visual beats a static one every time you're trying to hold a sponsor's or a trustee's attention for more than four seconds. Imagine sending a prospective club sponsor not a PDF one-pager but a small interactive tool, built with the same kind of AI-assisted approach Gemini just demonstrated, where they can drag a slider and watch projected reach or projected donor engagement change in front of them. That's a genuinely low-effort, high-impact use of exactly the capability Google just shipped, and it's the sort of thing you could prototype with Claude in an afternoon.

The OpenAI story is more of a quiet operational win than a headline: if GPT five point six is now roughly eighty per cent cheaper to run on coding tasks inside Kiro, and Claude's own pricing keeps trending the same direction, the cost of asking an AI coding agent to build small internal tools for PlayFundWin or LeaguePages, a better reconciliation dashboard, a tidier volunteer sign-up form, keeps dropping. Worth periodically asking "is there a fiddly internal process I've been putting off automating because it felt like too much effort to build," because the answer to "how much effort" keeps shrinking.

Put those two stories together and there's a small pattern worth naming: the cost of building things is falling at roughly the same time as the ceiling on what you can build with a single prompt is rising. Eighteen months ago, an interactive sponsor-facing tool and a custom internal dashboard were both "hire a developer" projects. Today they're both realistically "an afternoon with Claude" projects. That compounding effect, cheaper and more capable at the same time, is the actual story underneath most of what this show covers week to week, more than any single release.

The build idea itself is the most direct crossover of the three. As laid out above, a safeguarding-focused video screening feature for volunteer coach vetting is a natural extension to LeaguePages rather than a separate business to bootstrap from zero, and it's worth sketching out properly rather than filing away as "someday."

Part four. One action today.

Here's a single action, thirty minutes or under. Open a blank document and write out the three questions you'd actually ask a new volunteer coach applying to a grassroots club, if you were the safeguarding officer doing the vetting. Not generic hiring questions, the specific, slightly uncomfortable ones a club actually needs answered before letting someone near a youth team: prior experience working with young people, why they want the role, how they'd handle a difficult parent. Then open Claude and ask it to sketch, in plain English, what a two-minute video-response flow for those three questions would look like end to end, from the club sending the link to the committee reviewing the answers.

You're not building anything yet. You're testing, in thirty minutes, whether the safeguarding angle on today's build idea actually has legs before you spend a weekend coding it. If the flow you sketch out feels genuinely useful when you read it back, that's your signal to prototype it properly. If it feels like a solution looking for a problem, you've lost thirty minutes instead of a weekend, which is exactly the point of doing it this way round.
