Part one. What shipped.

Let's start with what actually shipped in the last forty eight hours, because there's rather more of it than the quiet stretch we had a couple of episodes back.

First, OpenAI. On Monday the twenty fifth of August, they launched something they're calling the Admin plugin, built straight into ChatGPT Work and into Codex. Instead of a separate admin panel with menus and settings screens, you just talk to it. Add a team member. Set a spending cap. Review who's using what. Route an approval request through Slack or Teams without leaving the chat window. OpenAI says this one feature already resolves around forty five per cent of their own internal IT tickets. That's not a number cooked up for a slide deck at a sales pitch, that's them running it on their own staff, at scale, and being willing to publish the result.

Why you care: if you're running ChatGPT seats across PlayFundWin or LeaguePages, and you've ever had to fire off an email just to change someone's permissions or check what they're spending, that friction is about to disappear. You won't need a technical hire, or even a spare afternoon, just to manage who has access to what.

It's also a useful reminder of a pattern worth watching generally. Every big AI company eventually turns its own internal pain points into a product feature, because they've already built the thing to fix their own headache before they ever thought to sell it. If you ever find yourself wondering what OpenAI or Anthropic might ship next, a decent guess is usually whatever their own operations team has been quietly nagging engineering to fix.

Second, also from OpenAI, also announced Monday: the first public benchmarks for their own custom inference chip, which they've named Jalapeño. Early results show somewhere between one and a half and just under two times more computing work per watt of power, and up to three and a half times lower latency, compared with the systems OpenAI measured it against, including some tests run against a rival model called Kimi two point five. Wider deployment is pencilled in for the end of this year, and a second generation chip is already being developed behind it.

Why you care: this one's plumbing, not a feature you'll ever click on. But plumbing is exactly what makes AI subscriptions and API calls cheaper eighteen months down the line. Every time one of the big labs builds its own silicon instead of renting someone else's, that's quiet downward pressure on what you pay per query, eventually. File it away, don't act on it today.

Worth putting in context too: a company the size of OpenAI building its own inference chip, rather than relying entirely on hardware bought in from outside suppliers, isn't a side project. It's usually a sign they expect to be running inference at a volume where even small efficiency gains translate into serious money. When the company selling you the picks and shovels starts mining its own gold, that tells you something about how big they think the gold rush still has left to run.

Third: Claude Code, the tool a good chunk of this show gets built with, shipped a genuinely chunky update on Tuesday. There's a new rules tab inside the permissions settings so you can see, in plain language, exactly what an unsupervised agent is allowed to touch before you let it loose on real code. There's a timer in the status line that shows how long the current task has actually been running, so you're not left guessing whether it's working or stuck. There's a model picker you can now curate yourself, instead of scrolling past half a dozen models you never use. Keyless sign-in through the Anthropic Console. New controls over how long your prompt cache lasts, which matters if you're running this daily show the way I am. And a batch of fixes for background sessions that were quietly crashing on some setups.

Why you care: none of this is flashy, and none of it will make a headline anywhere else. But if you, or anyone working for you, is letting an AI coding agent run semi-unsupervised on anything that touches real customer data or a live website, tighter permission visibility and fewer silent crashes are exactly the kind of unglamorous upgrade you want before you hand over more trust, not less.

Fourth, a smaller one out of Google: Gemini's "take notes for me" feature is coming to the physical touch controllers fitted in Google Meet hardware rooms, so someone in the room can start automatic note-taking with a single tap, instead of someone needing to open a laptop first. It was announced Monday, and it starts rolling out on the thirty first of August, so it isn't actually live yet.

Why you care: modest, but if any of your team's meetings happen in an actual meeting room with a screen on the wall, rather than round a laptop on a kitchen table, this removes one more excuse for nobody to write anything down afterwards.

Part two. The build.

Right, on to the part of the show where we go looking for a business idea backed by actual published numbers, not vibes.

Today's pick is a bit different from most of what we've covered here, and it's the one I'm most curious to hear your honest reaction to, because the evidence behind it is thinner than usual, and I want to be upfront about that before I get into it. Most weeks I try to bring you the sturdiest evidence I can find — a founder with a public dashboard, a payment processor corroborating the figure, a multi-year trajectory rather than a single data point. This week's pick doesn't have all of that. What it has instead is a mechanic I think is genuinely worth your attention regardless of how solid the underlying number turns out to be, so bear with me.

Back in February this year, Bryce Edwards — working alongside Andrew Wilkinson, the entrepreneur behind Tiny, who you may already know from other ventures — launched a short online quiz that analyses your relationship or compatibility style. Eight or so questions, a few minutes to complete, and at the end you get a free partial result plus the option to pay for the full, more detailed report. According to Edwards' own post on Indie Hackers, the thing brought in eleven thousand dollars in its first thirty days.

Now, let me flag the caveats straight away, because that's the deal on this show. That number comes from a single self-reported post, over a very short window, with no independent site or payment processor corroborating it the way we've had on some previous deep dives. I have this one from a named, credible source, but not from anything I could independently verify beyond that source itself. So take the number as directional, not gospel.

Here's why I still think the mechanic underneath it is worth twenty minutes of your time. Strip away the relationship framing, and what you actually have is: a short, personalised quiz, built and hosted for almost nothing, that uses a free-then-paywalled result to convert a stranger into either an email address or a payment, in one sitting, with no sales call and no advertising required to make the first sale. That mechanic isn't new — quizzes have been a growth-marketing staple for a decade — but bolting an AI-generated, genuinely personalised readout onto the end of it is what's changed. It's the difference between "you got put in bucket three of eight" and something that reads like it was actually written about you.

Now here's where this gets interesting for you specifically, and this is the bit I actually want you to build, not clone.

PlayFundWin already runs on exactly this mechanic, you just call it a prize draw instead of a quiz. Someone shows up, does a small thing, and gets something back, with an upgrade path in the middle. What if the "small thing" for a club's supporter base was a quick, genuinely fun quiz — "what kind of supporter are you", "guess your team's next result", "how well do you actually know your own club" — that ends with a free, shareable result card, and a call to action to enter that week's live prize draw for a proper reward. You already have the audience. You already have the prize infrastructure. What you don't have yet is the quiz layer that gets someone who wasn't already thinking about entering to click through, because a personality-style quiz shared by a friend converts completely differently to a plain "enter our prize draw" banner.

Build steps, roughly. One, pick a genuinely fun, low-stakes topic tied to a specific club or league on LeaguePages, not a generic one — specificity is what makes something shareable, a quiz "for football fans" gets ignored, a quiz "for anyone who's ever stood on the touchline at under-twelves on a Sunday morning" gets shared. Two, build the actual quiz flow with Claude: eight to ten multiple choice questions, a scoring function, and a short prompt that turns the score into a personalised written result — this part genuinely is close to entirely buildable by an AI coding agent in a matter of days, it's a form, some logic, one API call for the personalised text, and a results page. Three, put the entry to your live prize draw behind the "get your full result" button, rather than a payment, since for PlayFundWin the monetisable thing isn't the quiz itself, it's the fundraising entries and donor data it feeds. Four, ship it to one club first, on their existing WhatsApp group or social page, and watch what the share rate actually looks like before you build a second one.

Let me actually paint the picture, because a good example beats an abstract description every time. Imagine you've picked a specific under fourteens team. Question one might be "what's your matchday role, even if nobody's ever officially given you one" with answers like the loud one on the touchline, the one who brings the half-time oranges, the one filming it for the parents' group, or the one who's already worrying about next week's fixture. Question four might be about what actually gets you out of bed on a freezing Sunday morning. None of these questions need to be clever. They need to be specifically, recognisably about that club, because that's what makes someone tag three teammates in the comments rather than scrolling past.

Let me make that a bit more concrete on the technical side too, because "build a quiz" is one of those instructions that sounds simple and then quietly eats a weekend if you don't scope it first. The actual build is four small pieces, and none of them are technically hard on their own. A front end that shows one question at a time and stores the answers, which is standard web form work. A scoring function that turns eight answers into one of, say, six result categories, which is a lookup table, not artificial intelligence at all. One call out to an AI model that takes the category plus the club's name and turns it into two or three warm, specific sentences, which is the only part doing anything clever. And a results screen with a share button and your prize draw call to action underneath it. If you can describe that in four sentences to Claude, which you now more or less can, you can have a working version by the end of a single sitting, and then spend the rest of the week just making the questions and the copy genuinely good, which is honestly the harder part.

Honest money maths, kept conservative. Hosting and a domain will run you next to nothing, call it ten to fifteen pounds a month. The AI cost per quiz completion for the personalised text is a matter of pennies, even at real volume, if you keep the prompt tight. There's no advertising spend required to test this, because you're launching it into an audience you already own. The actual return here isn't quiz revenue — there isn't any, by design, in this version — it's the uplift in prize draw entries and email captures per campaign, which only shows up once you've actually run it and measured it against a normal week. That's the honest answer: I can't hand you a revenue number for this version, because nobody has published one for this specific use, which is also exactly why it's worth testing quietly on one club before you tell anyone it's a strategy.

The risks, plainly. Quiz virality is notoriously hit or miss, and what works for one audience can fall completely flat for another with no obvious reason why. An AI-generated "personalised" result can feel gimmicky and generic fast if the prompt behind it isn't actually good, and a bad first impression on a small club's page burns trust you can't easily get back — clubs are small, tight-knit communities, and a naff or spammy-feeling quiz spreads word of mouth just as fast as a good one, just in the other direction. And the underlying evidence for the eleven thousand dollar figure is thin, a single post over thirty days, so don't mistake this for a proven category the way some of our previous deep dives have been. If the whole thing falls flat, the actual cost of finding that out is one afternoon of writing questions and a few pounds of hosting, which is precisely why it's the sort of experiment worth running rather than debating in a meeting first.

Your edge, and it's a real one: a cold-start quiz site has to build an audience from nothing before any of this works. You already have club networks, existing supporter lists, and a live prize draw product to plug the payoff into. You're not testing whether quizzes can drive traffic in general, you're testing whether one specific mechanic increases entries inside an audience you already have permission to talk to. That's a much smaller, much cheaper experiment than the one Bryce Edwards had to run.

Three runner-ups, more briefly, for anyone who'd rather look elsewhere this week, or who just wants a sturdier evidence trail than today's main pick can offer. HeadshotPro, built by Danny Postma, generates professional headshots from a handful of selfies, and according to both Starter Story and a separate breakdown on Indie Hackers, it reached three hundred thousand dollars a month in revenue, around three point six million a year, having taken roughly thirty days to build the first version. Strong evidence, from two independent sources, but it's a crowded category now precisely because it's so well known, so a straight copy is a hard sell. SpeechyAI, built by UK entrepreneur Heidi Ellert-McDermott, writes wedding speeches, and a press release marking its first anniversary states it sold over a thousand speeches in its first twelve months. That's a volume figure rather than a disclosed revenue number, worth noting. And Aftershoot, built by Harshit Dwivedi and Anshul Kumar, helps event photographers cull and edit huge batches of photos automatically, and the company's own year end post says it processed eight point eight billion images for over a hundred and eighty eight thousand photographers last year. Part four. One action today.

Here's the one thing worth doing before you close this episode, and it should take you well under thirty minutes. Open a blank document and write down eight quiz questions for one specific club or league you already work with on LeaguePages — not a generic sports quiz, one built around the in-jokes and specifics of that actual club. Don't build anything yet. Just write the questions, and a rough idea of what a "fun, shareable" result might say for each combination of answers. That's the entire task. If the questions make you smile while you're writing them, that's actually a decent signal the audience will share it. If they feel like homework to write, they'll feel like homework to answer, and nobody shares homework.

While you're at it, jot down which club you'd actually test it on first, and why. Pick the one with the most active WhatsApp group or social page, not necessarily the biggest club — reach matters more than size for a first test, and a smaller, chattier club will give you a cleaner read on whether the thing gets shared at all before you spend any more time on it.
