First up, and the one I think matters most long-term: Anthropic opened a research preview of something called the Model Hardware Standard. In plain terms, it's a shared specification that lets an AI agent safely operate physical devices — lab equipment, robotic arms, microscopes — through one unified driver interface, instead of every piece of kit needing its own bespoke integration. Anthropic's own post claims this cuts hardware-integration time from weeks or months down to hours, and frames the first use cases around scientific labs specifically: a research assistant that can queue up a microscope, run a plate reader, and log the results without a human touching a single dial. This landed on Wednesday the twenty-sixth. Why you care: this is the same shift Computer Use and Browser Use represented a few weeks back, except now it's reaching past the screen into anything with a plug. If you've ever looked at a piece of specialist equipment in your business — a card-payment terminal, a stock-scanning gun, a ticket printer, even a simple turnstile counter at a club gate — and wondered whether an AI agent could just run it directly instead of you keying data in by hand, this is the standard that starts making that plausible. It's early and it's aimed at labs first, so don't expect a plug-and-play version for your till next month, but it's the direction of travel worth watching.

Today's deep dive is a category I haven't covered on this show before: AI answer-engine visibility monitoring. Some people call it GEO — generative engine optimisation — and the pitch is simple. When someone asks ChatGPT, Gemini, or Perplexity a question your business could answer, does your name actually come up? Or does a competitor get recommended instead, while you never even know it happened? A small monitoring tool solves that: it runs a set of realistic questions through the big AI chat engines on a schedule, logs whether and how your brand gets mentioned against your competitors, and puts it on a simple dashboard.

The evidence for this one is unusually well-documented, because three different builders have published real numbers at three completely different scales. The clearest is a builder who goes by Yogesh online, who noticed his own job board was getting referral traffic directly from ChatGPT and built a monitoring tool called Promptmonitor as a minimum viable product in about three weeks. It sat flat around five hundred dollars a month in recurring revenue for roughly eight months, through most of last year — and then, as the whole AI-search-visibility category caught fire, it took off. He listed the business in early January this year for around a hundred thousand dollars asking price, and it sold nine days later for eighty-five thousand dollars. That trajectory — three weeks to build, eight months of nothing much, then a fast exit once the category turned — is exactly the kind of realistic, non-hyped story I want to bring you. It's also worth sitting with why the flat eight months happened at all: the tool was fine, but almost nobody was searching for "AI visibility monitoring" yet, because almost nobody outside the AI-tools crowd realised ChatGPT and friends were already sending them real customers. The moment that awareness caught up with the product, growth followed. That's a pattern worth remembering any time you're tempted to give up on something after a slow start — sometimes the product's ready before the market notices it needs to be.

It's corroborated at a bigger scale too. Otterly dot A I, co-founded by Thomas Peham, bootstrapped with no outside investment and reached an estimated seven hundred and seventy thousand dollars in annual recurring revenue by October last year, seven employees, with a partnership deal alongside Semrush announced back in January — Semrush being one of the biggest names in traditional search-engine optimisation, effectively endorsing this whole category as the natural next step once you've already optimised for Google. And a builder named Cameron Trew — who already runs a LinkedIn personal-branding tool called Kleo — spun up a sister product called Mentions doing the same brand-monitoring job, and as of the end of last year was sitting at twenty thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue, aiming for a hundred thousand a month across this year, according to his own post on Indie Hackers. Three separate people, three different outcomes — a small flip, a mid-size steady business, and a fast-growing new entrant — all built around the same core mechanic, which is about as good as evidence gets in this game: not one lucky outlier, but a pattern that keeps repeating whenever someone builds it.

And that mechanic is refreshingly simple to build. You need a set of realistic questions people actually type into AI chat tools, a scheduled job that fires those questions at ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity through their APIs, a bit of logic that scans the responses for brand mentions — yours and named competitors — and a dashboard that shows the trend over time. None of that requires anything exotic. It's an API-calling script, a database table, and a chart. A weekend, realistically, if you're using Claude to write the scaffolding, and well under a hundred pounds in AI-API costs to run for a month while you're testing it.

Break the build down and it's four separate, small pieces, each one a single afternoon at most. Piece one: write and gather your question bank — twenty to thirty realistic phrasings, sourced from what clubs actually ask on forums and social groups, not what you assume they'd ask. Piece two: a scheduled script, running maybe twice a week, that sends each question to the three main chat APIs and saves the raw response text with a timestamp. Piece three: a simple text-matching pass — does the response contain your brand name, a competitor's name, or neither — logged into a database table alongside which platform said it. Piece four: a dashboard, which can be as basic as a spreadsheet with a chart for the first version, showing mention rate over time per platform. Claude can write almost all four pieces from a plain-English description of what you want each one to do; your job is mostly deciding what questions go in the bank and what "counts" as a mention.

Here's where it gets properly interesting for you specifically. Build this not as a generic SaaS tool aimed at marketing agencies — that market's filling up fast — but as an AI-visibility audit specifically for grassroots sports clubs and small charities, sold through LeaguePages to the club network you already have relationships with. Ask ChatGPT "how do I raise money for my youth football club" or "best grassroots football league near Manchester" and see who gets recommended. Right now, almost nobody in that space is checking, because almost nobody in that space knows this is even a thing worth checking. You'd be selling clubs a monthly report: are you showing up when a parent or a sponsor asks an AI chatbot for a recommendation, and if not, what's the one thing to fix. PlayFundWin gets an obvious extension of the same idea — track whether AI tools recommend prize-draw fundraising as an option at all when someone asks how to fund a sports club, which doubles as market research for where PlayFundWin sits in that conversation.

Honest money maths: charge clubs somewhere around fifteen to twenty-five pounds a month for a simple monthly visibility report — that's affordable for a volunteer-run committee, and even fifty clubs gets you close to a thousand pounds a month in recurring revenue on a product with low running costs, mostly API calls that should stay under a hundred pounds a month even at that scale. Push it to two hundred clubs, which is a realistic ceiling if LeaguePages' existing network is anywhere near what I think it is, and you're looking at three to four thousand pounds a month before you've spent a penny on advertising, because the distribution is already built.

The real risk isn't technical, it's distribution — you need real questions that real parents and sponsors actually ask, not marketing-department guesses, or the reports will feel generic and clubs will churn after month one. And OpenAI, Google, and the rest could change how their answer engines cite sources at any point, which would mean rebuilding your detection logic — a fair chance in a market this new, and worth building the monitoring logic modular from day one so a single provider's format change doesn't take the whole thing down. There's also a subtler risk worth naming: this only stays valuable if AI chat tools keep growing as a place people actually go for local recommendations rather than search engines. That trend looks solid right now, but a business built entirely on one behavioural shift is worth watching for signs it's slowing, not just assuming it'll keep accelerating forever.

Where you've got a real edge over a stranger trying this cold: you already have the club relationships through LeaguePages, and you already have the fundraising-sector credibility through PlayFundWin. Most people building in this space are starting from zero distribution and guessing at a market. You're starting with an actual list of prospective customers who already trust you for something adjacent.

Worth noting on pricing, too: Otterly and the bigger players in this space charge agencies and mid-size brands anywhere from around one hundred to several hundred dollars a month, because they're selling into marketing departments with proper budgets. A volunteer-run under-twelves football club doesn't have a marketing department, let alone a budget line for one — which is exactly why nobody's built a version priced for them yet. You're not competing with Otterly. You're serving a customer Otterly was never going to bother with, at a price point that would look like a rounding error on their spreadsheet and looks like a real product on yours.
