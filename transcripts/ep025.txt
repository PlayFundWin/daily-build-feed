And somewhere out there a man who says he'd barely written a line of code before this year built a tool in a weekend that's now clearing thirty thousand dollars a month from people who sell on Amazon.

Let's get into it.

First, the lawyers. On Friday the twenty-ninth of August, Sony Music Publishing, Warner Chappell and a group of other music publishers filed suit against Anthropic in federal court in California. Their claim, in their own words, accuses Anthropic of running a brazen campaign of illegally torrenting, scraping and downloading copyrighted material to train Claude — this time books containing song lyrics and sheet music, rather than the recordings themselves. It follows a similar suit from Concord and Universal Music back in January, and it lands eight months after Anthropic agreed to pay one and a half billion dollars to settle a separate case brought by authors — a settlement where the court drew a careful line: training a model on copyrighted work might be defensible, but acquiring that work through piracy is not. Anthropic's response so far is one flat sentence: it disagrees with the publishers' claims and intends to defend itself robustly in court.

Here's why that matters if you don't own a record label. Every business built on top of a large language model — yours included, if you're using Claude, ChatGPT or anything similar to write copy, build tools or answer customer questions — is sitting on legal ground that's still being argued over, publisher by publisher, dataset by dataset. The distinction the courts have already drawn, in that earlier authors' settlement, is a useful one to hold onto: training a model on copyrighted material can be lawful use, but how the company got hold of that material in the first place can still land it in court regardless. That's a distinction that will matter far beyond music and books, as more industries work out what they think their own back-catalogue is worth to an AI company. Nothing here should stop you building today. But it's a nudge to keep your own content and data practices clean, because the industry above you is still working out exactly where its own lines sit.

Second, a fast follow-up to a story we covered on Saturday. If you remember, OpenAI announced it's cutting Cursor off from GPT models by the twelfth of November, after SpaceX bought Cursor's parent company, Anysphere — OpenAI cited concerns over what it called SpaceX and Elon Musk's compliance history. Anthropic didn't wait long to respond. Less than two hours after that announcement, Anthropic's Chief Compute Officer, Tom Brown, posted publicly that Cursor has been, in his words, a trusted partner of Anthropic since the days of Claude Sonnet three point five, and that Anthropic will keep increasing the compute it makes available to support Claude models inside Cursor. Read between the lines and that's Anthropic positioning itself as the default model Cursor falls back on once the OpenAI relationship ends.

If you build with Cursor, or you're thinking about starting, this is useful to know now rather than in November. Claude is shaping up to be the safe long-term bet inside that particular tool, and Anthropic putting its own Chief Compute Officer's name to a public promise of more capacity is a stronger signal than a routine partnership announcement would have been — companies don't usually rush a named executive onto a public post within two hours unless they're genuinely worried about losing ground to a rival. And if any of your own workflows are quietly leaning on GPT models through Cursor, consider this your two-and-a-half-month warning to start testing the Claude equivalent before the switch gets forced on you, rather than discovering the gap the week it actually happens.

Third, and this one's properly actionable today rather than just interesting. Anthropic put out a security warning on Sunday the thirtieth about infostealer malware — nasty little programs with names like Vidar, LummaC2, StealC and RedLine on Windows, and Atomic Stealer on a handful of Macs — that are stealing people's live Claude login sessions straight off infected machines. The unpleasant clever part is that this doesn't need your password at all. It steals the actual session — the cookie that already says you're logged in — which means it skips two-factor authentication entirely. Anthropic is signing affected accounts out, pulling saved payment details, and refunding unauthorised charges where it finds them. But, and Anthropic says this themselves, logging someone out doesn't remove the malware from their machine. It can simply come back and steal the next session too.

If you or anyone on your team uses Claude for business work, take two minutes today and check your account for devices or sessions you don't recognise, and for any charges that don't look like yours. It's worth doing this from a device you trust rather than the machine you're worried about, in case that machine is the compromised one. Windows machines appear to be carrying most of the actual infections here, with only a small number of confirmed Mac cases, so if your team is mixed hardware, prioritise the Windows laptops first. And the old, boring advice still holds: don't download pirated software on any machine that's logged into a paid tool with your card attached. One of the confirmed cases Anthropic traced started exactly that way.

One more to file away rather than dwell on. The Wall Street Journal reported today that SB Energy, a SoftBank subsidiary building a large data-centre campus in Ohio for OpenAI, handed OpenAI stock warrants worth three and a half billion dollars back in January, now worth five and a half billion, purely to lock OpenAI in as its anchor tenant. It's financial plumbing, not product news, but it's a useful reminder of how much capital is being nailed down behind the scenes to keep these tools running at roughly the price and speed you currently enjoy — and a reminder that when a frontier lab's pricing suddenly shifts, cheaper or dearer, a deal exactly like this one is usually sitting somewhere underneath it.

Everyone else went quiet in the last two days. Google, Cursor, Replit, Lovable, Bolt and ElevenLabs — I checked each of their own changelogs directly, and there's nothing new dated in this window. Said plainly rather than padded out: this was a loud stretch for lawyers and security teams, and an unusually thin one for shipped features.

Now the part of the show where we go deep. Today's build idea steps away from documents and dashboards and heads into the unglamorous back office of selling things on Amazon.

The tool is called Launch Fast. It's a Chrome extension, paired with a small agent-facing server behind the scenes, that does AI-driven product research for Amazon sellers — pulling market data, scoring potential products, and collapsing hours of spreadsheet guesswork into something closer to a conversation with an AI. The founder is Hasaam Bhatti, who also runs his own small Amazon brand on the side and builds tools for a coaching programme called LegacyX.

Here's the evidence, and I'll tell you exactly where it's strong and where I'd take it with a pinch of salt. The primary source is a first-person account Bhatti wrote for Indie Hackers back in June, and every number in it is his own, not independently audited. In his words: by day thirty, Launch Fast had hit ten thousand dollars in monthly recurring revenue. By day sixty, seventeen to eighteen thousand. By day ninety, twenty-one thousand eight hundred. And as of writing, he says the business sits at thirty thousand dollars a month, with three hundred and thirty active users spread across three pricing tiers — forty-nine, eighty-nine and one hundred and ninety-nine dollars a month. His own website independently confirms the product is real, describes the same function, and lists the tech stack behind it: Claude, Next-dot-J-S, Python and JavaScript, wrapped in a Chrome extension.

Now, the pinch of salt. Bhatti's Indie Hackers post frames him as someone who'd never written a line of code before this project. His own site, in the same breath, says he's spent close to a decade doing blockchain development and Amazon selling, and that he now writes more code with AI than without it. Those two self-descriptions don't quite square with each other. He's clearly self-taught and outside the traditional computer science path — but "never touched a computer" and "a decade in blockchain and e-commerce" aren't the same story. Take the ninety-day revenue climb at face value. Just don't picture a total novice pulling it off.

On to how it was actually built, because this is the useful part for anyone actually planning to sit down and do this. His own account: the initial version came together in forty-eight hours using Cursor for the coding, Vercel for hosting, Supabase for the database and login, Resend for onboarding emails, and Apify to pull the underlying Amazon data — all fairly standard, mostly free-tier-friendly tools that any of you could open an account with by lunchtime. For the harder engineering days that followed, he says he ran two AI coding assistants side by side depending on the task, switching between them rather than picking one and forcing every problem to fit it — a habit worth stealing regardless of what you're building. The thirty-thousand-dollar version, three months later, obviously has a lot more built into it than a weekend project ever could. Be honest with yourself about that gap. The forty-eight-hour build is real. It's the seed, not the finished tree, and the distance between those two states is where most of the actual work — and most people's actual quitting — happens.

So what would you actually build, realistically, this week? Not the whole platform — the ad-optimisation and catalogue-management pieces are a much bigger lift than a solo build justifies starting with. The sensible slice is narrower: an AI tool that takes a seller's current product listing and rewrites the title, the bullet points and the backend search terms against whatever actually ranks in that category, using Claude to do the rewriting and either scraped or manually pasted competitor listings to ground it in reality. Package that as a simple web form, or a lightweight browser extension if you want to go further. You could have a working first version live by the weekend.

Now the money maths, kept deliberately conservative and boring. Getting started costs you almost nothing: a Claude or Cursor subscription at roughly twenty pounds a month, free tiers on Vercel and Supabase to begin with, and a small budget — call it twenty to thirty pounds — for a scraping service like Apify if you need to pull live listing data rather than working from whatever a seller pastes in themselves. All in, comfortably under two hundred pounds to get a working version live, well inside the five-hundred-pound ceiling we set for ourselves on this show. Charge even ten sellers thirty pounds a month for a basic listing-rewrite service, and that's three hundred pounds a month in revenue against essentially no fixed costs. Not thirty thousand dollars. Not yet. But real, working proof that people will pay for it — which is the actual hard part that most people never get past.

Name the risks rather than skate past them, because they're real. Amazon changes its search algorithm and category structure fairly regularly, so anything you build needs ongoing upkeep, not a one-off build-and-forget — budget an hour or two a month to check your prompts and rules still match how the category is actually ranking, rather than assuming day-one accuracy holds forever. Scraping Amazon data sits in a legal and technical grey area — services like Apify exist specifically to manage that risk for you, but you're still one terms-of-service change away from a genuine headache, so lean on a compliant provider rather than scraping Amazon directly yourself. And the market is more crowded than it first looks from the outside — plenty of Amazon-seller tools already exist, some with venture funding behind them and years of head start. That cuts both ways: it proves real demand, but it means your edge has to be meaningfully narrower and sharper than "yet another listing tool," not broader — a single category, a single problem, done properly, beats a general-purpose tool built by one person competing against a funded team's general-purpose tool.

So does this niche onto PlayFundWin, LeaguePages or EV-Partnerships? Honestly, no — and I'd rather tell you that straight than force a connection that isn't there. This is an e-commerce tool built for people who sell on Amazon, and none of your three ventures sell on Amazon. If I stretch for a link at all, it's a thin one: some grassroots clubs run kit and merchandise shops through Etsy or Amazon Handmade to raise a bit of extra money on the side, and a stripped-down version of this — an AI tool that rewrites a club's shop listings so they actually get found — is technically adjacent. But that's a stretch, not a strategy, and I'd rather flag it as a stretch than dress it up as a perfect fit. Sometimes the best idea on this show is simply a good idea on its own terms, not one wearing one of your three ventures' logos.

Three runner-ups, quickly, all tied to named founders with published figures. Sales-dot-co, built by Ryan Doyle and Jakob Greenfeld, combines scraped prospect lists with AI-written cold email copy, sold as a monthly retainer to business clients — Indie Hackers has them going from five hundred dollars a month to ninety thousand dollars a month, and past a million dollars in annual recurring revenue, all while the founders were travelling. It's a genuinely large jump, and worth remembering that a retainer model like theirs means the revenue is recurring service income, not a one-off tool sale — closer to running a small agency with AI doing the heavy lifting than to shipping software. ColdClicks, built by William Paul, does something similar at a much smaller, more relatable scale — six hundred dollars in monthly recurring revenue within six weeks of launch, according to his own Indie Hackers post, a far more realistic first milestone to aim at than either of the other two. And Gamma, the AI presentation and slide-deck generator built by Grant Lee, is the outlier of the three — venture-funded rather than bootstrapped, and reportedly sitting at a hundred million dollars in annual recurring revenue with a team of fifty, according to TechCrunch. I'm not suggesting you clone Gamma. I'm pointing at it as proof that AI-generated presentations are a large and proven market, and that a tiny, sharply niched sliver of it — say, an AI deck generator built specifically for grassroots sponsorship pitches or grant applications — is worth ten minutes of your thinking time this week, even if the full platform is obviously out of reach for a solo build.

Let's bring this back to your own three businesses.

Start with the security story, because it applies to all three of them at once. PlayFundWin, LeaguePages and EV-Partnerships all lean on AI tooling somewhere in the stack — content, admin, code, all of it. That infostealer warning isn't abstract. If anyone on your team, or you yourself, uses Claude on a personal machine that also handles ordinary browsing and downloads, this is the week to check account activity across all three ventures' AI logins — not as a one-off, but as a standing five-minute habit going forward. It costs nothing, and it closes a door that malware is actively walking through right now, on other people's accounts if not yet on yours. Worth extending the same two-minute check to any shared or team logins on Claude Code sessions specifically, since those often carry more permissions — repository access, deployment keys — than a single chat account ever would, and a stolen session there does more damage than a stolen session on a normal account.

On the Cursor and Anthropic story: if any of LeaguePages' build tooling touches Cursor — and given how much of this show is about building fast with AI coding tools, it's worth checking — you now have a clearer signal than you had on Saturday. Anthropic wants to be the model sitting inside Cursor once OpenAI's access cuts off in November. Worth a five-minute audit of any Cursor-based workflows to confirm they're already pointed at Claude models rather than anything that stops working on the twelfth of November.

On PlayFundWin specifically: the DCMS call for evidence on how National Lottery good-causes money gets distributed is still open, still on track to close on the twenty-third of September — a little over three weeks from today. Nothing new happened on it this week, which is fine; it means the window hasn't moved and you haven't missed anything. Sport currently holds a fixed twenty per cent statutory share of that funding, split between Sport England, UK Sport and the devolved nations, and this is the first review of the whole system since nineteen ninety-four. That's the kind of once-a-generation review where the loudest voices in the room tend to be national charities with policy teams whose entire job is writing consultation responses — grassroots operators with the actual on-the-ground evidence are usually the quietest, simply because nobody on a small team has a spare afternoon for it. If PlayFundWin has a view on how digital, tech-enabled fundraising like prize draws should factor into that funding mix going forward, three weeks is still enough time to put together a proper written response, built around the actual numbers you're sitting on — clubs signed up, funds raised, the administrative overhead a digital platform removes compared with a bucket collection or a raffle book. That's not a five-minute email, so if you want to do it justice, this is the week to start drafting rather than the week before it closes.

On EV-Partnerships: nothing new on the charging-point statistics themselves this week — the Department for Transport's numbers are settled for the quarter, and we covered the actual figures on Saturday. What is worth another quick mention is the gap we've now been tracking for eight straight episodes: not one charging operator — not IONITY, not RAW Charging, not Allego, none of them — has put a destination-charging hub at an actual sports or leisure venue. They've gone to retail parks, generic leisure parks, even a Formula E team's own headquarters, but never a football ground, a rugby club or a leisure centre. Allego's hundred-million-euro UK rollout is still prioritising London and major road corridors over anything sports-specific, according to trade coverage this month. Eight episodes of an open gap like that isn't a coincidence. It's either a genuine blind spot in how these operators think about site selection, or evidence that nobody's actually put the numbers in front of them yet. A rough case is easy enough to sketch even without hard data to back it: a sports club car park sits mostly empty on weekday evenings and full on match days, which is a completely different usage pattern to a retail park or a motorway services, and an operator willing to model that pattern properly might find the economics work out better than they expect, not worse. Either way, the gap is still sitting there, unclaimed, and it's been unclaimed for long enough now that patience alone isn't going to close it — somebody has to actually go and make the pitch.

Here's today's one action, and it'll take you under thirty minutes.

Open Claude, paste in the current listing text for any product one of your businesses sells online — even if it's not Amazon, even if it's a LeaguePages merchandise item on Etsy or a PlayFundWin prize listing — and ask it to rewrite the title and the first three bullet points as if it were optimising for a marketplace search algorithm rather than a human skim-reading it. Then ask it one sharper follow-up question: what would someone actually type into the search bar to find this, and does your current listing contain any of those exact words?

That's the whole exercise. It costs nothing, it takes half an hour, and it's the exact core mechanic behind a tool that's reportedly making its founder thirty thousand dollars a month — just done manually, once, for one listing, rather than built as a product for hundreds of sellers. Do it for two or three listings if you've got the time, not just one, since a single result could easily be a fluke of that particular product rather than a pattern worth acting on. If it turns up something useful across more than one listing, that's your evidence the bigger build idea is worth a proper weekend. If it doesn't, you've lost thirty minutes and learned something concrete about your own listings either way, which is still a better use of half an hour than most of what fills a Monday.
