Now, the build.

Today's pick is an odd one, and I mean that as a compliment: an app that looks at a photograph of an insect bite and tells you what probably bit you. It's called Insect Bite ID: AI Scanner, published by a company called Angel App Publishing. You point your camera at a bite or a sting, the AI gives you its best guess, a confidence score, and some care guidance, and it tracks the bite over time as it heals or, worryingly, doesn't.

Here's the evidence, and I want to be upfront about its shape before I give you any numbers. This app is currently listed for sale on a marketplace called TrustMRR, and the founder is identified only by a first name, Matt. I could not find a fuller name published anywhere, so treat "named source" loosely here — this is one step short of the fully verified founder interviews I usually bring you. What I can tell you is properly corroborated, though: the revenue figure comes from RevenueCat, the subscription-billing platform actually running the app's payments, not a number the seller typed into a listing themselves. As of early August, the app was pulling in three thousand eight hundred and forty-eight dollars over the trailing thirty days, with two thousand eight hundred and six dollars in monthly recurring revenue and one hundred and eleven active subscribers. I checked Apple's own App Store listing directly, and it confirms the pricing structure: six dollars ninety-nine a week, or a one-time payment of thirty-four dollars ninety-nine to unlock everything permanently, released back in June last year, currently sitting at three point six out of five stars from one hundred and sixteen ratings.

So this is a case where I'd take the person behind it with a pinch of salt, while trusting the money a fair bit more than usual, because it's coming from the payment processor rather than from someone's own telling of their own story. That's actually a useful general rule for judging any of these business stories, not just this one: a number attributed to Stripe, RevenueCat, or a similar billing platform has already been checked by a computer that doesn't care about anyone's ego, while a number in a tweet or a forum post has been checked by nobody but the person who benefits from you believing it. Neither kind of evidence is worthless, but they don't deserve the same level of trust, and I'll keep flagging which is which as we go.

What actually makes this interesting isn't insect bites at all. It's the mechanic sitting underneath the product: photo in, AI vision classification out, wrapped in a subscription. That pattern doesn't care what the photograph is actually of. Swap the bite for a skin rash, a houseplant disease, a wine label, a car part that needs identifying, a piece of machinery missing its manual — the build behind each of those is nearly identical every single time, and that's the real takeaway to steal, not the insects.

This is also, if you think about it for a second, one of the plainer arguments for why vision-capable AI models have become one of the most useful tools available to a solo builder right now, with surprisingly little fanfare given how much has changed. Two years ago, a product like this needed its own trained image-classification model, a labelled dataset of thousands of bite photos, and someone who actually understood machine learning to stitch it together. Today it's a prompt. The hard, expensive part of the old version of this business has been handed to you for the price of an API call, and the entire remaining job is picking a niche worth photographing and wrapping it in a decent user experience.

Here's how you would actually build a version of this yourself. Skip the app store entirely to start with — build it as a mobile web page instead, so there's no Apple review process to wait through and no developer account fee to pay on day one. You need four pieces, really. First, a page where someone can take or upload a photograph straight from their phone's browser, no app download involved. Second, a call out to a vision-capable AI model, prompted carefully to classify the image, give a confidence score, and explain its own reasoning in plain language rather than just spitting out a label. Third, some sensible guardrails around that prompt: a hard rule telling the model to recommend a doctor whenever it isn't confident, and to never present a guess as a diagnosis. And fourth, Stripe wired up for either a monthly subscription or a pay-per-scan model, whichever fits your idea better. Claude can write essentially all of that scaffolding in a couple of focused days, prompt guardrails included. If you decide later you want a proper native app, Apple's developer fee runs to about eighty pounds a year, which barely dents the five-hundred-pound budget this show always works within.

The honest, conservative money maths: at one hundred and eleven subscribers averaging a shade under twenty-five dollars each a month, you are looking at something that could realistically be run by one person in their spare time, with payment-processing fees eating maybe three to five per cent, and the AI vision API calls being the main variable cost as usage grows. Scale that up gently and honestly rather than dreaming big: get to three hundred subscribers at the same average spend, and you're looking at something in the region of seven and a half thousand dollars a month before costs, still comfortably a spare-time project rather than a full-time job. It is not going to make anyone rich on its own, and I'd be doing you a disservice if I dressed it up otherwise. It is a small, real, and repeatable business, built and run by one person, with numbers a payment processor will actually vouch for.

The risks here are worth naming directly rather than skating past. Health-adjacent AI advice sits in a genuine liability minefield if you are not careful with your disclaimers — this kind of product only really works long-term if it is positioned firmly as informational rather than medical, with a real "go and see a doctor" prompt built into anything that looks remotely serious. The three point six star rating tells you accuracy complaints are already a live issue on the existing app, not a hypothetical one. There's a data-protection angle too, and it matters more here than in most of the builds I bring you: people are uploading photographs of their own skin, sometimes of children's skin, and under UK data protection law that counts as sensitive personal data, which means proper consent wording, a real retention policy, and no quietly training your next model on customer photos without saying so upfront. None of that is expensive to get right, but all of it needs doing before launch, not after a complaint. And a marketplace listing means the current owner is actively trying to exit the business, which is sometimes a warning sign and sometimes just someone moving on to whatever they're building next — from the outside, there's no way for me to tell you which one this is.
