Start with the story that's easiest to picture: Meta's new assistant.

On Tuesday the eighth of September, Meta launched Muse, a personal AI agent that goes further than a chatbot. It books travel, pays bills, fills out forms, turns a recipe into a shopping list, and keeps working after you've closed the app. It runs inside something Meta calls a Muse Secure V M, a sandboxed environment with a separate watchdog agent, Sentinel, monitoring what Muse actually does with your accounts. There's a free metered tier, a twenty-dollar-a-month Power tier, and a hundred-dollar-a-month Maximum tier. It's US-only for now, available through the web, iOS, Android and WhatsApp, with support for Meta's Ray-Ban glasses coming later. I read TechCrunch's direct coverage of this rather than Meta's own announcement, so I'm confident on the facts but this is solid secondary sourcing, not primary. Why you care: this is the first mainstream, do-real-tasks agent from a company with billions of existing users. It won't reach the UK for a while, but it's a preview of what your customers, and eventually your own back office, will expect an assistant to just handle. If Meta gets the trust question right, the bar for "a chatbot that answers questions" moves overnight to "an agent that actually finishes the task," and that's the bar every AI tool bolted onto a small business website will eventually be measured against, including anything PlayFundWin or LeaguePages ever ships.

Also on the eighth, OpenAI pushed out ChatGPT Images two point five. Sharper output, roughly fifty per cent faster generation, and, this is the bit that actually matters for a small operation, much better preservation of people, pets and products across edits, so you can change a background or a caption without the subject warping into something unrecognisable. There's a new sketch tool, so you can rough out a layout by hand and have it built from that reference, ready-made templates for flyers and product shots, and a commenting feature so you can point at one part of an image and ask for just that bit to change. I read OpenAI's own post on this one directly, so it's as solid as sourcing gets. Why you care: if you've ever paid a freelancer forty pounds for a flyer or a product photo touch-up, this closes a good chunk of that gap for free or close to it. Worth a real test rather than a glance, next time you need a poster for a prize draw or a fixture list graphic for a club page, try it first before opening a design tool out of habit.

Now the part of the show where I go looking for evidence, not hype.

Today's pick is Habit Pixel, a habit tracker built by a solo developer named Hirvesh Munogee. The idea is simple enough to explain in one sentence: every habit you complete adds another block to a growing piece of pixel art, so your streak becomes something you can actually watch take shape, rather than just a number ticking up in a list. There's streak tracking, support for habits you're trying to quit as well as ones you're trying to build, and some light automation on top.

The numbers come from a post Munogee published on Indie Hackers back in late January, walking through eight months of real figures. First paying subscriber in mid-May last year. Twenty-eight dollars a month in recurring revenue by June. Two hundred and eight dollars by early November. Four hundred and seven dollars in November itself, up ninety-six per cent, three hundred and forty-one subscribers. Eight hundred and forty dollars in December, up over a hundred per cent again, six hundred and ninety-seven subscribers, four thousand seven hundred and forty-three dollars in total revenue that single month. And by January this year, past a thousand dollars a month in recurring revenue with roughly nine hundred active subscribers. I'll flag this clearly: these are the founder's own numbers on Indie Hackers, not independently verified through a payment processor, so take them as a credible story rather than an audited one. But the month-by-month detail and the consistency of the growth curve make this read like a real business rather than a highlight reel.

One detail worth pulling out on its own: Munogee says he's already using Claude Code in production, specifically to localise the app into twelve languages, and that a new language now takes him about ten minutes of work instead of a proper translation project. That's not a hypothetical use case I'm bolting on for this show, it's the actual founder telling you what tool he reaches for.

Worth a word on how those subscribers actually showed up, because the number is meaningless without it. From what's in the post, growth came from the usual solo-founder playbook rather than any single lucky break: building in public on Indie Hackers and X, posting the pixel-art screenshots themselves as the marketing asset since the product is visually distinctive enough to stop a scroll, and a slow trickle from App Store search once enough reviews accumulated. No paid advertising mentioned anywhere in eight months of numbers. That matters because it tells you the ceiling on cost here isn't just development, it's the founder's own time spent posting, replying, and iterating in public, which is free in pounds and expensive in hours.

So what would it take to build something like this yourself. The honest core of the app is streak logic, a grid that fills in as a picture, push notifications to nudge you back if you miss a day, and a subscription paywall. None of that is exotic. A modern AI coding agent can scaffold a cross-platform app in something like Flutter or Expo, wire up local storage for the habit data, and hook in RevenueCat to handle the App Store and Play Store subscription plumbing without you touching payment code directly. Local notification scheduling, the reminder that fires if you haven't logged today's habit by evening, is a well-trodden path in both frameworks, so that's another piece an agent handles cleanly rather than something you're debugging from scratch. The pixel-art rendering itself is the one properly creative piece, you're either drawing a small library of art assets yourself, commissioning someone for a few hundred pounds, or generating a starting set with an image tool and cleaning it up by hand.

Getting an app through Apple's review process is the one step no AI agent can do for you. Apple looks specifically at subscription apps for a genuine free tier or trial, clear pricing before payment, and a working restore-purchases button, miss any of those and you're into a rejection-and-resubmit cycle that costs you days, not money. Building that into the plan from day one, rather than discovering it on your first submission, is the difference between a one-week build and a three-week one.

Money maths, kept deliberately conservative. An Apple Developer account runs about seventy-nine pounds a year. A Google Play developer account is a one-off twenty-five dollars, call it twenty pounds. Budget another hundred to two hundred pounds for art assets if you're not doing them yourself, and your backend can sit on a free tier for as long as you're small, Supabase and Firebase both offer one. All told you could have a working prototype live for under three hundred pounds, comfortably inside the five-hundred-pound ceiling I try to hold this segment to. The real cost isn't cash, it's the weeks of iteration to get the habit loop and the art actually feel satisfying enough that someone pays for a subscription instead of using the free habit tracker already on their phone.

The risks are exactly what you'd expect from a subscription consumer app. The habit-tracker category is crowded, Streaks, Habitica and a dozen others were here first, so you need a properly distinct hook, and pixel art is Munogee's hook, not automatically yours. App Store and Play Store approval and policy changes sit entirely outside your control. And subscription churn is brutal in this category, people start strong in January and stop opening the app by March, which is exactly the kind of pattern a streak mechanic is designed to fight, but never fully solves.

Where does this actually connect to what Steve's already running. There's no clean, obvious fit here, this isn't an e-commerce or content tool that slots into PlayFundWin or LeaguePages directly, and I'd rather say that outright than force a connection that isn't there. But the underlying mechanic, visible progress tied to a streak, is worth stealing as a feature idea rather than a whole product. Picture a supporter-side streak for PlayFundWin, a visible, shareable marker for someone who's entered a club's prize draw every month for six months running, turning a repeat donor into something closer to a habit than a one-off transaction. Or picture it on LeaguePages as an attendance or volunteering streak for parents and coaches at a grassroots club, the kind of small recognition that costs nothing to build once you already have the sign-in data. Both of those are speculation on my part, nothing built or tested, but they're the sort of two-day prototype worth trying against a single friendly club before you'd ever pitch it as a real feature.

There's a second, less obvious edge worth naming. Steve's ventures already sit on exactly the kind of recurring, low-friction sign-in and transaction data that makes a streak mechanic work in the first place, monthly draw entries, weekly match attendance, regular top-ups. Most solo founders building a habit app have to invent a reason for someone to open it daily from nothing. That data already exists inside PlayFundWin and LeaguePages, sitting unused as a growth lever, which is a genuine head start over Munogee's position when he started Habit Pixel with zero users and zero data.

On to how this actually touches Steve's three businesses, starting with the one that has real paperwork attached this week.

The Fundraising Regulator's registration-threshold consultation is the story I flagged as needing an actual check last time, and I did check. Two things to correct and confirm. First, the threshold itself: I'd previously described this as a flat twenty per cent minimum charitable contribution for free-draw and prize-competition operators, that's not quite right. The Regulator's actual proposal, confirmed through Civil Society and Third Sector's coverage, is phased: ten per cent of ticket sales in the twenty twenty-six to twenty twenty-seven financial year, rising to fifteen per cent the year after, and reaching twenty per cent by twenty twenty-eight to twenty twenty-nine, matching where society lottery minimums already sit. So the eventual number I gave you was right, the timeline to get there was not. Second, the actual status: the Regulator's own consultation page still shows the consultation closed back in April, new-operator registration still suspended, and the page still says reopening is expected in September without confirming it's actually happened yet. So as of today, this is still pending rather than resolved, and I'll check again next time there's real reason to expect movement rather than running this same update on a fixed schedule. Worth saying plainly why this one's different from the prize-draw ruling thread we retired months ago, that one was genuinely stuck with nothing left to say, this one has a live deadline attached and a threshold that actually changes what PlayFundWin pays out, so it earns its airtime until it's actually resolved one way or the other.
