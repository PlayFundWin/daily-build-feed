Right. What shipped.

OpenAI opened up something called GPT-Live-1 this week, a voice model built into the API that listens and talks at the same time instead of doing the usual relay race — hear you, transcribe you, think about it, write a reply, turn that back into speech. That relay race is why every voice bot you've ever shouted at pauses for a beat too long and then talks over you, or barrels on regardless when you try to interrupt it. OpenAI says this cuts bad interruptions by roughly eighty per cent, ships with more than a dozen new voices and accents, and plugs straight into phone lines rather than needing you to bolt a telephony provider onto the side of it yourself. Pricing sits at five cents a minute for the voice layer, on top of whatever the reasoning model underneath costs to run. If you've ever priced up a booking line or a support bot for one of your ventures and choked on the ElevenLabs-plus-stitching bill, or given up entirely because the latency made the whole thing feel like talking to someone down a bad phone line from the nineties, this is the first real alternative worth costing out side by side before you write it off again.

Cursor, the coding tool a fair few of you are already living inside, launched something called Projects on Wednesday. Up to now their agents were sprinters — you gave them a task, they ran it while you watched the terminal scroll, and you checked the output when it was done. Projects is a coordinator that plans out a whole feature or migration, hands pieces of it to subagents running on cloud machines rather than your own laptop, keeps the shared context so nothing gets lost between handoffs, and can sit there subscribed to a Slack channel or a pull request waiting to be triggered without you lifting a finger. Cursor's own numbers claim thirty per cent more pull requests merged for a typical new user, and six times that for their heaviest users. Take a vendor's own benchmark with the usual pinch of salt — nobody publishes the number that makes their product look average — but the underlying shape of it, an agent that manages other agents rather than just executing one task at a time, is the direction this whole category is heading. Worth watching closely if any of your own tooling for LeaguePages or PlayFundWin already lives inside Cursor.

Lovable, the no-code builder, opened a partner programme on Tuesday, wiring up vetted freelance builders and small agencies to companies that want a custom build but don't want to learn the platform themselves. If you've ever thought about hiring someone to bash out a quick internal tool rather than doing it yourself at eleven at night after a full day running three ventures, this is now an actual marketplace with some form of vetting behind it, rather than a DM to a stranger on a forum and a hope for the best. It also tells you something about where Lovable thinks its growth comes from next — not more solo builders teaching themselves the platform from scratch, but a whole layer of professionals building for other people who'd rather pay than learn.

Google shipped a proper native Gemini app for Windows on Wednesday. Alt-plus-space and it's there, no browser tab required, with Workspace document summarising, image generation and research tools built straight in rather than scattered across half a dozen separate web pages. It sounds like a small thing. It isn't, for the audience this show is actually made for — every extra click between "I have a question" and "I get an answer" is a reason your least technical member of staff, the one who still prints emails, never bothers asking the question at all. This removes one of those clicks, and sometimes one click really is the difference between a tool getting used and a tool getting ignored.

And OpenAI had a second announcement this week, opening its Agents API into public beta on Wednesday as well — one API call spins up a production agent on their managed infrastructure, with a choice of sandbox from OpenAI's own hosting or third-party options. This one's more for the developers among you than the send-a-text-and-see-what-happens crowd, but it's the same story as Cursor's Projects: every major lab is racing to make "an agent that runs your task while you do something else" a one-line setup rather than an afternoon of scaffolding.

OpenAI also quietly rolled out a couple of enterprise-flavoured tools this week — a version of ChatGPT tuned for financial-services compliance work, and a new data agent that plugs into a company's own warehouse for business intelligence. Both are real, both are dated this week, and both are aimed squarely at people with a Bloomberg terminal and a compliance department, not a listener running three ventures out of a laptop. Part two. The build.

If yesterday's story was Claude models getting confused about which internet they were on, today's is a lot more grounded: two people, a phone number, and an app that now tracks five million dollars a year in other people's receipts.

The company's called Receipt-AI, and the pitch is almost insultingly simple. You take a photo of a paper receipt — the crumpled one from the hardware shop, the faded thermal one from the petrol station — and you text it. That's the entire user interface. No app to download, no login to remember, nothing to open. The system reads the photo, pulls out the vendor, the date and the amount, files it into the right expense category, and pushes it straight into Xero or QuickBooks so it's sitting there waiting at reconciliation time.

Founders Queenie Peng and Ian Heraty built the first version on Ruby on Rails over about three months, self-funded, no outside money. A Starter Story interview has them at one and a half thousand dollars a month in revenue not long after launch — self-reported, so take it as a data point rather than gospel, but it's corroborated independently by a case study from We Are Founders UK, which lines up on the founders' names and the mechanics of the product. And the founders' own blog, published this past May, gives the longer arc: past a hundred businesses using it, more than twelve thousand receipts processed, and five million dollars of total expenses tracked across two years. Three separate sources, over two years, all telling roughly the same story — that's a stronger pattern than most single-post claims you'll hear on this show, even without a Stripe-verified number behind it.

Here's why it's buildable fast, and why it's buildable by you specifically. The stack is almost embarrassingly modern for something that took three months to build the hard way back when Peng and Heraty started. Break it into four pieces and none of them is hard on its own. First, a phone number from Twilio set up to receive picture messages, which is a few lines of webhook code an AI coding agent will write for you in minutes. Second, when a photo lands, it gets handed straight to Claude with a simple instruction: read this receipt, extract the vendor, the date, the total, and take a best guess at a spending category. Third, a small lookup table that maps whatever category Claude suggests onto the categories your accounting software actually uses, because "coffee and biscuits" needs to become "subsistence" or "sundries" before anyone downstream is happy. And fourth, a one-way sync that pushes the finished record into Xero or QuickBooks through their own published API, so the treasurer or the bookkeeper opens their software at month-end and the entry is simply sitting there, done. None of that needs a team, and none of it needs the three months Peng and Heraty spent building their own OCR pipeline by hand — that was the hard, expensive part of their build, and it's now something Claude does for pennies. Realistically it's a focused weekend, a Twilio number costing about a pound a month, and an API budget that'll run you a few pence per receipt processed. Call it comfortably under five hundred pounds to get a working version in front of your first ten users, and most of that budget is buffer, not necessity.

Now, the honest money maths, not the enthusiastic version. If you charged fifteen pounds a month per user — reasonable for a self-employed tradesperson who currently loses an afternoon a month hunting for receipts down the back of the van — your running costs at even a hundred active users would still only be a few hundred pounds a month in Twilio and API fees, leaving most of that fifteen pounds as margin. The catch, as always, is getting from zero users to a hundred. You'd need roughly twenty paying customers just to clear your running costs with room to spare, and that's before you've spent a penny or an hour on actually finding those twenty people. Getting to Receipt-AI's own five-million-dollar mark took two people two years of actual grinding — steady growth, not a weekend and a viral post. Anyone telling you this kind of thing happens overnight on this show would be lying to you, so I won't.

Where it gets interesting for you specifically is that you already have the audience Peng and Heraty had to build from a standing start. Grassroots club treasurers are exactly the people this problem is worst for — volunteers, not accountants, holding a shoebox of receipts for pitch hire, kit orders and referee fees, expected to produce a clean set of accounts for the AGM. Build the text-a-receipt mechanic straight into LeaguePages as a treasurer's tool, and you're not selling to strangers on the internet — you're selling to people who already trust the platform and already have the exact pain this app was built to solve. That's the edge a cold-start founder doesn't get.

The risks are worth saying out loud rather than glossing over, because every build idea on this show gets the same treatment and this one's no exception. Real-world receipts are a nightmare for any vision model — faded thermal ink that's gone grey after a week in a wallet, a crease straight through the total, a handwritten amount scrawled on the back of a till roll because the printer ran out. That accuracy has to be tested against genuinely bad receipts, not clean demo photos taken under good lighting, before you trust it with anyone's actual books. You're also now handling other people's financial documents, which in the UK means thinking about data protection properly rather than as an afterthought bolted on after launch — this isn't a toy app, it's someone's tax return waiting to happen, and getting that wrong has real consequences for real people. And you're not walking into an empty market: Dext and Expensify already do a version of this, with real budgets, real sales teams and real integrations behind them. Your entire wedge is that neither of them lets someone text a photo and be done with it — everyone else still wants you to download an app, create an account, and remember to open it. If AI receipt-reading turns into commodity technology inside the next year or two, and every accounting package just builds this in for free, that wedge gets thin fast. Today, while nobody's accounting software does this natively, it's a real one, and worth moving on while it still is.

Three other ideas came up in research, each with its own asterisk attached.

Quandri is two brothers, Jackson and Jamieson Fregeau, who built AI "digital workers" that check insurance policies for brokers at renewal time, flagging anything that's changed between last year's policy and this year's. Starter Story has their early numbers at twenty-seven to thirty thousand dollars a month, built on credit cards, first paying customer inside three weeks — proper scrappy stuff. Since then they've raised twelve million dollars and grown a real staff, so that revenue figure now belongs to a funded company that no longer looks anything like a weekend build. Take the early insight instead of trying to copy the current company: a much smaller version of the same idea — an AI that reads two versions of any document, a contract, a policy, a set of terms and conditions, and tells you in plain language exactly what changed between them — is a real gap in warranty and insurance-claim admin that nobody on this show has touched yet, and it's a far smaller build than the full brokerage platform Quandri has since grown into.

ReviewFlowz is a Slack bot from founder Axel Lavergne that pulls in customer reviews from sites like G2 and Capterra and pings your team the moment a new one lands, good or bad. The evidence is thin — seven hundred and ten dollars a month, from a Starter Story piece published back in twenty twenty-two, built in three weeks with a co-founder who'd since left the business — and it's a single, dated, self-reported figure with nothing else to corroborate it. Still real, still named, just small and a little stale. The distinction from the testimonial widgets we've already covered on this show is that this one watches and alerts rather than displaying — closer to a reputation early-warning system for your inbox than a trust badge bolted onto your homepage. For any of your three ventures, a version of this watching local review sites and social mentions rather than G2 would be the more relevant build.

And then there's AI Publisher Pro, which I'll flag with more scepticism than usual, because this one has a tell that the other three don't. Founder Joe Popelas told Starter Story he built an AI-generated-book publishing business to eighty-three thousand three hundred dollars a month, starting with five hundred dollars, using ChatGPT and a landing page and Pinterest ads. The bit that should make you cautious rather than excited: Popelas also sells a paid course teaching this exact method, under the same name. That's not proof he's lying — plenty of honest founders sell courses too — but it's a direct financial incentive to round the numbers up, and I can't independently verify a single dollar of it against anything outside his own telling of the story. The underlying mechanic, AI-written niche non-fiction sold through Amazon's self-publishing platform with a thin paid-ads funnel behind it, is a real and well-worn playbook that's been around since well before this show started, long before anyone was using AI to write the books themselves. The specific number attached to it today should sit in your head with an asterisk the size of a house, even while the mechanic underneath it stays perfectly sound.

Part four. One action for today.

Skip the diary and the spreadsheets for once. Take an actual receipt out of your wallet or your car — ideally the worst one you've got, creased, a bit faded, not a clean demo shot taken specially for the purpose — photograph it, and drop it into Claude with a simple prompt: read this receipt and tell me the vendor, the date and the total. Time how long it takes, and check by hand whether it actually got the total right, not just whether it produced something that looked plausible. If it nails a genuinely bad receipt first try, that's a strong signal the whole mechanic is worth an actual weekend. If it fumbles the total or invents a vendor name that isn't there, you've learned that cheaply, before spending a single hour building anything. That's the entire test of whether the Receipt-AI mechanic is worth chasing further — and it costs you nothing but a phone and a receipt you were probably about to throw away anyway.
