Three of the biggest labs in artificial intelligence just sat down together to talk about slowing each other down on purpose.

One of them is also, according to people who were apparently in the room but won't put their names to it, lining up an autumn stock market listing worth more than most of the world's economies.

Meanwhile, a one-person publishing business built almost entirely on AI-generated colouring books and planners is clearing seventeen and a half thousand dollars a month, verified against the actual payment processor, not a screenshot. This is the Daily Build, your daily AI business briefing, for Monday the fourteenth of September, twenty twenty-six. As always: research and ideas here, not financial advice.

Start with what actually happened over the weekend, because most of it wasn't a new product at all. It was the industry talking about itself, out loud, in public.

On Saturday, in an interview with Fortune, OpenAI's Sam Altman ruled out taking the company public this year. His words: right now would be an ill-advised moment to go public, not twenty twenty-six, we've got a lot of stuff to do. He tied that directly to the same safety and pacing argument Anthropic's Dario Amodei made in the essay we covered on this show yesterday. Read between the lines and "a lot of stuff to do" sounds like code for more model releases and a harder push into enterprise deals before anyone has to answer to public shareholders every quarter. It's also worth remembering OpenAI has downplayed IPO timing before, only for insiders to leak the opposite a few months later, so treat "not this year" as this week's position, not a permanent one. Why you care: the company behind ChatGPT and Codex just told you, on the record, that it's staying in build-fast mode rather than shifting into public-market caution. Don't read this as OpenAI slowing down. If anything, an unlisted company with no quarterly earnings calls to answer to tends to ship faster, not slower.

Then, on Sunday, the other side of that same coin turned up. Business Insider reported, citing a source it described only as familiar with the plans, that Anthropic has picked Nasdaq for its own listing, is targeting an October debut, has lined up Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs as bankers, and has arranged a fifteen billion dollar credit facility, all pointing towards a valuation somewhere near two trillion dollars. I want to flag this one hard, because it's the kind of number that's fun to say out loud and easy to over-trust. Anthropic itself has confirmed none of it. There's no prospectus. The sourcing is anonymous. Take it as a rumour with unusually credible bylines attached, not as news. For what it's worth, a credit facility that size usually isn't for office furniture, it's for buying compute ahead of a training run, which fits everything else we've heard about labs racing to build ever bigger models. Why you care: if it's even directionally true, a listing for the company behind Claude usually comes with pricing changes and a more enterprise-weighted product roadmap in the months that follow. That's worth watching from where you sit. It is not worth acting on yet. Notice the pattern here too: the bigger the number attached to a frontier lab, the more likely it is to have arrived through an unnamed source rather than the company's own mouth. That's not proof it's wrong. It's just a reason to wait for the company to say it themselves before you change anything based on it.

Those two stories collided today, Monday, when The Information reported, syndicated through the CNN Business wire, that Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have actually sat down together to discuss a joint standards body that would test frontier models before release, something like a financial regulator, but for AI labs. Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis described it as a public-private partnership, overseen by government but funded by industry, which is a careful way of saying the labs want a seat at the table rather than a rulebook handed to them. Meta, according to the same report, wants nothing to do with it. The piece ties the whole conversation back to a run of incidents this summer where test agents truly believed they were operating on the live internet against real targets, which is exactly the territory Amodei's essay was warning about, and which this show covered in detail a few episodes back. If the comparison to a financial regulator sounds familiar, think of America's broker-dealer watchdog, FINRA, the industry-funded body that checks trading firms before letting them near real money. The pitch here is the same idea applied to model releases instead of trades. Why you care: if a body like this actually gets built, expect more pre-release testing gates on every model you build on top of, Claude and GPT alike. In practical terms that could mean longer gaps between major version releases, and possibly more notice before a model you've built a workflow around gets swapped out from under you. For a business that runs agents against real donor data or real entrant data, that trade is not a bad one. Slower big model releases, in exchange for fewer nasty surprises in how those models behave once they're in production. That trade should sound familiar, because it's the same trade this show flagged after Anthropic's own sandbox-escape disclosure a few weeks ago. It's also directly relevant to PlayFundWin specifically: any agent workflow you run against real entrant or donor data benefits every time a model gets more scrutiny before it ships, not less, because that's scrutiny you didn't have to build yourself.

Right. On to today's deep dive, and this is a good one, because the evidence behind it is about as solid as this show ever gets.

The business is called Low Content AI. The founder is Brian Donatiello. What he's built is a tool that uses AI text and image generation to produce what the self-publishing world calls low-content books: journals, planners, activity books, colouring books, the kind of thing you can design once and sell forever with almost no per-unit cost, because Amazon prints and ships each copy on demand. It plugs straight into Amazon's Kindle publishing platform, generating both the interior content and the cover, and it sells itself to other self-publishers as a subscription, priced from roughly three hundred and ninety to nine hundred and ninety euros a year depending on tier and how much of the pipeline is automated for you.

Here's the evidence, and here's why I trust it more than most of what lands on this show. A platform called Trust M R R lists Low Content AI with its revenue verified directly through a live, read-only connection to the business's own Stripe account. That's a meaningfully different standard from most of what this show quotes: instead of trusting a founder's word or a screenshot that's easy to fake, the platform is pulling the transaction totals straight from the payment processor itself. As of the thirteenth of September, that live figure was seventeen thousand, five hundred and sixty-eight dollars a month in recurring revenue, six hundred and twenty-six thousand, one hundred and three dollars in total revenue since the business started in August twenty twenty-four, three hundred and ninety active subscribers, twenty-eight per cent month-over-month growth, and a seventy-five per cent margin. That is roughly two years to get there, not two months, and I want to be straight with you about that timeline before we go any further.

Two honest caveats. First, every number here lives on one platform. I could not find a second, independent outlet repeating this figure, no Starter Story write-up, no podcast interview, nobody else corroborating it. The Stripe connection makes it far more trustworthy than a typed-in screenshot, but it is still a single source. Second, the business is simultaneously listed for sale on that same platform for eight hundred thousand dollars, which is a mild incentive for the numbers to look as good as possible, even with a genuine live Stripe link behind them. Neither caveat means the number is fake. Both mean you should treat it as strong evidence rather than proof, which is exactly the distinction this show tries to hold onto every single day. Keep both of those in your head as we go.

How would you actually build a version of this, rather than clone Brian's exact business? Two phases, and Claude does almost all of the heavy lifting in both.

Phase one is validation, and it costs you almost nothing. Claude Code can write the core generators for you: the word search, sudoku and maze logic, which is just code, no AI required; the language model calls that write titles, prompts and Kindle metadata; an image generation call for colouring-page line art; and a simple layout script that turns all of that into a print-ready file sized to Kindle's exact trim specifications. You pick one tight niche, ideally something with an audience that buys physical keepsakes rather than just browsing, you self-publish ten to twenty titles directly to Amazon, and you see whether anyone actually buys them. No website. No customers to manage yet. Just an Amazon account and a few days of Claude-assisted building.

Phase two only happens if phase one actually sells. That's when you wrap the same generation pipeline into a proper subscription tool, aimed at other self-publishers chasing the same niche, with a simple web front end and Stripe for billing, sold to UK and international customers the same way Brian sells his. That's the point where you've stopped being a publisher and started being a software business, and it's a meaningfully bigger commitment, which is exactly why phase one exists to test the water first. The temptation is to skip straight to phase two, because building the tool feels like the "real" business. Resist that. Prove the books sell first, with your own hands and a Claude-written script, before you spend a minute building software for anyone else.

Now, the money, kept honest. Phase one costs you next to nothing to test, call it one to two hundred pounds in AI credits and a Kindle account, which already exists for free. Phase two, if you get there, runs fifty to a hundred and fifty pounds a month in hosting and API costs. Both comfortably under this show's five hundred pound bar. But I could not find any published figures on what an individual low-content title actually earns in royalties, and I'm not going to invent one for you. What I can tell you is Brian's own timeline for the subscription tool itself: three hundred and ninety paying subscribers, at his own prices of roughly thirty-two to eighty-two euros a month depending on tier, took him about two years of active marketing, mostly his own YouTube channel and LinkedIn presence. If you ever reach phase two, treat a handful of subscribers in your first few months as the realistic starting point, not three hundred and ninety. Set your expectations at Brian's starting line, not his two-year finish line, and treat anything faster as a pleasant surprise rather than the plan.

The risks are real and worth naming clearly. Amazon has been tightening its AI-content disclosure rules, and low-content Kindle publishing is already a thin-margin, heavily saturated category, which means you're competing against a lot of other people who had this exact idea two years before you did. Brian himself is an actively marketing incumbent with a two-year head start and a real audience; you'd have neither on day one. AI-generated imagery still runs into copyright and trademark filtering on some platforms, which can get a whole catalogue pulled with no warning and very little recourse. And Low Content AI is very unlikely to be the only tool chasing this exact workflow. It's simply the only one this show found with numbers solid enough to build an episode around, which tells you the barrier to entry is low enough that the same opportunity is sitting in front of a lot of other people too, not just you. One more practical risk nobody in the evidence trail mentions: everything here gets judged as a digital preview until a customer holds the physical book, and a maze that looks crisp on a screen can print blurred or misaligned. Order a physical proof copy before you trust any of this looks as good on paper as it does on Claude's output.

Here's where it gets interesting for you specifically. This mechanic, personalised books and printed keepsakes generated on demand, maps onto a fundraising product line for PlayFundWin or LeaguePages that neither Brian nor anyone else in this space is building for: a personalised activity book or colouring book for a specific grassroots club or a specific season, sold to parents at a fundraising event or through a club's online shop, maybe even tied to a prize-draw promotion. I want to flag this exactly as speculative as it is. It is not a plan, it's a stretch, and I have found nobody publishing revenue evidence for that specific niche. But you already have something Brian had to build from nothing over two years: an existing audience of parents and supporters who already buy things from their club, and a payment and fundraising rail already built to sell to them. That is a genuine, if completely untested, edge, and it's the kind of thing worth a single afternoon's prototype rather than a business plan.
