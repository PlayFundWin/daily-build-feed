Claude Code spent the last two days patching itself, and the details are more interesting than "bug fixes" would suggest. Saturday's release, version two point one two seven one, added something called per-command allowed domains — you can now tell Claude Code exactly which web domains a given command is allowed to touch when it's running in a sandbox, rather than the old all-or-nothing permission where a command either had the run of the internet or none of it at all. It also picked up fast mode support for remote sessions, mouse support in the config panel, styled rendering for markdown artifacts, and a batch of fixes for organisation-policy caching and a stale git lock bug that had been annoying Linux users specifically. Sunday's release, two point one two seven two, was the smaller reliability follow-up, the kind of release that exists mostly to clean up after the bigger one. I read both change entries straight off Claude Code's own changelog, so this one's as confirmed as it gets.

Why you care: if you've been nervous about letting an AI coding agent run commands without you watching every single keystroke, domain-level sandboxing is exactly the kind of control that makes unattended use less of a leap of faith. It's the difference between "this agent can talk to anything on the internet while it works" and "this agent can talk to these three specific services and nothing else" — worth revisiting your own permission settings if you haven't touched them since you first set the tool up.

GitHub Copilot reorganised how it picks models on your behalf, and it's a smaller-sounding change than it actually is. GitHub's own changelog, dated the fourteenth of September, confirms Copilot's automatic model selection now offers three named tiers: efficiency, balance, and intelligence. Before this, Copilot would silently choose a model for you behind the scenes, and you'd only find out afterwards, on your bill, if it had reached for something expensive. Now you can set the trade-off yourself: cheap and fast for routine autocomplete and boilerplate, a middle ground for everyday feature work, or the most capable model regardless of cost when you're properly stuck on something hard. I read this directly off GitHub's changelog page, so it's confirmed, not secondhand. Why you care: if any part of your development spend runs through Copilot, this is a lever you didn't have last week — set routine work to efficiency, and save the expensive tier for the problems that actually earn it.

Perplexity announced something called Portable Computer for Windows on the fourteenth, and it's a different shape of product from the cloud-agent tools we usually talk about on this show. It's a local AI agent — the model, the orchestrator, and the scheduler all running entirely on your own machine, no round trip to the cloud, no ongoing credits burned just for using it. Today's deep dive is a lesson in patience as much as it is a business idea. The company is called VideoDubber dot AI, and it does roughly what it sounds like: upload a video, get it translated and dubbed into any of over a hundred and fifty languages using AI voice cloning, so the speaker sounds like themselves, just fluent in Japanese or Portuguese or whatever you need. It's run by a solo operator, Doctor Souvic Chakraborty, based in India.

Here's the evidence, and I want to be specific about how strong it is, because the strength of the source matters as much as the number itself. VideoDubber dot AI has a listing on TrustMRR, a platform that verifies revenue through a live connection to a company's actual Stripe account, not a screenshot or a founder's say-so. As of the fifteenth of September, that listing shows all-time revenue of forty-seven thousand, four hundred and twenty-seven dollars, monthly recurring revenue of six hundred and forty dollars, and thirty active subscribers. The business was founded in May twenty twenty-three, so that forty-seven thousand has taken a little over three years to accumulate, and the current monthly figure is honestly modest. I want to say that straight rather than dress it up: this is not a breakout success story. It's a real, small, Stripe-verified business that's been grinding along for three years and is still grinding.

Why is that worth twenty minutes of your time rather than a flashier pick with a bigger number? Because the mechanics underneath it are close to a solved problem, and because an honest small number teaches you more about what building one of these actually looks like than another ninety-two-thousand-dollars-a-month story does. The hard technical work here — transcription, translation, and voice-cloned dubbing — already exists as an API you can call. ElevenLabs, among others, sells a Dubbing Studio API that does the actual heavy lifting. Building a product like VideoDubber dot AI is really about wrapping that API in an upload screen, a delivery flow, and a billing system. Claude Code can scaffold that entire wrapper — front end, API glue, authentication, Stripe billing — in a matter of days, not months.

Let me put some actual days against that, because "Claude Code can build it in days" means nothing without a rough plan behind it. Day one is proving the core loop works at all: get a single test video through the dubbing API and out the other side as a translated, voice-cloned file, with no interface around it yet, just a script. Day two is wrapping that in a basic upload-and-download web page, so a stranger could use it without you standing over their shoulder. Day three is authentication and Stripe billing, so people can actually pay you before you let them use it. Day four and five are the unglamorous bits that determine whether anyone ever finds it: a proper landing page, and a little search-engine-friendly content built around specific language pairs — "dub your course into Spanish," "dub your course into Mandarin" — since that's genuinely how a tool like this gets found by someone who isn't already looking for you by name. None of those five days require you to write the dubbing engine yourself, which is exactly why this sits at the buildable end of the scale rather than the six-month end.

The money math, kept honest: domain registration is around ten pounds a year, basic hosting on something like Vercel or a small virtual server runs five to ten pounds a month, Stripe costs nothing to set up, and the dubbing API itself is pay-as-you-go — ElevenLabs' cheapest paid tier is roughly five American dollars a month for around thirty thousand credits, and dubbing eats about two thousand credits per minute of audio. Put a few hundred pounds behind this and you've covered the build, several months of hosting, and enough API credits to serve your first real customers. That's comfortably under the five-hundred-pound ceiling this segment always works to.

Time to first paying customer, realistically: two to six weeks after you actually launch, assuming you do the unglamorous work of getting in front of people — a Product Hunt listing, some targeted search-engine content around specific language pairs, maybe a direct pitch to e-learning companies or course creators who already localise content. And even then, don't expect fireworks. VideoDubber dot AI's own three-year trajectory to six hundred and forty dollars a month is the honest benchmark for what a solo, bootstrapped, no-marketing-budget version of this idea looks like. If you go in expecting that and you beat it, wonderful. If you go in expecting an overnight AutoShorts-style story, you'll be disappointed by month two.

Three risks worth naming before you'd sink a weekend into this. First, you're building on someone else's API, which means your entire margin sits downstream of their pricing and their terms of service — if ElevenLabs or a competitor raises prices, tightens rate limits, or decides to sell dubbing directly to consumers themselves, a thin wrapper gets squeezed hard and fast. Second, this is a crowded corner of the market — Rask dot AI, Pinch, Geckodub, and VideoDubber itself are all circling the same customers, which is exactly why the honest six-hundred-and-forty-dollar figure matters more than a hypey one would. And third, there's a real trust and quality risk in voice cloning specifically: mistranslation or a badly cloned voice generates refunds and bad reviews fast, and cloning someone's voice without clear consent is exactly the kind of thing that's attracting regulatory attention here in the UK — so a proper consent checkbox and terms of service aren't optional extras, they're the whole legal foundation of the product.
