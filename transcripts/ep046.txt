Cursor first, because it has been a long wait. Its changelog had sat still since the tenth of September. On Wednesday the twenty third it shipped two features, and neither is a tweak to autocomplete.

The first is called Rollouts. You merge a change, and Cursor follows it out into the world, first to staging and then to production. At each step it gives a verdict: verified healthy, regression detected, or inconclusive. If something breaks it can open a pull request that reverts the change for you, or it can send an agent in to try a fix. It connects to monitoring tools such as Datadog, so it isn't guessing from the code alone. It's reading the same dashboards a person would.

The second is Security Review. It's a bot that reads every pull request looking for exploitable bugs. The list is injection attacks, authentication bypasses, secrets committed by mistake, server-side request forgery, unsafe deserialisation, and risky changes to dependencies. Every finding gets a severity rating and a suggested fix.

I read this on Cursor's own changelog. The limits are that both features are for Teams and Enterprise plans only, and they come with ten days of free credit, which covers roughly fifty changes on Teams and roughly five hundred on Enterprise.

Why you care: a small software shop has never been able to afford a dedicated security reviewer or someone watching every deploy. This doesn't replace that person, but it does the dull first pass that nobody gets round to. If LeaguePages or PlayFundWin has code going live every week, a bot that says "this one leaks a key" before it ships is cheap insurance. That also closes the Cursor quiet-watch thread we opened in episode forty three. The silence wasn't a sign of trouble after all. They were building.

Why pick it? Because it's a new mechanic for us. Forty five episodes in, we've done captions, dubbing, headshots, carousels and a formula bot. We've never done music. It also shows something the show keeps circling: a consumer AI app at a weekly price point can build a serious revenue base without any public founder brand at all.

Before I get to how you'd build it, there's a question I want to put to you. Five thousand six hundred subscriptions at seven dollars a week is not people who need a song. It's people who enjoy making songs. That matters, because it tells you what's being sold. The product is fun, a gift, the joke at a leaving do. The music is secondary. Keep that in mind, because it changes where you'd point this.

How it's built. I'd say this is about ninety per cent buildable with Claude, with one important piece rented.

Claude does the words. It turns "a song about the under-twelves finally beating Harpenden" into a verse, a chorus and a bridge, sets the mood, and writes the prompt for the music model. The music itself comes from a third-party music generation API. Several audio companies now sell one. Pick one whose terms clearly allow commercial use of what it generates, and check that before anything else.

Around those two pieces sits a very ordinary app: a form, a payment step, a player, a download button and a share link. Claude Code can build that in days. Put Stripe on the web version and skip the App Store at first. Starting costs are a domain, hosting and some API credit, well under five hundred pounds. Most of your early spend is the cost of each song generated, so do the maths on that before you set a price.

If I were sequencing it, it would go like this. Days one and two: generate twenty songs by hand with the music API and play them to people you know. You're checking whether the output is good enough that a parent would share it, not whether the software works. Days three to five: have Claude Code build the form, payment and player, with a hard cap on how many generations anyone can trigger, so a bored teenager can't run up your bill overnight. Week two: one pilot club, one weekend, with a single link sent out through the club's usual parent WhatsApp group. By the Monday you'll know the only number that counts, which is what share of parents paid, and no spreadsheet forecast can tell you that in advance.

Now the part that matters for you, because I don't think you should build a consumer song app. The app stores are crowded, the weekly-subscription model depends on buying installs through paid ads, and you'd be competing on ad spend with people who do nothing else.

What you have is a distribution channel into clubs, plus a working model for turning small payments into club funds. So here's the niche.

Call it Season Songs, as a PlayFundWin add-on. At the end of a season a junior club offers parents a personalised thirty second song about their child's season. It uses the kid's name, the team, the moment they scored against the old rivals, sung in whatever style the child likes. The parent pays, say, five pounds. The club keeps a share, just as it keeps a share of a prize draw. It becomes a fundraising product that doesn't feel like fundraising, because what the parent is buying is a gift.

It also works for adults. There's the club anthem for the centenary, the walk-out track for the veterans' cup final, and the leaving song for the groundsman who's been there thirty years. On LeaguePages, the team page is the natural place for it to live, with a "make this team's song" button next to the fixtures.

Conservative money maths, and I mean conservative. Take one junior club with a hundred and fifty players. Say one parent in five buys a song at five pounds. That's thirty songs and a hundred and fifty pounds, before the club's share and before generation costs. Across ten clubs in a season, you're looking at roughly fifteen hundred pounds gross, shared with the clubs. That's not a business. It's a feature that pays for itself and gives clubs one more reason to stay on your platform. The upside is volume. If it spreads across a few hundred clubs through PlayFundWin's existing relationships, the arithmetic changes. But I'd plan on the feature, and treat the business as a bonus if it arrives.

The risks, and there are real ones.

One: children's data. You're putting a named child into a system that generates content about them. That means parental consent, UK GDPR, and clear retention rules, set up before launch and not added after the first complaint. Keep the child's surname out of every prompt. Only the parent should be able to share the song, and your platform shouldn't publish it anywhere by default.

Two: the legal ground under AI music is still moving. There's live dispute over what these music models were trained on. You don't want a club's centenary anthem tangled up in that argument. Choose a provider whose licence terms explicitly give you commercial rights, keep a record of those terms, and avoid prompts that ask it to sound like a named artist.

Three: model dependency, which the ElevenLabs news showed this very morning. Your song feature rests on someone else's model. If they retire it, as OpenAI is doing with Sora today, your feature stops working. Build the music step so it can be swapped, and know your second choice from day one.

Four: novelty fades. The first round of season songs will be delightful. By the third year some parents will be tired of it. Treat it as a seasonal campaign, not a monthly subscription.
