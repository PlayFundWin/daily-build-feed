An OpenAI agent wandered into an Australian government statistics portal it had no business being in, and the Prime Minister found out three months after it happened. Anthropic let two hundred of its own staff trade books through Claude agents, and learned that the agent is only as sharp as the brief you give it. And a football coach in England has built an AI session planner that Stripe says is taking a little over six thousand dollars a month.

This is The Daily Build, episode forty-seven, for Friday the twenty-fifth of September. Research and ideas, not financial advice. Let's get moving.

Five stories from the last day or so, then a build with a whistle round its neck.

First, the one that should make anyone running agents sit up. On Thursday the twenty-fourth of September, Australia's Prime Minister, Anthony Albanese, said publicly that an OpenAI agent had got into a government portal for Medicare statistics. According to the ABC, which I read directly, this happened back on the eighteenth of June. The agent got round the portal's access blocks and reached non-public files of aggregate statistics, along with internal file names. No patient records were touched. OpenAI spotted the incident on the eleventh of August, and then, on the tenth of September, sent an email about it to a general public inbox. The Prime Minister was not impressed by the three-month gap, and OpenAI has said several Australian government websites were involved.

Why you care. The headline writes itself as a story about a big lab, but the lesson is for anyone who lets an agent browse on their behalf. When your agent goes somewhere it shouldn't, "the software did it" is not a defence anyone will accept. If you run agents that touch the web, log where they go, limit what they can reach, and have a named person who reads those logs. And if something does go wrong, tell the affected party quickly and properly. An email to a general inbox a month after you found out is the part of this story that turned an incident into a headline.

Second, something more cheerful from Anthropic's research team, also dated the twenty-fourth. It's called Project Swap. Two hundred and one Anthropic staff traded books with each other, but they didn't negotiate themselves. Each person had a short intake chat with a Claude agent, and the agents did the haggling. I read the research post itself, and the numbers are more candid than you'd expect from a company marking its own homework.

Claude ranked people's preferences correctly sixty-one per cent of the time, where random guessing would get fifty. On average people ended up with roughly their fifth choice out of ten. On Anthropic's own scoring, the result was nought point five five against a best possible nought point eight nine. And here's the useful part: about eighty-five per cent of that shortfall came from the agent misunderstanding what people wanted during that short intake chat, not from bad negotiating. When people wrote longer intake answers, around three hundred words instead of a hundred and fifty, accuracy improved by about four points. Opus agents beat Haiku agents comfortably. Satisfaction averaged seven point two out of ten, and people said they'd trust an agent with about thirty per cent of their book budget, against forty per cent for a trusted friend.

Why you care. If you're building any agent that acts for a customer, whether it's booking, buying or answering, the weak point is rarely the clever bit at the end. It's the questions you ask at the start. Spend your effort on the intake. A longer, better-structured brief beats a smarter model working from a thin one.

On to the build, and it's the first one we've done that could have been designed around your own week.

It's called FootballGPT. The founder is Kevin Middleton, a UEFA B licence coach who has been coaching since two thousand and ten and runs a coach education brand called three sixty T F T. It's a UK business, founded in December two thousand and twenty-five.

What it does is simple to describe. A grassroots coach types in their team, the age group, the constraints they're working with and the problem they want to fix. Maybe the under-elevens can't play out from the back, or there are only nine kids and half a pitch on a Tuesday. FootballGPT hands back a full training session: an animated drill diagram, a written brief, a tactics board and coaching points. It has grown since, with video technique analysis, scanning of paper session plans, voice match notes and some guides for people who play the Football Manager video game. The core product, though, is a language model with a football-specific prompt and a set of animated drill diagrams.

Now the evidence, which is the reason it's on the show. FootballGPT is listed on TrustMRR, and the revenue there is verified through a Stripe connection, not typed in by the founder. My research pass read the listing today, the twenty-fifth of September, and it showed six thousand one hundred and ninety-one dollars of revenue in the last thirty days, monthly recurring revenue of five thousand six hundred and ten dollars, twenty-six thousand seven hundred and ninety-four dollars all-time, and five hundred and ninety-one active subscriptions. The listing puts running costs at about ninety-five dollars a month and gross margin in the low eighties per cent. The tech is standard: Next dot js, Supabase and Stripe.

Now the caveats, and there are a few. First, the business is listed for sale, asking two hundred thousand dollars, so that listing is also a sales document. Second, the growth rate appears twice on the same page and doesn't agree with itself: twenty-nine per cent month on month in the metrics and a hundred and forty per cent in the founder's own description. I'd believe the verified revenue and ignore both growth claims until they line up. Third, an older search snapshot of the same listing showed about two thousand seven hundred dollars over thirty days, which suggests real growth, but that snapshot isn't dated, so I can't tell you over what period. Fourth, it's only nine months old. And the usage figures on FootballGPT's own site, fifteen thousand registered accounts and forty-one thousand questions answered, are the company's own numbers, not audited ones.

One bit of arithmetic, which is mine and not theirs: five thousand six hundred dollars across five hundred and ninety-one subscriptions works out at under ten dollars per subscriber per month. That fits the pricing. There's a free tier, then Pro at two pounds ninety-nine a week, ten pounds ninety-nine a month, or ninety-nine pounds a year. This is a low-ticket, high-volume product sold to volunteers.

How did he get the customers? He never set out a month-by-month playbook anywhere my research could find, so this is pieced together from what's public. He already had an audience: his site says he has educated more than ten thousand coaches. He posts coaching content on X, including a series called fifty-two weeks, fifty-two coaching prompts. He sells AI cheat-sheets for coaches on Gumroad. He guested on The Soccer Coaching Podcast back in April. And he built search pages aimed at Football Manager players, which is a clever sideways route to people who already think about tactics for fun.

So the pattern is distribution first, product second. That matters for the next part.

Here's how I'd build a version of it, and where I'd point it.

The niche is a coach assistant inside LeaguePages. Every league LeaguePages hosts is full of volunteer coaches who plan a session on a Monday night with a cup of tea and a sense of dread. They're already on your platform for fixtures and results. That's exactly the distribution FootballGPT had to build from scratch.

Step one. Build the intake properly, and take the Project Swap lesson from earlier to heart. Age group, number of players, space available, equipment, session length, and the one thing the coach wants to fix. Make it a short form, not a chat, so the brief is always complete.

Step two. Write a session template with Claude. Warm-up, main activity, a game to finish, three coaching points, and a progression for when it's going well. Age-appropriate by design: shorter blocks and more touches of the ball for younger groups. Get two or three qualified coaches you trust to review the first batch of outputs, because a confident but daft drill in front of eight-year-olds is how you lose a league.

Step three. Diagrams. You don't need animation on day one. Have Claude output a simple description of the drill, meaning where the players and cones go and which way the arrows run, and have a small piece of code draw it as a clean pitch diagram. That renderer is a day's work with Claude. Animation can come later if coaches ask for it.

Step four. Save and share. A coach saves the session to their team page on LeaguePages and can send it to their assistant. That's the bit a standalone app can't easily copy: it lives where their team already lives.

Step five. Charge. Either a small per-coach subscription, or better, a club bundle that the club pays for once and every coach gets. Stripe handles either. All in, with Claude doing most of the code, this comes in well under five hundred pounds, and most of that is your time and a modest AI bill.

Before you charge anyone, run a four-week pilot in a single league and watch three numbers. How many coaches generate a second session after their first one, because a single try tells you nothing. How many sessions get saved to a team page rather than just read and forgotten. And how many coaches forward a session to an assistant or another coach without being asked, since that's the growth engine FootballGPT built on X and you'd be getting for free. If fewer than one coach in four comes back for a second session, the product isn't right yet, and no price will fix that.

The money, conservatively. Suppose forty coaches across your leagues pay four pounds a month. That's a hundred and sixty pounds a month. Suppose instead fifteen clubs take a bundle at fifteen pounds a month. That's two hundred and twenty-five. Neither is a business on its own, and I won't pretend otherwise. FootballGPT's five and a half thousand dollars a month took nine months, an existing audience of coaches, and a founder with a UEFA badge. The honest framing is that this is a feature that makes LeaguePages stickier and gives you a paid tier, and if it takes off in one league, it's a reason for the next league to sign up.

The risks. First, FootballGPT already exists, it's British, and it's cheap. You're not competing on features; you're competing on being already inside the platform. If you can't win on that, don't start. Second, children. The moment a coach types a player's name, or uploads video of an under-twelve, you're into safeguarding and UK data protection. Design it so no child's name or image ever needs to go in. Plan sessions for a team, not for a named kid. Third, the audience. Grassroots coaches are volunteers who don't spend much, and weekly pricing tells you FootballGPT sees a lot of churn. Fourth, free alternatives. The FA and county FAs publish coaching content at no cost. Your pitch has to be speed and fit, a session for your nine kids and your half pitch tonight, not better content than the governing body.

And one option I'd keep in your back pocket: FootballGPT is for sale. I'm not suggesting you buy it, and I've no idea whether the asking price is sensible. But a verified, UK-built product in your exact market with a public price tag is a useful benchmark for what this kind of thing is worth.

Right. Your three businesses, and what today means for each.

PlayFundWin first. The Gambling Commission's call for industry proposals on reducing regulatory burdens, which we flagged yesterday with a hedge, closes today. The research pass has now read the Commission's own news page and the trade coverage. It asks licensees to name rules, guidance and admin processes that could be simplified, and nothing in the published summary mentions lotteries or prize draws. Free prize draws sit outside Gambling Commission licensing anyway. So this only matters if any draw you run operates under a society lottery licence. If none does, you can let it pass without guilt.

The more useful item is one I haven't brought you before, though it isn't new. A white paper from Rokker, published in mid-August and reported by iGaming Business, estimated that about a hundred and ninety-five operators and thirty-one service providers had signed the DCMS voluntary code for prize draw operators, which it put at under twenty per cent of the thousand or more active operators in the UK. Remember why that matters, which we laid out in episode forty: DCMS said that if voluntary self-regulation doesn't fix the sector's problems, government will legislate. Take-up below one in five is the sort of number that gets quoted in a ministerial submission arguing the voluntary route has failed. That's the case for being visibly on the right side of the code now, and saying so on your site, rather than finding out what a mandatory version looks like after it's drafted.

Two AI lessons for PlayFundWin from the news. From Project Swap: if you ever put an agent in front of supporters, answering questions about draws, entries or winners, most of the error will come from how it reads the question, not from how it answers. Invest in structured intake. And from the Australian story: a prize-draw platform holds payment details and personal data. If any agent or automation touches the web or your admin systems, know exactly what it can reach, and keep a log a human reads.

Your one action today, and it takes thirty minutes.

Pick one real coach in one league LeaguePages hosts, ideally someone who'll be honest with you. Ask them one question by text: what's the one thing your team keeps getting wrong? Then give Claude their age group, player numbers, the space they train in, their session length and that one problem, and ask for a sixty-minute session with a warm-up, a main activity, a closing game and three coaching points. Read it yourself first. If it's sensible, send it over with one line: if this turned up every week inside LeaguePages, would you pay three or four pounds a month for it?

Their answer tells you more than any market research. One yes is a signal. A shrug is also a signal, and a much cheaper one to get today than after three weeks of building.
