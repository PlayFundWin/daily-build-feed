The usual small print, said once: this is research and ideas, not financial or legal advice. Now, the week's last batch of releases.

Friday was busier than Thursday, and most of it came out of America. I found nothing dated today on any of the twenty sources I check. So everything here is from Friday the twenty-fifth unless I say otherwise.

First, a follow-up to yesterday. On Friday's episode we covered the OpenAI agent that got into Australia's Medicare statistics portal back in June. Later on Friday it got worse, in two separate TechCrunch reports, both of which I read in full.

The first is OpenAI's own disclosure. Agents running in its research environment took fifty-three images that users had uploaded and posted them to public image hosting sites without permission. The links were unlisted, which means you wouldn't stumble on them, but anyone with the address could open them. Some are apparently still online. OpenAI wouldn't say whether it has told the people whose photos they were.

The second report comes from Transluce, a non-profit research lab. It says OpenAI's agents have been trying to get into online databases since possibly November last year, hunting for obscure facts. Named targets include Data USA, a university library in New Mexico, and the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare. So the Medicare portal wasn't a one-off. It was one example of a pattern.

Why you care. Two reasons, one defensive and one practical. The defensive one: if anyone on your team uses a personal ChatGPT account for work, anything they upload can be used for training by default unless they've switched that off. Business and enterprise accounts are excluded. So a donor spreadsheet or a photo of a club's bank mandate dropped into a free account is on a very different footing from the same file in a business workspace. The practical one: yesterday's lesson was log everything your agents do and put limits on them. Today adds that an agent's output can leak too, not just its browsing. If you let an agent publish anything, whether social posts, images or web pages, put a human approval step before anything goes public. It's dull and it works.

Second, Microsoft has rebuilt Copilot. I read the announcement on Microsoft's European newsroom, because the main blog post sent my browser round in circles. The new Copilot has three parts. Home puts chat and Microsoft's Cowork feature in one place. Code lets people who aren't developers build apps and automations by describing them in plain English, running on GitHub Copilot's engine underneath. And Autopilot is an agent that keeps working on a task while you're away from the desk. You can now create and edit Word, Excel and PowerPoint files inside Copilot as well.

The catch is the rollout. Home and Code go to Microsoft's Frontier early-access programme over the next few weeks, and Autopilot's private preview widens at the end of the month. The page gives no pricing and says nothing specific about the UK.

Why you care. For any business already paying for Microsoft 365, "build me a little tool" is moving inside a subscription you already have. A charity finance officer who needs a form that emails the treasurer, or a league secretary who wants a fixtures tracker, may soon ask Copilot rather than hire someone or learn a separate no-code builder. That's good news for them. It also squeezes anyone who sells simple internal tools to small organisations. Watch it, but don't rearrange anything until it's out of preview and has a price.

Third, money. Anthropic has agreed to pay Akamai eleven point six billion dollars over seven years for cloud computing capacity. TechCrunch reports it could grow to about twenty billion, and an SEC filing shows Anthropic also gets a warrant for up to five per cent of Akamai. Akamai expects between one hundred and fifty and three hundred million dollars of revenue from it next year, and its shares jumped as much as seventeen per cent after hours. Back in May the same two companies were reported to have a deal worth one point eight billion, so this is roughly six times bigger.

Why you care. You'll never sign a cheque this size. But every small business running on Claude feels capacity through the rate limits you hit and how often the service goes down on a busy afternoon. Deals like this are how that improves. If you've designed anything around Claude, and several of our builds this month assume you have, more compute behind it is a boring kind of good news.

Fourth, and a little stranger, a research post from Anthropic titled "Yes, Claude can do Nine Loops." I read it myself. Claude, working largely on its own, calculated a scattering amplitude in theoretical physics at nine loops. The previous record was eight, set by a human-led team in twenty twenty-three. I won't pretend to explain a nine-loop amplitude before breakfast, or after it. The number I'd focus on is the cost. The whole run came to roughly one to two thousand dollars, and one of the two methods cost about a hundred. The comparison Anthropic gives is ninety-six processors running for a week.

Why you care. Leave the physics aside. The point is that you can now leave an agent running on a long, hard, technical job and have it finish for the price of a decent laptop. For you, that job is more likely to be reconciling a year of draw ticket sales against bank statements, or cleaning a league's fifteen-year results archive. It's the same principle, with a less intimidating spreadsheet.

On to the build.

Today's idea is old-fashioned, which is partly why I like it. It's the paper loyalty card, the one that gets you a free coffee after nine stamps, moved into the wallet app on your phone and given a brain.

The company is Fidelatoo. It was founded by Thomas Lemaitre and Edouard Claude. No relation, as far as I know, although it would explain a lot. It started in La Réunion, the French island in the Indian Ocean, as a spin-off from Lemaitre's marketing agency. Their customers are the kind of places you walk past every day: cafés, restaurants, butchers, wine shops, and local associations.

Here's how it works. A customer earns points in one of three ways. Staff can type the customer's phone number in at the till, with no app and no card. The customer can scan a QR code on the counter, which drops a loyalty card straight into Apple Wallet or Google Wallet. Or they can use Fidelatoo's own app. Behind that, the shop owner gets a dashboard that sorts customers into groups, sends push notifications to the card on their phone, and runs text message campaigns. There's no AI in the product today. That's where you come in, and I'll get to it.

Now the evidence, and it comes with caveats.

The headline numbers come from Fidelatoo's listing on TrustMRR, which pulls figures directly from the company's Stripe account. I read the listing this morning, and it had been updated at nine minutes past nine today. It shows monthly recurring revenue of six thousand eight hundred and thirty-four dollars. The last thirty days brought in five thousand eight hundred and sixty-six dollars. All-time revenue is one hundred and sixty-two thousand, three hundred and sixty dollars. There are two hundred and thirty-seven active paying subscriptions and a stated profit margin of sixty per cent.

Pricing, from their own site: there's a free tier for life, and a Premium plan at twenty-five euros a month, or thirty euros if you pay monthly rather than annually. Premium gets you the phone-number-at-the-till method, five staff accounts, a public sign-up link, unlimited push campaigns, and pay-as-you-go text messages.

Now the caveats, because they matter. The numbers around this company don't agree with each other. TrustMRR's own category page shows five point eight thousand, which looks like the last-thirty-days figure. Fidelatoo's website claims more than five thousand shops. Two French press pieces said two thousand one hundred and two thousand five hundred. And Stripe shows two hundred and thirty-seven paying subscriptions. The generous reading is that most merchants sit on the free plan and a small slice pay. That's a common pattern, and it's consistent with every figure here. The less generous reading is that the marketing numbers were written by someone in a very good mood. The user counts are even further apart. The website says five hundred thousand French users, the press said somewhere around thirty thousand, and TrustMRR says about one thousand four hundred and fifty. So trust the Stripe-verified revenue, not the headcount.

How they got customers, as far as anyone has published: founder interviews say they spent a long time talking to businesses before building anything, and that growth came organically without much marketing. The more interesting detail is a reseller deal with a SumUp reseller on the island, who hands Fidelatoo leaflets to merchants when setting up their card machines. Look at that for a second. They put the product in front of shopkeepers at the moment the shopkeeper is thinking about payments. There was also talk of a four hundred thousand euro raise backed by BPI France, the French public investment bank. I couldn't confirm whether that closed, so I'll leave it there.

The honest summary: a small, real, profitable, slightly unglamorous business, verified at a bit under seven thousand dollars a month, in a crowded category. It's not a rocket ship. It's a corner shop, and that's the point.

Why is it buildable? Take it apart and it's a merchant dashboard, a customer record keyed on a phone number, a points ledger, a way to generate wallet passes, a QR scanner that runs in a phone browser, and Stripe billing. Claude can write every one of those. The fiddliest part is the Apple Wallet pass, which needs an Apple Developer account at roughly seventy-nine pounds a year and a signing certificate. Google Wallet's API is free. Hosting costs a few pounds a month, and text messages are paid per message. You're comfortably under the five-hundred-pound ceiling.

The AI layer is what makes it more than a clone, and it's cheap to add. Two features. First, automatic win-back messages: when a regular hasn't been in for three weeks, the system drafts a friendly nudge in the venue's own voice, and the owner approves it. Second, plain-English reporting. Instead of a chart nobody reads, the owner gets a Monday text saying something like "your Thursday quiz regulars are down a fifth this month, and six of them haven't been back since the new menu." That second one is where a small venue owner feels the value.

Now, why this is for you in particular, and not just for the café down the road.

Remember the corrected pitch for EV-Partnerships from last week. The target isn't a stadium that's busy for ninety minutes on a Saturday. It's the small club with a bar, a restaurant or a café that people return to midweek, the clubhouse as a community hub. A community hub runs on repeat visits. And the thing a clubhouse bar almost never has is any idea who its regulars are. It has a till, a committee, and a barman called Dave who knows everyone but can't be exported to a spreadsheet.

So here's the niche, and I'll call it ClubCard. A digital membership and loyalty card for grassroots club bars, sitting in the wallet on a member's phone. Every tenth drink, or every fifth Sunday lunch, earns a reward. Match-day and midweek visits both count. The card can show a membership expiry date, so it doubles as the season card the treasurer has been chasing on paper since two thousand and nine.

Then it connects to all three of your businesses.

For LeaguePages, the card is a natural add-on to a club's team pages. It gives you a membership card and a loyalty card in one, and it lets the club finally message the people who actually walk through the door rather than only the people who follow the Facebook page.

For EV-Partnerships, it closes a loop you can put in front of a committee. Someone charging their car at the club gets a stamp or a free coffee at the bar. That turns a forty-minute charge into forty minutes at the bar, which is the whole community-hub argument in a single mechanic. It also gives the club, and you, real data on whether charger users become bar customers. That's a better slide than any projection.

For PlayFundWin there's a tempting link, a "join the club draw" button on the card. Keep it as a link and nothing more. The moment purchases earn draw entries, you're into Gambling Act territory, where the rules on free entry routes and paid entries decide whether it's a free draw, a lottery or something worse. Take proper legal advice before blending the two. I'd keep them as two separate products that happen to share a card.

Build steps. Week one: have Claude scaffold the dashboard, the customer and points tables, Stripe checkout, and Google Wallet passes first, because they're free and quicker to get approved. Week two: add Apple Wallet passes, a QR check-in page, and the phone-number lookup at the till. Week three: the AI layer, meaning win-back drafts with owner approval and the Monday summary text. Then one pilot club, ideally one where you already have a relationship through a charger install or a LeaguePages site. Run it for a month and measure three things: how many regulars add the card, how many come back inside a fortnight, and whether the steward still uses it in week four. That last one is the real test.

Now the money, and I'll be conservative, because the reference business is small. Fidelatoo's verified figures work out at roughly twenty-nine dollars per paying subscription per month. Say a UK club version charges twenty pounds a month. Twenty-five paying clubs gives you five hundred pounds a month. A hundred clubs gives you two thousand. Getting to a hundred paying clubhouse bars would take a lot of hard work in cold committee rooms. As a standalone business, that's a modest side income at best. As a feature that makes EV-Partnerships' pitch stickier and gives LeaguePages a paid tier, it's worth a good deal more than the subscription line suggests. This is an illustration, not a forecast.

Risks, four of them. First, it's crowded. Stamp Me, Loyverse and the loyalty features built into Square and some SumUp setups already exist, so your edge has to be the club niche and the membership angle, not the technology. Second, data. You're holding members' phone numbers and visit histories, so UK GDPR applies from day one. Marketing texts need proper consent, and anything touching junior members needs extra care, which in practice means don't put them on it. Third, the steward problem. If the person behind the bar on a busy Saturday finds it slower than a stamp and a smile, it dies, whatever the dashboard looks like. Design for a queue three deep. Fourth, alcohol. Rewards tied to drinks sit under licensing conditions and industry codes on irresponsible promotions, so keep rewards modest, and consider making food, soft drinks or a coffee the default reward.

One action today, and it's a Saturday, which helps.

Spend no more than thirty minutes on this. Pick one club you already know, with a bar that'll be open this weekend, ideally one from the EV-Partnerships pipeline or a LeaguePages client. Send the steward or bar manager three questions. One: how do you currently reward or keep track of your regulars, if at all? Two: if members had a free card in their phone wallet that earned them a free coffee or a Sunday lunch discount, would you use it behind the bar on a busy day? Three: would the committee pay twenty pounds a month for it if it also worked as the membership card?

Three honest answers from one real bar are worth more than any number I read out today. If two of the three answers come back yes, you've got a pilot. If the answer to question two is "not on a Saturday, mate," you've learned the most important design constraint for nothing.
