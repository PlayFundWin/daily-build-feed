Google has a new top model, and you are not allowed to use it yet. An American regulator has opened a probe into the biggest AI labs at once. And a German founder appears to be making serious money from the least glamorous idea imaginable: a QR code on a table at a party.

This is The Daily Build, episode fifty-three, for Thursday the first of October. I'm your host. As ever, this is research and ideas, not financial advice.

October, then. The clocks haven't gone back yet, the club bars are filling up for the autumn, and the AI labs have spent the last day and a half doing what they do best, which is announcing things with an asterisk attached. Let me take you through what landed.

First up, the headline from Wednesday the thirtieth of September. Google announced Gemini four Argon, its new frontier model, built for long, multi-step work: software engineering, office knowledge work, and cyber defence. I read Google's own announcement for this one, so the specifics are first-hand.

The numbers that matter. Output can now run to one million tokens in a single response, up from sixty-four thousand. That is the difference between a model writing you a chapter and a model writing you the whole book in one go. On Google's own software engineering benchmark, DeepSWE, it scores seventy-seven point nine per cent, and on a business automation test called AutomationBench it ranks first, at fifty-one point three per cent. Google also claims leading results on resisting prompt injection, which is the attack where hidden text in a web page or document hijacks the model's instructions.

Pricing is aggressive. An introductory rate of two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output, with cached input ninety-five per cent off. After the introductory period, it doubles to four and twenty. If those numbers sound familiar, they should: that introductory price lands exactly where Claude Sonnet five point five and OpenAI's new Sol model sit, which we covered on Tuesday and Wednesday. Three labs, one price point. That is not a coincidence, that is a market settling.

Now the asterisk. Right now Argon is only rolling out to what Google calls trusted cyber defenders, through something named the Fairwind Program. Paid API customers and Google AI Ultra subscribers get it next, as soon as possible, which is corporate for "no date". Nothing in the announcement about the UK or Europe.

Why you care: you cannot build on it today, so there's nothing to switch. But the price war at the top end is now a three-way tie, which means the model you choose for any automation should be picked on how well it does your specific task, not on the sticker. Same lesson as the Grok episode a few weeks back. Benchmark the job, not the brochure.

Right. Enough of the giants. Let's talk about something you could have live by next weekend.

Today's build is the event photo wall. The mechanic is almost embarrassingly simple. The host of an event gets a QR code printed on a table card or a sign. Guests scan it with their phone camera, and upload photos and videos straight from the browser. No app to download, no account to create. Everything lands in one private gallery. There's a live slideshow that can run on a screen in the room, and at the end the host downloads the lot. The customer pays once, per event.

The second case is messier, and I'm including it precisely because it's messy. And the counter-evidence, because a good build segment should hurt a bit. Two tools that do only face-blurring of photos, which sounds like a neighbour of this idea, have gone nowhere. So, why does this work for you, Steve, rather than for any random builder?

Because you already stand in rooms full of the right customers. Every club on LeaguePages has an end-of-season presentation night. Every PlayFundWin partner club has a draw night or a fundraising dinner. Every club EV-Partnerships talks to has a bar, which means it has events: quiz nights, race nights, sixtieth birthdays in the function room. The incumbents in this market, and there are plenty, are selling to brides through Google Ads. Nobody is selling to the club secretary of a cricket club in Hertfordshire through a platform she already logs into.

And there's a sponsor angle the wedding apps can't touch. A club gallery and a slideshow on the clubhouse television is a place to put a local sponsor's logo, which gives the club something extra to sell, and gives you a reason for the club to want it rather than merely tolerate it.

Call it the Clubhouse Photo Wall. Here's how I'd build it, roughly in order.

One. Have Claude build a simple web app: a front end in Astro or Next, file storage on Cloudflare's R two service, a small database, and Stripe Checkout for payment. That is close to Denis's own stack, so you know the approach can carry a business of that size.

Two. Each event gets its own short link, a QR code, and a print-ready A five table card as a PDF, with the club crest and the sponsor logo on it.

Three. Upload from the phone browser with no login. Big button, works on a bad signal, retries if it drops. Most clubhouse wifi is a rumour.

Four. A slideshow page designed for a TV screen, cycling new uploads as they arrive. That's the bit that sells it in the room, because people see their own photo pop up and immediately take three more.

Five. A moderation check on every upload, using a vision model to hold back anything inappropriate for the organiser to approve. And this is the non-negotiable part for junior clubs: launch for adult events first. If you ever open it to junior presentation nights, it needs the club's welfare officer involved, clear consent from parents, an option to blur faces, and a firm rule on deleting everything after a set period. That's UK GDPR and basic safeguarding, not paperwork for its own sake.

Six. A download-all button at the end, and an automatic deletion date the organiser can see from day one.

Costs: a domain, storage and a few printed sample cards. Comfortably under a hundred and fifty pounds to start, well inside our five hundred pound ceiling.

Money maths, and this is illustration, not a forecast. Price it at twenty-nine pounds per event, or seventy-nine pounds a year per club for unlimited events. Ten single events a month is two hundred and ninety pounds a month. Fifty clubs on the annual plan is just under four thousand pounds a year. Neither of those changes your life. A hundred and fifty clubs on the annual plan, plus a trickle of one-off events from their members' own parties, starts to look like a proper side stream, with storage costs that stay small because most events are a few hundred photos.

Now the risks. It's crowded: Kululu, Guestpix, Wedibox, GuestCam and a long tail of others already do this. Google Photos shared albums are free and good enough for plenty of people. Denis's revenue looks ad-driven, so you can't assume organic growth. Demand is seasonal, peaking around presentation nights in spring and early summer and the Christmas do. And the safeguarding point above is the one that could hurt you if you get it wrong, which is why adults-first isn't optional.

Where you win is distribution and fit. You're not trying to out-advertise the wedding apps. You're adding a feature to relationships you already have.

So, one thing to do today, thirty minutes, no code.

Open a blank page and write the Meridian FC members' charging offer. Three lines. Line one: the price per kilowatt hour, set somewhere between what a member pays at home and what Be E V's Flexi Pass charges, which is thirty-nine pence. You know the club's electricity cost; I don't, so you pick the number. Line two: one monthly option, benchmarked against Be E V's forty-nine pound Go Pass, but sized for a club member who plugs in twice a week during training or at the bar, not for a high-mileage driver. Line three: one thing that ties it to the clubhouse, such as a pint or a coffee with a monthly pass, or a discount on evenings from seven o'clock.

Then send it to whoever runs the bar at Meridian, with one question: "Would your members understand this in ten seconds?" If the answer is yes, you've got a template for the next club. If it's no, you've learned that before printing a single sign.
